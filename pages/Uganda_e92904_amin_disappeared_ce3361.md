## Introduction

The mystery surrounding Idi Amin's disappeared victims is not whether large-scale enforced disappearances occurred, but why so many individual fates remain impossible to reconstruct. Between Amin's seizure of power in January 1971 and his overthrow in 1979, thousands of Ugandans were arrested by soldiers or intelligence officers and never returned. Historians, human rights investigators and survivors broadly agree that many were murdered. What remains uncertain is where they died, where their bodies were taken, who gave the specific orders in many cases, and why so much documentary evidence vanished or was never created in the first place. These gaps have left families without answers, while making the historical record unusually fragmented despite the overwhelming evidence of systematic state violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>

## How the disappearances were carried out

Disappearances under Amin followed recognisable patterns rather than random acts of violence. Victims were often taken by members of the army, the State Research Bureau, the Public Safety Unit or Military Police. Arrests frequently occurred in broad daylight, at workplaces, homes or on roads, with little attempt to disguise official involvement. Once detained, many people were transferred to military barracks, intelligence compounds or prisons from which they never emerged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>

Unlike ordinary criminal investigations, these arrests usually generated no reliable paperwork. Families were rarely informed where relatives had been taken. Security agencies denied holding detainees, while officials sometimes spread contradictory stories that victims had escaped, fled abroad or been released. This administrative uncertainty was itself part of the machinery of repression, making it difficult for relatives, lawyers or courts to challenge unlawful detention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>

Witnesses also described deliberate efforts to erase physical evidence. Bodies were reportedly buried secretly, disposed of in rivers, abandoned in remote areas or removed from detention sites before outsiders could investigate. Such practices explain why many disappearances remain unresolved despite widespread agreement that victims were killed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>

## Why so many victims were never identified

The disappearance of records mirrors the disappearance of people. Several factors combined to make later reconstruction exceptionally difficult.

* **Fear silenced witnesses.** The International Commission of Jurists found that virtually all witnesses insisted on anonymity because they feared retaliation against themselves or relatives. This meant many first-hand accounts could not immediately become public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>
* **Security agencies kept little transparent documentation.** Many arrests were conducted outside normal judicial procedures, leaving no reliable chain of custody or official detention records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>
* **The regime had little incentive to preserve evidence.** Public acknowledgement of disappearances would have implicated state institutions responsible for the arrests. Official investigations therefore remained limited and often lacked credibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>
* **Conflict destroyed additional evidence.** The fighting that ended Amin's rule in 1979 disrupted government institutions, while years of political instability further complicated efforts to preserve or recover records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr590071978en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty Internationalafr590071978en.pdfThe International Commission of Jurists made important submissions on Uganda to the United Nations...</span></span></span>

As a result, historians often know the approximate date of an arrest but cannot establish the exact sequence of imprisonment, execution and disposal of the victim's remains.

## Kiwanuka, Ruhesi and other unresolved cases

Individual disappearances illustrate how the broad pattern of repression still leaves unresolved personal histories.

### Benedicto Kiwanuka

Perhaps the best-known case is that of Benedicto Kiwanuka, [Uganda]({{ 'what-still-haunts-ugandas-historical/' | relative_url }})'s first Prime Minister and later Chief Justice. On 21 September 1972 he was seized by armed security personnel directly from the High Court in Kampala after decisions that displeased the regime. Numerous later accounts agree that he was almost certainly killed soon afterwards.

The unresolved questions concern the details rather than the outcome. Different witnesses have placed his death at different detention facilities, including Makindye military prison. No authenticated remains have ever been recovered, and no definitive official account has been established. His disappearance became a powerful symbol because even the country's highest judicial officer could be removed from a courtroom without legal process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>

### Francis Xavier Ruhesi

The disappearance of engineer Francis Xavier Ruhesi demonstrates that many respected professionals vanished with far less public attention than senior politicians. He reportedly disappeared in September 1972 after being taken by security personnel and has never been accounted for. Decades later, his family continues to seek definitive information about his fate.

Cases like Ruhesi's remind historians that the archive of disappearances extends far beyond internationally known figures. Thousands of civil servants, teachers, doctors, engineers, businesspeople, soldiers and ordinary civilians disappeared with similarly incomplete records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>

### The unknown majority

The famous names are exceptional largely because some documentation survives. Most victims left only fragmentary traces: a final sighting, an arrest witnessed by neighbours, an abandoned vehicle or rumours passed through frightened communities. In many instances there is insufficient surviving evidence even to establish which security agency carried out the arrest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>

## Why official investigations produced few answers

The Amin government occasionally announced inquiries into disappearances, including investigations involving prominent missing figures. However, contemporary observers criticised these efforts as lacking independence and producing little practical accountability.

One notable example was the Commission of Inquiry into disappearances established during Amin's rule. Although intended to examine cases involving prominent missing Ugandans, its membership, methods and findings remain poorly documented, and it failed to resolve the overwhelming majority of disappearances or identify those responsible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://atjhub.csvr.org.za/commission-of-inquiry-into-the-disappearances-of-people-in-uganda-since-january-25-1971-1974-1975/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atjhub.csvr.org.za">[African Transitional Justice Hub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atjhub.csvr.org.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The identity of the committee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">African Transitional Justice HubCommission of Inquiry into the Disappearances of People in...1 Nov 2023 — In 1973, Amin established an a...</span></span></span>

## Why bodies, orders and records remain missing

Three separate mysteries overlap.

The first concerns **missing bodies**. Without identified remains, many families have never been able to confirm where relatives died or conduct traditional burial ceremonies. In some instances, alleged burial locations have never been excavated or verified.

The second concerns **missing orders**. Historians generally agree that disappearances formed part of systematic state repression, yet documentary evidence identifying precisely who authorised many individual arrests has rarely survived. Responsibility can often be established at the institutional level without identifying every officer involved in a particular case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/Uganda-violations-of-human-rights-thematic-report-1974-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsViolations of human rights and the rule of law in ugandaJune 4, 2013 — This study of violations of hum...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2013</span></span></span>

The third concerns **missing archives**. Uganda has preserved some important official material from the Amin era, and recent archival work has highlighted the rediscovery and preservation of large collections of government photographs, films and recordings from the period. These collections greatly expand understanding of how the regime presented itself publicly. However, they do not solve the central mystery of enforced disappearances because propaganda archives are fundamentally different from police files, detention registers or execution records that were never preserved or remain lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uwa.edu.au/news/article/2025/july/lost-archives-of-idi-amin-preserved-and-protected" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwa.edu.au">[UWA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwa.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lost archives of idi amin preserved and protected</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lost archives of Idi Amin preserved and protected14 Jul 2025 — The archives include 60,640 official photographs from Idi Amin&#x27;s regime...</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery still matters

The disappeared remain central to Uganda's historical memory because their cases combine certainty and uncertainty in unusual ways. There is overwhelming evidence that systematic enforced disappearances occurred under Amin's rule, yet countless individual stories remain unfinished.

For historians, the missing record complicates efforts to reconstruct the mechanics of repression. For families, the absence of bodies and documentation means grief often lacks closure. For Uganda's legal and political history, these disappearances demonstrate how authoritarian governments can erase not only lives but also much of the evidence needed to establish precisely what happened.

The enduring mystery is therefore not whether the disappearances occurred. It is how many individual identities, burial places, official orders and documentary traces were successfully erased—and whether enough evidence can ever be recovered to restore those missing chapters of Uganda's history.
