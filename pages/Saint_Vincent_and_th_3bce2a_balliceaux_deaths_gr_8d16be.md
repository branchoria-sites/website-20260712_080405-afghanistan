## Introduction

The mystery of Balliceaux is not whether large numbers of Garifuna prisoners died there, but how many. Between late 1796 and early 1797, British colonial authorities confined thousands of Garifuna men, women and children on the small, water-poor island of Balliceaux after the Second Carib War. Within a matter of months, disease, hunger, thirst and exposure caused catastrophic loss of life before the survivors were deported to Roatán, off the coast of present-day Honduras. The broad outline is well documented. What remains uncertain is the exact death toll and the location of the victims' graves. Because the dead were buried hurriedly, records are incomplete and no systematic archaeological investigation has been carried out, Balliceaux remains both a historical crime scene and an unresolved archaeological puzzle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islandstudiesjournal.org/api/v1/articles/84630-mourning-balliceaux-towards-a-biography-of-a-caribbean-island-of-death-grief-and-memory.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandstudiesjournal.org">[islandstudiesjournal.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandstudiesjournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Island Studies Journal, 15(2), 2020, 255-272November 5, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2020</span></span></span>

## Who was imprisoned on the island?

Balliceaux was never intended to support thousands of people. It is a small island with no reliable natural source of fresh water, making it an unsuitable place of long-term detention.

After the British defeated organised Garifuna resistance in 1796, prisoners captured across Saint Vincent were transported there. Contemporary records and later historical studies do not agree precisely on the number of detainees. Estimates generally fall between about 4,300 and just over 5,000 people because different officials counted different categories of prisoners and because surrender, capture and transport occurred over several months.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343015907_Mourning_Balliceaux_Towards_a_biography_of_a_Caribbean_island_of_death_grief_and_memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Mourning Balliceaux: Towards a biography of a Caribbean island of death, grief and memoryJanuary 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2020</span></span></span>

Most prisoners were Garifuna, then described by British officials as "Black Caribs", although smaller numbers of Indigenous people classified as "Yellow" or "Red Caribs" were also present. The survivors followed different paths. Most Garifuna survivors were deported to Roatán in 1797, where the modern Garifuna diaspora traces an important part of its history, while many of the surviving Indigenous people classified separately by the British were eventually returned to Saint Vincent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343015907_Mourning_Balliceaux_Towards_a_biography_of_a_Caribbean_island_of_death_grief_and_memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Mourning Balliceaux: Towards a biography of a Caribbean island of death, grief and memoryJanuary 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2020</span></span></span>

## Why the death estimates differ

The uncertainty surrounding the number of deaths is not the result of a single missing document. Instead, it reflects several overlapping problems in the surviving evidence.

First, historians disagree about how many prisoners reached Balliceaux in the first place. Different military returns and later compilations produce totals ranging from roughly 4,300 to more than 5,000 people. If the starting population is uncertain, the number of deaths inevitably becomes uncertain as well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343015907_Mourning_Balliceaux_Towards_a_biography_of_a_Caribbean_island_of_death_grief_and_memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Mourning Balliceaux: Towards a biography of a Caribbean island of death, grief and memoryJanuary 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2020</span></span></span>

Second, colonial records were administrative rather than humanitarian. British officials were primarily concerned with managing prisoners and arranging deportations, not creating detailed death registers. Many records counted those transported rather than those buried, and some documents appear to record only selected categories of detainees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343015907_Mourning_Balliceaux_Towards_a_biography_of_a_Caribbean_island_of_death_grief_and_memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Mourning Balliceaux: Towards a biography of a Caribbean island of death, grief and memoryJanuary 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2020</span></span></span>

Third, historians differ over which figures should be treated as authoritative. Common estimates include:

* Around **2,000 deaths**, based on several modern historical studies comparing the numbers sent to Balliceaux with those later transported to Roatán.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343015907_Mourning_Balliceaux_Towards_a_biography_of_a_Caribbean_island_of_death_grief_and_memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Mourning Balliceaux: Towards a biography of a Caribbean island of death, grief and memoryJanuary 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2020</span></span></span>
* Approximately **2,400 to 2,500 deaths**, calculated from higher estimates of the original prisoner population and lower counts of survivors. These figures are widely used in Garifuna remembrance and reparations discussions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/313940892_Indigenous_Land_Rights_and_Caribbean_Reparations_Discourse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) Indigenous Land Rights and Caribbean Reparations Discourse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Indigenous Land Rights and Caribbean Reparations DiscourseFebruary 1, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2017</span></span></span>
* More general descriptions stating that **about half** of those detained died before deportation, a conclusion repeated by United Nations reporting and several historical syntheses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/nanna/record/755522/files/A_HRC_23_34_Add.2-ZH.pdf?registerDownload=1&amp;version=1&amp;withMetadata=0&amp;withWatermark=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library Systemuntitled</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library Systemuntitled...</span></span></span>

Although these totals differ by several hundred people, they point to the same conclusion: mortality was catastrophic. No serious historical interpretation suggests only a small loss of life.

## Why the graves remain unknown

One of the most disturbing aspects of the Balliceaux story is that the burial places have never been securely identified.

Accounts indicate that prisoners who died were buried on the island, but there is no surviving map of burial grounds, no marked cemetery and no complete burial register. Over more than two centuries, vegetation, erosion, changing land use and the absence of formal memorials have obscured the landscape. The result is an island widely regarded by Garifuna communities as a single sacred burial place rather than a location containing one known cemetery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islandstudiesjournal.org/api/v1/articles/84630-mourning-balliceaux-towards-a-biography-of-a-caribbean-island-of-death-grief-and-memory.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandstudiesjournal.org">[islandstudiesjournal.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandstudiesjournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Island Studies Journal, 15(2), 2020, 255-272November 5, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2020</span></span></span>

Local traditions have long referred to human remains being found on Balliceaux, but these reports have not been verified through modern archaeological investigation. As a result, historians cannot confidently identify where most victims were buried or estimate the number of graves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gahfu.org/garifuna-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gahfu.org">[GAHFU.ORG]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gahfu.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GARIFUN A HISTORY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GARIFUN A HISTORY</span></span></span>

The absence of visible graves also has practical consequences. Without identified burial areas, it is difficult to protect the dead from future development or natural erosion, and impossible to produce an archaeological count that could help narrow the historical estimates.

## How archaeology could locate the graves

Researchers who have written about Balliceaux consistently note that the island has never received a dedicated archaeological investigation focused on the 1796–1797 detention camp.

Several approaches could improve understanding without immediately disturbing human remains:

* **Detailed landscape survey** to identify traces of the camp, pathways and possible burial areas.
* **Remote sensing**, including ground-penetrating radar and other geophysical methods, to detect grave shafts or soil disturbances before excavation.
* **Targeted excavation**, undertaken only with appropriate permissions and in consultation with Garifuna descendants, if non-invasive surveys identify likely burial sites.
* **Environmental and historical mapping**, combining old maps, military records and modern terrain analysis to reconstruct where prisoners were housed and where burials were most likely to have taken place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islandstudiesjournal.org/api/v1/articles/84630-mourning-balliceaux-towards-a-biography-of-a-caribbean-island-of-death-grief-and-memory.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandstudiesjournal.org">[islandstudiesjournal.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandstudiesjournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Island Studies Journal, 15(2), 2020, 255-272November 5, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2020</span></span></span>

Archaeologists also emphasise that Balliceaux is not simply a research site. For many Garifuna descendants, it is a place of mourning and ancestral memory. Any investigation therefore raises ethical questions about whether graves should remain undisturbed or whether careful archaeological documentation would better honour those who died.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islandstudiesjournal.org/api/v1/articles/84630-mourning-balliceaux-towards-a-biography-of-a-caribbean-island-of-death-grief-and-memory.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandstudiesjournal.org">[islandstudiesjournal.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandstudiesjournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Island Studies Journal, 15(2), 2020, 255-272November 5, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2020</span></span></span>

## Why the missing death toll still matters

The uncertainty surrounding Balliceaux is more than a statistical problem. Every unidentified grave represents a person whose death was never properly recorded.

For historians, the missing figures reveal the limitations of colonial archives, which often documented military success more carefully than civilian suffering. For Garifuna communities in [Saint Vincent and]({{ 'saint-vincent-and-the-grenadines/' | relative_url }}) throughout Central America, the uncertainty has become part of the history itself. Annual pilgrimages and campaigns to preserve Balliceaux reflect the belief that the island is a sacred memorial regardless of whether the exact number of victims is ever known.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baliceaux" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The most evidence-based conclusion is therefore a modest but powerful one. Thousands were imprisoned on Balliceaux, roughly half died before deportation, and the exact number cannot yet be established because incomplete colonial records and the absence of archaeological investigation leave many of the island's graves unidentified. Until those burial places are properly documented—or unless new archival discoveries emerge—the true human cost of Balliceaux will remain one of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines' most significant unresolved historical questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islandstudiesjournal.org/api/v1/articles/84630-mourning-balliceaux-towards-a-biography-of-a-caribbean-island-of-death-grief-and-memory.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandstudiesjournal.org">[islandstudiesjournal.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandstudiesjournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Island Studies Journal, 15(2), 2020, 255-272November 5, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2020</span></span></span>
