## Introduction

The country’s most important enigma is [Nan Madol]({{ 'nan-madol/' | relative_url }}) on Pohnpei. Archaeology now provides a credible date for its great monuments and identifies probable sources of its basalt, but major questions remain about labour, political power and the city’s abandonment. Kosrae’s walled centre of [Leluh]({{ 'leluh/' | relative_url }}) presents a related puzzle about the rise of island-wide monarchy and unusual mortuary practices. Yap’s famous account of a lost stone currency is stranger still, although the best-known version comes through an early twentieth-century foreign observer and should not be treated as a complete explanation of Yapese economic life.

## Nan Madol: what archaeology has solved

Nan Madol occupies a reef beside Temwen Island, off the eastern coast of Pohnpei. Its monumental core consists of more than 90 artificial islets formed from coral fill and enclosed or connected by walls, channels and platforms of basalt. It was not an ordinary residential town in the modern sense. UNESCO describes it as a ceremonial centre associated with the Saudeleur dynasty, containing residences, ritual spaces and mortuary structures that expressed a highly organised political and religious order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1503/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreNan Madol: Ceremonial Centre of Eastern MicronesiaThe site was also inscribed on the List of World Heritage i...</span></span></span>

For generations, outsiders presented the basic fact of its construction as inexplicable. Yet the broad archaeological picture is no longer mysterious. Researchers have used uranium-thorium dating of building coral and chemical analysis of architectural stone to place the beginning of major monumental construction at about AD 1180–1200. Basalt sourcing also connects some of the columns to volcanic outcrops elsewhere on Pohnpei, including sources on the opposite side of the island. The city was therefore created by medieval Pohnpeian society, not by an Ice Age civilisation, vanished continent or unidentified foreign race.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fsmculture.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/mccoy_athens_2011-libre.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fsmculture.wordpress.com">[wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fsmculture.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FSM National Archives Sourcing the Megalithic Stones of Nan Madol</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FSM National ArchivesSourcing the Megalithic Stones of Nan MadolJanuary 28, 2014 — by MD McCoy · Cited by 28 — Nan Madol is a massive hec...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 28, 2014</span></span></span>

That answer does not make the achievement less extraordinary. Builders had to quarry or collect heavy naturally formed basalt columns, transport them around a mountainous and heavily vegetated island, move them across water and stack them without modern lifting machinery. The precise working arrangements have not survived. Archaeologists can propose rafts, ropes, levers and coordinated labour, but they cannot reconstruct every journey or building operation from the remaining material evidence. The enduring question is consequently not whether Pohnpeians could have built Nan Madol, but how its rulers mobilised the food, specialists, transport crews and sustained labour needed to do so.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/68" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[Journal of Pacific Archaeology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">D. and Athens, J. Nan Madol’s scale points towards concentrated authority. A ruler able to summon labour from across Pohnpei, support elite residents and construct monumental tombs possessed more than ceremonial prestige. The earliest securely dated royal tomb is therefore important because it marks a period when political leadership was becoming materially visible on an unprecedented scale. Archaeology can identify this transformation, although it cannot reveal whether workers participated through obligation, religious devotion, tribute, coercion or some combination of all four.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/d7185280-e420-4b9a-8812-3eef59317d31" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.cam.ac.uk">[Cambridge Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Apolloby MD McCoy · 2016 · Cited by 35 — Nan Madol is an ancient administrative andmortuary center and the former capital of the island o...</span></span></span>

## Who founded the city?

Pohnpeian oral traditions describe Nan Madol through the story of two brothers, often rendered in English as Olisihpa and Olosohpa. They arrived from elsewhere, possessed sacred or magical powers and established the line from which the Saudeleur rulers descended. Some versions say supernatural assistance enabled the movement of the enormous stones. The traditions then portray later Saudeleurs as increasingly oppressive before their rule was ended by Isokelekel, an outsider who invaded Pohnpei and inaugurated a new political order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nps.gov/places/nan-madol.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nps.gov">[National Park Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nps.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nan madol</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nan madol</span></span></span>

These accounts should not be reduced to either literal reportage or meaningless fantasy. Oral traditions can preserve political relationships, remembered migrations, genealogies and explanations of legitimate authority while also organising them through sacred narrative. A flying creature or magical stone transport does not function like an engineer’s logbook, but the stories may still encode the belief that Nan Madol’s founders possessed unusual ritual power and came from beyond the community over which they eventually ruled. Research elsewhere in Micronesia likewise shows that archaeology and oral history can illuminate one another without necessarily matching in every detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.lib.washington.edu/index.php/BIPPA/article/view/11816/10444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.lib.washington.edu">[journals.lib.washington.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.lib.washington.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">oral traditions and archaeology in micronesia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">oral traditions and archaeology in micronesia</span></span></span>

The identity and origin of the founders therefore remain open to interpretation. Archaeology confirms local construction within Pohnpei’s cultural world, but it cannot establish whether the founding lineage descended from immigrants, arose locally and later claimed foreign origins, or combined outsiders with existing chiefly families. Claims that the traditions prove intervention by Egyptians, Americans, extraterrestrials or survivors of a lost global civilisation go far beyond the evidence.

The strongest conclusion is more restrained: Nan Madol was built by Pohnpeian ancestors during a period of increasing political centralisation. Its foundation stories preserve local explanations of sacred authority, migration and rulership, but they do not provide a simple chronological record that can be read independently of archaeology.

## Why was Nan Madol abandoned?

The end of the Saudeleur system is traditionally associated with Isokelekel’s conquest. Accounts differ over his origins and the details of the campaign, but his victory represents a decisive change from centralised Saudeleur rule to the chiefly arrangements that followed. Nan Madol continued to hold ceremonial importance, yet it ceased to operate as the permanent political centre it had once been. By the early nineteenth century, it was no longer inhabited as a functioning capital.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1503.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage Centre1503.pdfThe Japan Consortium for International Cooperation in Cultural Heritage (JCIC-. Heritage) funded a su...</span></span></span>

No single surviving source explains the abandonment. Political overthrow is the clearest traditional answer, but the physical setting may also have made permanent occupation burdensome. Nan Madol lacked easy access to agricultural land and depended on supplies brought from elsewhere. Its canals and artificial platforms required maintenance in a wet tropical environment. Once rulers could no longer compel regular tribute and labour, sustaining a large elite centre offshore may have offered fewer advantages than living within communities on the main island.

This interpretation fits the evidence better than a sudden unexplained catastrophe. There is no persuasive archaeological case that Nan Madol was destroyed by an ancient superweapon, submerged in a single apocalyptic flood or deserted because its non-Pohnpeian builders mysteriously vanished. Political decentralisation, changes in chiefly authority and the practical cost of supporting the complex provide a plausible combined explanation, even though the timing and sequence remain imperfectly documented.

Abandonment has also contributed to the site’s deterioration. UNESCO placed Nan Madol on the List of World Heritage in Danger when it was inscribed in 2016. Siltation, mangrove growth and weakening masonry threaten structures and obscure parts of the ancient waterways. The mystery is therefore partly archival: every collapsed wall or disturbed deposit can remove evidence that might have clarified how the city developed and declined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1503/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreNan Madol: Ceremonial Centre of Eastern MicronesiaThe site was also inscribed on the List of World Heritage i...</span></span></span>

## How the lost-civilisation myth took over

Nan Madol’s ruins attracted foreign speculation because early European and American visitors encountered a monumental landscape without written construction records familiar to them. Popular writers subsequently linked it to imaginary continents such as Mu or Lemuria. Modern versions extend the site’s history back tens of thousands of years and suggest that Pohnpeians merely inherited the work of a forgotten advanced civilisation.

The dating evidence directly contradicts that chronology. Monumental activity beginning around AD 1180–1200 is separated from the end of the last Ice Age by roughly ten millennia. The basalt can also be traced to Pohnpei itself, while the architecture fits the development of complex societies in the wider Pacific rather than an intrusive global culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fsmculture.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/mccoy_athens_2011-libre.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fsmculture.wordpress.com">[FSM National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fsmculture.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FSM National Archives Sourcing the Megalithic Stones of Nan Madol</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FSM National ArchivesSourcing the Megalithic Stones of Nan MadolJanuary 28, 2014 — by MD McCoy · Cited by 28 — Nan Madol is a massive hec...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 28, 2014</span></span></span>

These claims are not harmless additions to the story. They transfer achievement away from the ancestors of the people who preserve the site’s traditions. Pacific archaeologists and Indigenous critics have objected that presenting Nan Madol as evidence of an unknown superior civilisation repeats an old colonial assumption: that island communities could not have produced technically impressive monuments without outside direction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/article/2024/jul/01/netflix-ancient-apocalypse-canceled" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The real questions are both more defensible and more interesting. How did rulers organise work across Pohnpei? Did construction proceed continuously or in bursts? How were duties divided among districts and clans? What political events lie behind the remembered fall of the Saudeleurs? None requires imaginary continents, and none has yet been answered completely.

## Leluh and Kosrae’s missing royal dead

On Kosrae, the ruined centre of Leluh offers a significant comparison with Nan Madol. Leluh developed as the seat of a centralised island polity, with basalt and coral walls, elite enclosures, ceremonial structures and royal tombs. Research places its major development broadly within the period from the thirteenth century onwards, with political organisation becoming more clearly stratified by the mid-fourteenth to early fifteenth centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4643814/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Its royal tombs create an unusual archaeological puzzle. Oral information recorded by researchers indicates that the bodies of kings and high chiefs might be placed in monumental tombs temporarily. Their remains could later be exhumed and transferred to a reef opening or sea burial location. Excavation has supplied evidence of mortuary activity but has not produced a simple sequence in which every ruler remained permanently sealed within a named tomb.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://micronesica.org/sites/default/files/cordy2022.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: micronesica.org">[micronesica.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">micronesica.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on micronesica.org.</span></span></span>

This helps explain why monumental structures that resemble permanent mausoleums may not contain the complete burials expected by an outsider. The apparent “missing bodies” need not indicate tomb robbery, a massacre or secret escape. They may reflect a multi-stage funeral system in which a stone tomb served as one station in the treatment of royal remains.

Important uncertainties persist. Archaeologists cannot identify every tomb occupant or match all structures to remembered rulers. Nor is the exact relationship between Leluh and earlier chiefly centres fully settled. Sites such as Likinlulem, associated in oral history with paramount chiefs before Leluh’s ascendancy, indicate that Kosrae’s political centralisation developed from an older and more complicated landscape rather than appearing suddenly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likinlulem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Nan Madol and Leluh are sometimes connected through migration stories or suggestions that builders from one centre inspired the other. Similarities are real: both used imposing basalt architecture to express hierarchy, ritual and authority. However, resemblance alone does not demonstrate that one city was founded by refugees or specialists from the other. Dating, local development and regional exchange all need to be considered before oral links are treated as literal population movements.

## Yap’s stone that remained valuable underwater

Yap’s stone currency provides a different sort of mystery. Large carved discs were made from limestone obtained beyond Yap, especially in Palau, and transported over dangerous sea routes. Their value depended on more than dimensions. A stone’s history, workmanship, previous ownership and the risks involved in obtaining it could all matter. Physical possession was not always necessary: ownership could be publicly recognised even when a large stone remained where it stood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://repository.si.edu/bitstream/handle/10088/2422/SSHT-0023_Hi_res.pdf?isAllowed=y&amp;sequence=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.si.edu">[Smithsonian Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Research Online THE STONE MONEY OF YAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Research Online THE STONE MONEY OF YAP</span></span></span>

The most famous story was recorded by William Henry Furness, who visited Yap in 1903 and published *The Island of [Stone Money]({{ 'stone-money/' | relative_url }})* in 1910. He related that a particularly valuable stone had been lost during transport in a storm. Although it lay unrecovered beneath the sea, the community accepted the account of its loss and continued to recognise the owner’s wealth. The stone’s location had changed, but its socially acknowledged history had not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/72830" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

Was there really a specific stone on the seabed? Furness was reporting a story told to him, not documenting a wreck or inspecting the object. No known archaeological recovery has verified that individual stone. Its fame rests on the plausibility and meaning of the account rather than physical confirmation.

The episode is often described as an ancient version of a digital ledger or cryptocurrency. That comparison captures one feature—the separation of acknowledged ownership from possession—but can also distort the system. Yapese stone currency was embedded in kinship, rank, ceremony, exchange and remembered voyages. It was not simply a technical mechanism for recording anonymous balances. Later economists frequently extracted Furness’s picturesque anecdote from that social setting to illustrate their own theories of money.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://miltonfriedman.hoover.org/internal/media/dispatcher/215061/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: miltonfriedman.hoover.org">[hoover.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">miltonfriedman.hoover.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hoover.org.</span></span></span>

The underwater stone is therefore both a historical mystery and a warning about sources. The event may have happened much as Furness described, but almost everything widely repeated about it passes through one foreign author’s account. Its deeper significance lies not in proving that Yap invented modern finance, but in showing that value can depend upon collective memory, reputation and an object’s history rather than immediate physical control.

## What remains genuinely unresolved

The major mysteries of the Federated States of Micronesia are not evidence of absent civilisations. They concern the operation of real island societies whose archives were preserved through landscapes, structures, genealogies and oral traditions rather than large collections of contemporary writing.

Several questions remain particularly important:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* **Nan Madol’s labour system:** archaeological science identifies dates and stone sources, but not the exact obligations, ceremonies and political negotiations behind construction.
* **The historical core of the Saudeleur narratives:** the traditions probably preserve memories of political change, yet individual rulers, migrations and battles cannot always be matched to securely dated evidence.
* **The pace of Nan Madol’s decline:** Isokelekel’s victory is central to local memory, while practical, environmental and political factors probably contributed to the centre’s abandonment.
* **Leluh’s mortuary sequence:** royal remains may have been moved deliberately, leaving incomplete archaeological traces and uncertain identities.
* **The lost Yapese stone:** the story is historically influential and culturally plausible, but the object itself has never been independently verified.

</div>

These are productive uncertainties rather than invitations to invent grand conspiracies. They show how history is reconstructed when masonry, excavated deposits and living tradition preserve different parts of the past. In Micronesia, the most responsible investigation begins by taking all three seriously—and by recognising that uncertainty can survive even after supernatural or pseudoarchaeological explanations have been set aside.
