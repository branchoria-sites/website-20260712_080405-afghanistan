## Introduction

Hidden in the abandoned Allchar mine in southern [North Macedonia]({{ 'what-do-north-macedonias-mysteries/' | relative_url }}) is one of the world's most unusual scientific sites. Rather than preserving gold or silver alone, the deposit contains exceptional quantities of **lorandite**, a rare thallium mineral that may have quietly recorded the Sun's behaviour for more than four million years. The central idea is remarkable: as solar neutrinos passed through the Earth, a tiny number interacted with thallium atoms inside the mineral, leaving behind a measurable atomic signature. If that signature can be separated from every other process that also alters the mineral, Allchar could provide an average record of the Sun's neutrino output over geological time rather than the few decades covered by modern detectors. The scientific challenge is that the geological history of the mine is almost as important as the physics, and reconstructing that history has proved extraordinarily difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050008/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.ucl.ac.uk">[ucl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCL DiscoveryDetermining erosion rates in allchar (Macedonia) to revive the lorandite neutrino experiment - UCL Discovery...</span></span></span>

## Why lorandite is scientifically unusual

Lorandite (TlAsS₂) is exceptionally rare worldwide, but Allchar hosts the largest known economically significant concentration. That alone attracted mineralogists, yet physicists recognised another extraordinary property.

The naturally abundant isotope thallium-205 can capture an electron neutrino emitted by the Sun, transforming into lead-205. Because solar neutrinos interact with matter only extremely rarely, almost every neutrino passes straight through the Earth. Over millions of years, however, enough interactions should accumulate inside a large body of lorandite to leave a detectable record. This idea became the basis of the international **LORandite EXperiment (LOREX)**, first proposed to measure the long-term average flux of low-energy proton–proton (pp) solar neutrinos—the dominant neutrinos produced by nuclear fusion in the Sun.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4984835/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Unlike modern neutrino observatories, which record events in real time using enormous underground detectors filled with water or liquid scintillator, Allchar offers the possibility of a natural detector that has been "recording" continuously for roughly 4.3 million years. If successful, it would provide a geological archive extending far beyond the lifespan of any human-built experiment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.ugd.edu.mk/6422/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.ugd.edu.mk">[UGD Academic Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.ugd.edu.mk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UGD Academic RepositoryState of geoscientific research within the lorandite experiment (LOREX) - UGD Academic RepositoryMay 28, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 28, 2013</span></span></span>

## How the proposed solar neutrino record works

The proposed mechanism is conceptually simple but experimentally demanding.

Solar neutrinos constantly pass through the lorandite crystals. On very rare occasions, a neutrino converts a thallium-205 nucleus into lead-205. Scientists can then attempt to measure the amount of lead-205 that accumulated during the mineral's lifetime.

In principle, the process allows researchers to estimate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* the long-term average solar neutrino flux over several million years;
* whether the Sun's energy-producing core has remained stable across that period; and
* an independent comparison with predictions from solar physics and modern neutrino experiments.

</div>

Because the mineral formed naturally and remained in the ground for millions of years, it acts as a geological integrator rather than a short-term detector. Instead of measuring today's neutrino rate, it averages an immense span of solar history into a single measurable quantity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.ugd.edu.mk/6428/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.ugd.edu.mk">[UGD Academic Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.ugd.edu.mk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UGD Academic RepositoryStatus of the geochemical determination of the solar pp-neutrino flux by LOREX - UGD Academic Repository...</span></span></span>

## Why geology is the real obstacle

The mystery surrounding Allchar is not whether neutrinos exist or whether they can produce lead-205. Both are well established. The unresolved question is whether the measured lead comes predominantly from solar neutrinos or from other geological processes.

The greatest complication is **erosion**.

If the deposit originally lay much deeper underground than it does today, the surrounding rock would have shielded it from cosmic-ray muons. If, however, the deposit spent long periods close to the surface, cosmic rays could also have produced lead-205 through different nuclear reactions. The final measurement would then contain a mixture of signals rather than a clean record of the Sun.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050008/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.ucl.ac.uk">[UCL Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCL DiscoveryDetermining erosion rates in allchar (Macedonia) to revive the lorandite neutrino experiment - UCL Discovery...</span></span></span>

This means researchers must reconstruct millions of years of landscape evolution before they can confidently interpret the physics. Questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* How rapidly has the mountain been eroded?
* How deeply buried was the lorandite through time?
* Were parts of the deposit uplifted or buried during different geological periods?
* Did different ore bodies experience different erosion histories?

</div>

These are geological questions, yet they determine whether the neutrino experiment succeeds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4984835/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## What decades of erosion studies have shown

Much of the LOREX programme has therefore focused on measuring long-term erosion rather than neutrinos directly.

Researchers have analysed cosmogenic isotopes such as beryllium-10, aluminium-26, chlorine-36, helium-3 and neon-21 in surrounding rocks to estimate how quickly the landscape has worn away. Multiple independent studies broadly agree that erosion has occurred but suggest that the lorandite deposit spent most of its history at depths substantially greater than its present position. Those depths would have reduced unwanted muon-produced lead and improve the prospects for recovering a genuine solar signal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4984835/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

A 2018 reassessment was particularly significant. Earlier concerns suggested the deposit might have been too shallow for the experiment to work at all. New erosion-rate estimates instead indicated that the ore body probably remained more than about 400 metres below the surface for most of its existence, making the desired neutrino signal theoretically capable of dominating over cosmic-ray contamination. The study therefore revived scientific interest rather than proving the experiment had already succeeded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050008/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.ucl.ac.uk">[UCL Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCL DiscoveryDetermining erosion rates in allchar (Macedonia) to revive the lorandite neutrino experiment - UCL Discovery...</span></span></span>

Even so, uncertainties remain. Erosion rates vary across the landscape, some locations preserve evidence for more complex burial histories, and the deposit itself formed through multiple geological phases. These complications mean that converting measured lead-205 into an accurate solar neutrino history remains technically challenging.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4984835/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## What remains uncertain

Despite decades of work, Allchar has not yet delivered a definitive four-million-year record of solar neutrino activity.

Several issues continue to limit confidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The exact long-term burial history of every sampled lorandite body cannot be reconstructed with complete certainty.
* Separating neutrino-produced lead from lead generated by cosmic-ray muons requires exceptionally precise geological modelling.
* Measuring extremely small quantities of lead-205 is itself a demanding analytical problem.
* Nuclear physics measurements of the relevant reaction parameters have required continual refinement to reduce uncertainties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.ugd.edu.mk/6428/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.ugd.edu.mk">[edu.mk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.ugd.edu.mk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UGD Academic RepositoryStatus of the geochemical determination of the solar pp-neutrino flux by LOREX - UGD Academic Repository...</span></span></span>

</div>

As a result, the scientific discussion has shifted away from asking whether the concept is ingenious—it clearly is—to asking whether every geological uncertainty can be constrained well enough for the final neutrino measurement to be trusted.

## Why Allchar still matters

Allchar occupies a unique place in both North Macedonian natural history and international physics. Few abandoned mines have become central to attempts to reconstruct the long-term behaviour of the Sun.

The enduring mystery is therefore not an archaeological puzzle or a lost treasure, but an unresolved scientific one: can a rare mineral preserve a reliable archive of millions of years of solar activity? Research over the past decade has strengthened the case that the experiment is physically plausible by improving understanding of the site's erosion history. Yet the same studies also demonstrate why extracting the ancient neutrino record is so demanding. The rocks preserve an extraordinary possibility, but whether they preserve a sufficiently unambiguous record of the Sun remains an open scientific question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050008/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.ucl.ac.uk">[ucl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCL DiscoveryDetermining erosion rates in allchar (Macedonia) to revive the lorandite neutrino experiment - UCL Discovery...</span></span></span>
