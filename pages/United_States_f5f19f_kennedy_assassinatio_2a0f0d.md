## Introduction

The assassination of President John F. Kennedy on 22 November 1963 remains one of the most debated events in American history not because the central evidence is entirely unknown, but because confidence in the official investigations never fully recovered. The physical evidence linking Lee Harvey Oswald to the shooting is substantial and has survived repeated scrutiny. At the same time, investigative shortcomings, inconsistent witness testimony, delayed disclosure of government records and changing official conclusions created an atmosphere in which public suspicion flourished. Understanding why the case still divides America therefore requires separating two different questions: what the available evidence says about the shooting itself, and why so many people came to distrust the institutions responsible for explaining it.

## The case against Lee Harvey Oswald

The evidence placing Lee Harvey Oswald at the centre of the assassination remains stronger than many popular accounts suggest. Although historians continue to debate individual details, several points are supported by multiple independent lines of evidence.

Oswald worked at the Texas School Book Depository overlooking the presidential motorcade route. He was present in the building when the shots were fired, and investigators concluded that the rifle recovered from the sixth floor had been purchased by him under an alias. Ballistic examinations connected cartridge cases found at the scene to that rifle, while photographs, documents and witness testimony supported his ownership of the weapon. The Warren Commission concluded that Kennedy was struck by shots fired from behind, and later forensic reviews generally upheld that broad conclusion, even while reassessing aspects of the original investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-1a.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Findings &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesFindings &#124; National ArchivesAugust 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

Oswald's actions immediately after the assassination also shaped investigators' conclusions. He left the Depository shortly after the shooting, later killed Dallas police officer J. D. Tippit, and was arrested in a nearby cinema while carrying a revolver. These actions have long been interpreted as consistent with someone attempting to escape capture rather than an uninvolved bystander. The House Select Committee on Assassinations (HSCA), despite disagreeing with parts of the Warren Commission, likewise concluded that Oswald fired the shots from the Depository and that his behaviour strongly supported that conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/summary.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Summary of Findings &#124; National ArchivesAugust 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

This does not mean every forensic question has been settled beyond dispute. Debate continues over bullet trajectories, the interpretation of certain fragments and aspects of the autopsy. However, most mainstream historical and forensic assessments still regard the evidence that Oswald fired at President Kennedy as considerably stronger than claims that he played no role at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-1a.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Findings &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesFindings &#124; National ArchivesAugust 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

## Why the second-gunman debate refuses to disappear

The most enduring alternative explanation is that another gunman fired from the grassy knoll or elsewhere in Dealey Plaza.

The principal reason this theory gained official attention was not eyewitness testimony alone but the HSCA's 1979 conclusion that acoustic evidence from a Dallas police recording suggested a high probability of a second gunman. Based partly on this analysis, the committee stated that Kennedy was "probably assassinated as a result of a conspiracy", while simultaneously affirming that Oswald had fired at the President.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/summary.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Summary of Findings &#124; National ArchivesAugust 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

However, this conclusion soon became controversial. Later scientific reviews challenged the timing and interpretation of the recording, arguing that the supposed gunshot impulses were probably recorded after the assassination rather than during it. As confidence in the acoustic evidence declined, the principal scientific basis for the HSCA's conspiracy finding weakened substantially, even though the committee's report itself remained part of the historical record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-1b.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Findings &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Findings &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

Other disputed issues continue to attract attention:

* **The "single-bullet" theory.** Critics argue that one bullet causing multiple wounds appears improbable. Supporters respond that the relative seating positions of Kennedy and Texas Governor John Connally make the trajectory physically possible and consistent with available forensic evidence.
* **Witness testimony.** Some witnesses believed shots came from the grassy knoll, while others consistently identified the Depository. Modern researchers note that echoes within Dealey Plaza make the direction of gunfire difficult to judge reliably.
* **Ballistic fragments.** Later statistical studies questioned earlier interpretations of bullet composition, arguing that forensic conclusions about the number of bullets may have been more uncertain than once believed. These studies raised questions deserving further examination but did not themselves demonstrate the existence of a second shooter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/0712.2150" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chemical and forensic analysis of JFK assassination bullet lots: Is a second shooter possible?December 13, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 13, 2007</span></span></span>

As a result, the strongest current historical position is more limited than many conspiracy narratives suggest. There remain unresolved forensic questions, but none has produced a broadly accepted alternative reconstruction that explains the entire body of physical evidence better than the conclusion that Oswald fired from the Depository.

## Why secrecy damaged public confidence

Public distrust did not arise solely from competing interpretations of forensic evidence. It also reflected the behaviour of government institutions before, during and after the investigation.

The Warren Commission worked under enormous political pressure. President Lyndon Johnson feared that speculation about foreign involvement could provoke an international crisis during the Cold War. Although the Commission concluded that Oswald acted alone, later investigations criticised its treatment of conspiracy possibilities and found that it had not received all relevant information held by federal agencies. The HSCA concluded that both the FBI and the CIA had failed to share important information fully and that the Warren Commission's conclusions had been presented with greater certainty than the available evidence justified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/summary.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Summary of Findings &#124; National ArchivesAugust 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

Those criticisms proved historically significant. Even readers who accepted the basic case against Oswald could reasonably question why intelligence agencies withheld information or failed to cooperate completely.

Subsequent political events deepened this scepticism. The Vietnam War, the Pentagon Papers and the Watergate scandal convinced many Americans that senior government officials were capable of concealing important facts. The Kennedy assassination therefore became part of a broader crisis of institutional trust rather than an isolated criminal investigation.

## Record releases and the persistence of doubt

Another reason the assassination continues to generate suspicion is the exceptionally long history of classified records.

The 1992 President John F. Kennedy Assassination Records Collection Act required federal agencies to gather and release assassination-related records after public pressure intensified following renewed interest in the case. Millions of pages have since become publicly available through the National Archives, with repeated rounds of additional declassification over subsequent decades. While the overwhelming majority of records have now been released, some documents remained partially redacted for years because of intelligence or national security concerns, reinforcing public suspicion that important facts were still being withheld.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesFrequently Asked Questions about JFK Assassination Records &#124; National ArchivesApril 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2025</span></span></span>

Importantly, the gradual release of records has not produced decisive evidence proving a large conspiracy. Instead, historians have found extensive information about intelligence operations, Cold War politics, surveillance of Oswald and bureaucratic failures. These materials have enriched understanding of the historical context but have not fundamentally overturned the central physical evidence against Oswald.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesFrequently Asked Questions about JFK Assassination Records &#124; National ArchivesApril 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why the case still divides America

The Kennedy assassination illustrates an important distinction between uncertainty in evidence and uncertainty in trust.

The available forensic and documentary record continues to support the conclusion that Lee Harvey Oswald fired at President Kennedy. Yet the investigations that established this conclusion were marked by incomplete information sharing, disputed forensic interpretations and institutional secrecy that later official inquiries themselves acknowledged. Those failures created space for competing explanations that have endured for generations.

For many Americans, the lasting mystery is therefore not simply whether another gunman existed. It is whether the government fully disclosed everything it knew, investigated every plausible lead with sufficient rigour and communicated its findings honestly. Even where the physical evidence appears comparatively strong, the erosion of institutional credibility has ensured that the Kennedy assassination remains both a historical case study in forensic investigation and a lasting symbol of public distrust.
