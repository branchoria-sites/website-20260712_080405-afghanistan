## Introduction

Sigiriya is one of [Sri Lanka]({{ 'what-really-lies-behind-sri-lankas/' | relative_url }})'s most celebrated archaeological sites, yet its greatest mystery is not whether it existed as a royal capital, but what its most famous artworks were meant to communicate. Built during the reign of King Kassapa I (477–495 CE), the complex combines landscaped gardens, water engineering, monumental architecture, polished ceremonial spaces and cliff paintings into a carefully designed environment. While the site's broad historical outline is well established, scholars continue to debate the meaning of its surviving frescoes, the purpose of the Mirror Wall, and the symbolism of the rock itself. The mystery endures because no fifth-century text explains the original programme. Instead, historians must reconstruct its meaning from archaeology, later chronicles, medieval graffiti and comparisons with South Asian art.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01745-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An analytical review of ancient coating technology employed at Sigiriya, Sri Lanka &#124; npj Heritage ScienceJuly 30, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2025</span></span></span>

## What is firmly known about Kassapa's royal complex

There is little disagreement about the basic chronology. After seizing the throne, King Kassapa established a royal centre at Sigiriya, transforming an immense volcanic rock into an elaborate ceremonial landscape. The complex included symmetrical water gardens, terraced gardens, reservoirs, fountains, defensive works, monumental stairways and structures on the summit. After Kassapa's death, the site reverted to use as a Buddhist monastery for many centuries.

Rather than being simply a fortress, Sigiriya was carefully planned from the surrounding gardens to the palace summit. Archaeologists have long argued that its design deliberately combined practical engineering with symbolic display. The ascent itself was choreographed, leading visitors through increasingly dramatic spaces before reaching the summit. This strongly suggests that movement through the landscape formed part of the site's intended meaning rather than merely providing access to the palace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01745-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An analytical review of ancient coating technology employed at Sigiriya, Sri Lanka &#124; npj Heritage ScienceJuly 30, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2025</span></span></span>

The famous frescoes occupy only a small surviving section of what was probably a much larger painted programme on the western face of the rock. Early observers proposed that paintings once stretched across a vast area of the cliff, creating what one twentieth-century scholar described as an enormous outdoor picture gallery. Today only a few dozen female figures survive, preserved in sheltered rock pockets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01745-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An analytical review of ancient coating technology employed at Sigiriya, Sri Lanka &#124; npj Heritage ScienceJuly 30, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2025</span></span></span>

## Who were the women painted at Sigiriya?

The identity of the women remains the site's best-known historical puzzle. No surviving inscription identifies them, and several competing explanations continue to attract scholarly support.

The principal interpretations include:

* **Celestial maidens or divine beings.** This has long been the most widely accepted interpretation. The women emerge from banks of cloud, wear elaborate jewellery, carry flowers and appear suspended above the landscape rather than standing upon it. Their placement and idealised appearance resemble celestial attendants found in other South Asian artistic traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://imp-art.org/articles/sigiriya-murals-and-mirror-wall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: imp-art.org">[Impart]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">imp-art.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on imp-art.org.</span></span></span>
* **Royal women from Kassapa's court.** Earlier archaeologists suggested that the paintings portrayed queens, attendants or members of the royal household. Their luxurious dress and ornaments fit elite status, although no direct evidence links individual figures to historical people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://imp-art.org/articles/sigiriya-murals-and-mirror-wall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: imp-art.org">[Impart]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">imp-art.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on imp-art.org.</span></span></span>
* **Participants in religious rituals.** Some researchers argue that the women represent ceremonial flower bearers approaching a sacred shrine. The flowers held by many figures support this possibility, although the destination of the procession is unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sigiriya.com/article/sigiriya-frescoes-discover-the-beauty-of-ancient-murals" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sigiriya.com">[Sigiriya]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sigiriya.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frescoes: Discover the Beauty of Ancient Murals</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sigiriya Frescoes: Discover the Beauty of Ancient Murals...</span></span></span>
* **Symbols rather than portraits.** Other scholars interpret the paintings as allegorical figures representing clouds, fertility, prosperity or ideal feminine beauty rather than identifiable individuals. This view fits the broader symbolic character of the landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379481651_Centuries-Old_Whispers_of_Sentiments_Unveiling_Poetic_Sexual_Fantasies_in_Graffiti_on_Ladies_in_the_Sigiriya_Frescoes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

None of these explanations has achieved universal acceptance because the surviving evidence is incomplete. The paintings themselves contain no labels, while the written evidence comes centuries after their creation.

## Why the Mirror Wall matters so much

One reason the debate survives is that Sigiriya preserves an extraordinary record of how later visitors reacted to the paintings.

The Mirror Wall was originally coated with highly polished plaster that reflected light so effectively that later tradition claimed the king could see his reflection while walking beside it. Between roughly the sixth and fourteenth centuries, hundreds of visitors scratched poems and comments into the surface. These inscriptions are among the earliest surviving examples of Sinhala literature and preserve remarkably personal responses to the frescoes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01745-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An analytical review of ancient coating technology employed at Sigiriya, Sri Lanka &#124; npj Heritage ScienceJuly 30, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2025</span></span></span>

The poems rarely attempt to identify the women historically. Instead they praise beauty, compare the figures to heavenly beings, express longing or admiration, and sometimes reveal romantic or erotic reactions. Their value lies less in explaining the original paintings than in demonstrating how medieval audiences understood them centuries later.

Equally important, the graffiti show that Sigiriya remained an active destination long after Kassapa's reign. Poets, monks, officials, soldiers and women all left inscriptions, indicating that the site functioned as a place of cultural memory rather than simply an abandoned palace. Recent scholarship has also highlighted that some identifiable female visitors composed their own verses, providing unusually early female literary voices within Sri Lankan history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sjp.ac.lk/index.php/pumithiri/article/view/7820" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sjp.ac.lk">[USJ Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sjp.ac.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">USJ JournalsTHE SOCIAL ROLES OF WOMEN AS PORTRAYED IN SIGIRI POETRY &#124; පුමිතිරි &#x27;Pumithiri&#x27; e-Journal of Gender StudiesDecember 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## Was the rock meant to symbolise something larger?

The frescoes cannot easily be separated from the architecture surrounding them. Their position midway up the cliff, immediately above the Mirror Wall and below the summit, appears deliberate.

Several broader interpretations have emerged.

One sees Sigiriya as a royal palace whose splendour proclaimed Kassapa's legitimacy after his controversial seizure of power. The paintings would therefore reinforce ideals of wealth, beauty and royal authority.

Another argues that the complex represented a sacred mountain. In South Asian cosmology, mountains often served as meeting places between the earthly and divine worlds. The women emerging from clouds would then function as heavenly attendants welcoming visitors ascending toward an elevated symbolic realm rather than merely decorating a palace wall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://imp-art.org/articles/sigiriya-murals-and-mirror-wall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: imp-art.org">[Impart]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">imp-art.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on imp-art.org.</span></span></span>

A third interpretation combines these ideas. Rather than choosing between palace and sanctuary, many archaeologists now regard Sigiriya as a political landscape in which kingship borrowed religious symbolism to strengthen royal authority. The hydraulic gardens, lion gateway, elevated summit and celestial imagery all contributed to presenting the king as ruling within an idealised cosmic order rather than an ordinary residence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01745-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An analytical review of ancient coating technology employed at Sigiriya, Sri Lanka &#124; npj Heritage ScienceJuly 30, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2025</span></span></span>

## What modern research has added

Recent scientific research has shifted attention from simply identifying the women to understanding how the paintings were created and experienced.

Material analysis of the surviving coatings has examined the plaster, pigments and application techniques, showing that the paintings were the product of highly sophisticated local craftsmanship. Researchers continue to debate whether the works should technically be described as true frescoes or paintings executed on dry plaster, but there is broad agreement that their preparation involved multiple carefully engineered layers designed to survive the exposed cliff environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01745-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An analytical review of ancient coating technology employed at Sigiriya, Sri Lanka &#124; npj Heritage ScienceJuly 30, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2025</span></span></span>

Conservation studies have also reinforced how much has been lost. Medieval references suggest there may once have been several hundred painted figures. Weathering, rock falls and centuries of exposure have reduced this enormous programme to the small group visible today, making every interpretation necessarily provisional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sjp.ac.lk/index.php/pumithiri/article/view/8772" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sjp.ac.lk">[USJ Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sjp.ac.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">USJ JournalsWomen’s Ethics in Contemporary Society as Illustrated by Sigiri Paintings: A Study from Archaeological and Modern Perspective...</span></span></span>

Recent cultural scholarship has likewise examined the Mirror Wall not simply as evidence for identifying the women but as a remarkable archive of historical responses to art. The poems reveal changing attitudes toward beauty, devotion, memory and gender over many centuries, showing that Sigiriya acquired new meanings long after its original creators had disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379481651_Centuries-Old_Whispers_of_Sentiments_Unveiling_Poetic_Sexual_Fantasies_in_Graffiti_on_Ladies_in_the_Sigiriya_Frescoes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery endures

The enduring puzzle of Sigiriya lies not in hidden chambers or lost treasure but in missing intention. Archaeology can reconstruct Kassapa's ambitious landscape with considerable confidence, yet it cannot recover the explanation that once connected the paintings, architecture and ceremonial route into a single programme.

The surviving evidence supports several conclusions. The frescoes formed part of a much larger visual scheme. The Mirror Wall proves that generations of visitors found the women captivating, but their poems reflect medieval interpretation rather than fifth-century meaning. The architecture was carefully designed to communicate royal authority through spectacle, engineering and movement, yet whether that authority was presented chiefly as political power, sacred kingship or a vision of a mythical mountain remains unresolved.

That uncertainty explains why Sigiriya continues to fascinate historians. Its mystery is not whether the site was built, who commissioned it or how its engineering worked. The unanswered question is how all its surviving elements—paintings, poetry, polished walls, gardens and towering rock—were intended to speak to one another as a single expression of power, belief and kingship.
