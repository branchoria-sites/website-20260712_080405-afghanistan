## Introduction

The men and women whom British officials dismissed as the “Brigands” were not simply outlaws hiding in [Saint Lucia]({{ 'which-stories-shaped-saint-lucias-hidden/' | relative_url }})’s forests. They were a broad coalition of formerly enslaved people, maroons (people who had escaped slavery), free people of colour, French Republican soldiers and local supporters who fought during the 1790s against British occupation and attempts to restore plantation slavery. The mystery is not whether they existed, but why their historical reputation became so sharply divided. Were they criminals, revolutionary extremists or freedom fighters? The answer depends largely on whose records are being read. British military reports portrayed them as bandits threatening order, while French Republican accounts and much later Saint Lucian scholarship describe them as participants in one of the Caribbean’s earliest organised struggles for emancipation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Saint_Lucia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of Saint Lucia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of Saint Lucia</span></span></span>

The debate matters because the label “brigand” shaped public memory for generations. It obscured the fact that many of these fighters were resisting the re-imposition of slavery at a time when the French Republic had formally abolished it. Modern historians increasingly view the Brigand Wars as part of the wider Atlantic Age of Revolution, linking Saint Lucia to events in Guadeloupe, Haiti and other Caribbean colonies rather than treating the conflict as ordinary colonial banditry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Saint_Lucia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of Saint Lucia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of Saint Lucia</span></span></span>

## Revolution and abolition in the 1790s

Saint Lucia changed hands repeatedly between Britain and France during the eighteenth century, but the revolutionary decade of the 1790s transformed the island into a battlefield over slavery as much as empire. News of the French Revolution reached the Caribbean quickly, and French Republican commissioners encouraged ideas of citizenship and liberty among free people of colour and the enslaved population. In February 1794, the French National Convention abolished slavery throughout French territories, fundamentally altering the political landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Saint_Lucia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of Saint Lucia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of Saint Lucia</span></span></span>

Britain, however, occupied Saint Lucia during much of this period and had no intention of recognising French abolition. Plantation owners feared both economic collapse and a Haitian-style revolution. As British forces attempted to restore colonial rule, many enslaved people fled plantations, joined maroon communities and took up arms alongside French Republican forces. Resistance therefore became both an anti-colonial war and a struggle against slavery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.slucia.com/visions/negre.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slucia.com">[slucia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slucia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visions Official Tourist Guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Lucia Caribbean...</span></span></span>

This context explains why the same individuals could be described in completely different ways. To British commanders they were armed rebels disrupting lawful authority. To many participants they were defending newly declared freedom against a return to enslavement.

## The French Army in the Woods

The movement that British sources labelled the Brigands was known among its supporters as *L'Armée Française dans les Bois*—the French Army in the Woods. Rather than operating as scattered bands of fugitives, it developed into a surprisingly organised force.

French Republican officials, particularly those connected with the revolutionary administration in Guadeloupe under Victor Hugues, supplied weapons, officers and political direction. Arms were smuggled into Saint Lucia by sea, recruits were trained in forest camps, and military ranks were established. Contemporary accounts describe recruitment methods, hidden supply routes and coordinated attacks on British positions rather than random acts of violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.slucia.com/visions/negre.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slucia.com">[slucia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slucia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visions Official Tourist Guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Lucia Caribbean...</span></span></span>

The force itself was remarkably diverse. It included:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* formerly enslaved Africans seeking permanent freedom;
* maroons already living beyond plantation control;
* free people of colour, many of whom possessed military experience;
* French Republican soldiers and officers;
* local supporters who opposed the return of British plantation rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Saint_Lucia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of Saint Lucia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of Saint Lucia</span></span></span>

</div>

In February 1795 Brigand forces defeated a British detachment, and over the following months British authority collapsed across much of the island. For roughly a year, sometimes remembered locally as *l'Année de la Liberté* ("the Year of Freedom"), slavery was effectively abolished under French Republican administration before Britain launched another successful invasion in 1796.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Saint_Lucia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of Saint Lucia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of Saint Lucia</span></span></span>

## Why were they called "brigands"?

The word "brigand" was already widely used in eighteenth-century Europe for armed irregulars, robbers or insurgents. British officials deliberately adopted the term because it denied the fighters any political legitimacy.

Describing opponents as brigands rather than soldiers carried important consequences:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* it suggested they were criminals rather than combatants;
* it justified harsh military reprisals instead of negotiated treatment;
* it reinforced plantation owners' claims that order, not slavery, was the issue;
* it discouraged recognition that many fighters were defending emancipation already proclaimed under French law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Saint_Lucia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of Saint Lucia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of Saint Lucia</span></span></span>

</div>

This was not unique to Saint Lucia. Across the Caribbean and Europe, governments frequently labelled insurgents as bandits or brigands to strip away political motives. In Saint Lucia's case, however, the label proved especially durable because British rule ultimately prevailed, leaving British administrative records to dominate later historical narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brill.com/view/journals/nwig/88/1-2/article-p221_52.xml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brill.com">[Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jolien Harmsen, Guy Ellis &amp; Robert Devaux A History of St Lucia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Vieux Fort, St Lucia: Lighthouse Road Publications, 2012. xvi + 438 pp. (Paper n.p.) in: New West Indian Guide / Nieuwe West-Indische Gid...</span></span></span>

## How colonial labels shaped public memory

One of the enduring historical puzzles is how a movement seeking freedom became remembered primarily through the vocabulary of its opponents.

For much of the nineteenth century, British colonial histories focused on restoring order after "brigandage", paying relatively little attention to why so many enslaved people joined the resistance. The military campaign appeared as a security problem rather than a political revolution. Only later did Saint Lucian historians begin systematically reassessing surviving French records, local traditions and plantation documents to reconstruct the movement from multiple perspectives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brill.com/view/journals/nwig/88/1-2/article-p221_52.xml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brill.com">[Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jolien Harmsen, Guy Ellis &amp; Robert Devaux A History of St Lucia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Vieux Fort, St Lucia: Lighthouse Road Publications, 2012. xvi + 438 pp. (Paper n.p.) in: New West Indian Guide / Nieuwe West-Indische Gid...</span></span></span>

Works such as Robert J. Devaux's *They Called Us Brigands* argued that the term itself concealed the fighters' objectives and invited readers to reinterpret them as freedom fighters rather than criminals. Although historians still debate individual episodes and military decisions, there is growing agreement that reducing the movement to simple banditry fails to explain its scale, organisation and political goals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://slpl.edu.lc/cgi-bin/koha/opac-detail.pl?biblionumber=33670&amp;shelfbrowse_itemnumber=34377" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slpl.edu.lc">[slpl.edu.lc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slpl.edu.lc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.lc.</span></span></span>

The reassessment also reflects broader changes in Caribbean history. Modern scholarship increasingly examines resistance from the viewpoint of enslaved and free Black communities instead of relying almost exclusively on colonial administrators' reports. This shift has altered not only interpretations of Saint Lucia but of revolutionary movements throughout the region.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/florida-scholarship-online/book/19203/chapter/177680726" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lucia &#124; Archaeologies of Slavery and Freedom in the Caribbean: Exploring the Spaces in Between &#124; Florida Scholarship Online &#124; Oxford Acad...</span></span></span>

## What remains uncertain?

Although the overall character of the movement is much better understood than it once was, several questions remain only partly answered because records are incomplete or dispersed across British and French archives.

The precise size of the Brigand forces is uncertain, as estimates vary between military reports and later historical reconstructions. Individual participants often disappear from the documentary record after the British reconquest, making it difficult to trace their later lives. Some surrendered after receiving assurances that they would not be returned to slavery, while others were deported or vanished into maroon communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Saint_Lucia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of Saint Lucia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of Saint Lucia</span></span></span>

Researchers also continue investigating the movement's leadership, local support networks and the experiences of women who participated in or sustained the resistance. ## Why the Brigands still matter

The Brigands occupy an important place in Saint Lucian history because they challenge a long-standing colonial narrative. Their story demonstrates how political language can shape historical memory as powerfully as military victory.

Today they are increasingly understood as participants in the wider Caribbean struggle over slavery, citizenship and empire that linked Saint Lucia with revolutionary Guadeloupe and Haiti. Rather than being remembered solely as outlaws, they are now widely recognised as people who fought against the restoration of slavery and for a different vision of the island's future.
