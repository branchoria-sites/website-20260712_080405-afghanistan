## Is the Ark of the Covenant really in Aksum?

The most famous Ethiopian mystery centres on a guarded chapel beside the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion in Aksum. The Ethiopian Orthodox Church teaches that the biblical Ark of the Covenant is kept there. Entry is restricted to a single appointed guardian, who remains near the chapel for life. Outsiders are not permitted to inspect the object, so the claim cannot be independently verified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2021/jan/24/fabled-ark-could-be-among-ancient-treasures-in-danger-in-ethiopias-deadly-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Fabled ark could be among ancient treasures in danger</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianFabled ark could be among ancient treasures in danger...January 24, 2021 — 24 Jan 2021 — The ark is believed by Ethiopian Or...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 24, 2021</span></span></span>

The Ethiopian account is tied to the royal tradition that the Queen of Sheba had a son by King Solomon. That son, Menelik, later travelled to Jerusalem and returned to Ethiopia with the Ark. The story became central to the ideology of Ethiopia’s Solomonic monarchy, presenting its rulers as heirs to ancient Israel and Ethiopia as a new Zion.

There is a major chronological problem, however. The fullest Ethiopian version of the story survives in a medieval royal and religious work written many centuries after the supposed age of Solomon. The biblical account describes the Queen of Sheba visiting Solomon, exchanging gifts and returning home, but it does not say that she bore his son or that the Ark was taken to Ethiopia. Nor has an ancient inscription or archaeological find established the Ark’s transfer to Aksum.

The guarded object itself may be ancient, medieval or relatively recent; there is no publicly available examination from which to judge. It is also important not to confuse it with the consecrated altar tablets used throughout Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity. These tablets symbolically represent the Ark and are treated as exceptionally sacred objects. Eleven taken during the British expedition to Maqdala in 1868 remain in the British Museum, where even museum staff restrict how they are handled and displayed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/about-us/british-museum-story/contested-objects-collection/maqdala-collection" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British MuseumMaqdala collectionThe collection includes important liturgical objects such as processional crosses, chalices and umbrella...</span></span></span>

The strongest historical conclusion is therefore limited but significant. The Aksum claim cannot presently be authenticated as the biblical Ark, yet it is not merely an invention of modern tourism. It belongs to a deeply rooted Ethiopian sacred and political tradition whose influence can be seen in church practice, royal legitimacy and national memory. Popular books have turned the question into a treasure hunt, but the real mystery lies in the tension between a living faith and evidence that cannot be inspected.

## Who was the Ethiopian Queen of Sheba?

The Queen of Sheba is mentioned in ancient Jewish and later Christian and Islamic traditions, but none of the earliest accounts gives enough information to identify her securely as an Ethiopian ruler. Many scholars associate Sheba primarily with the ancient Sabaean kingdom in southern Arabia, where inscriptions, monumental architecture and the trade in incense provide a plausible historical setting for a wealthy monarch involved in long-distance commerce.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/3180936" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Queen of Sheba and Solomonby MMKR Monges · 2002 · Cited by 16 — The evidence that she was from Southern Arabia is compelling, be...</span></span></span>

That does not remove Ethiopia from the story. Communities on both sides of the Red Sea were connected by trade, migration, language and political influence. Aksum later ruled territory in Arabia, while earlier South Arabian cultural elements appeared in parts of northern Ethiopia. The ancient Red Sea world did not fit neatly within modern national borders.

Ethiopian tradition gives the queen a much larger role. She becomes the mother of Menelik and the founding ancestor of the monarchy that claimed descent from Solomon. This version helped provide sacred legitimacy to the Solomonic dynasty, which returned to power in the thirteenth century. The tradition was consequently more than a romantic tale: it offered an explanation of kingship, religion and Ethiopia’s place in sacred history.

Occasional archaeological announcements have claimed to locate the queen’s palace, mines or treasures in Ethiopia, but none has produced a scholarly consensus linking a particular site or named ruler to the visitor described in ancient scripture. The material evidence can establish prosperous societies and cross-Red Sea contacts; it cannot yet identify the biblical queen.

The most plausible interpretation is that the Sheba story preserves memories of a wealthy southern Red Sea kingdom, later reshaped by different cultures. Ethiopia’s version should therefore be understood both as a possible echo of real ancient connections and as a medieval statement about national and royal identity. Asking whether the queen was “Ethiopian or Arabian” may impose a modern either-or choice on a world joined by the Red Sea rather than divided by it.

## Were Lalibela’s churches built in one miraculous reign?

Eleven rock-hewn churches at Lalibela form one of Ethiopia’s most extraordinary historical landscapes. Tradition attributes the complex to King Lalibela, who ruled around the turn of the thirteenth century and sought to create a new Jerusalem after access to the Holy Land became difficult. The churches were not assembled from blocks: trenches, courtyards, passages, rooms and façades were cut downwards and inwards from the living rock.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/18/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

A popular sacred account says that human labourers worked during the day while angels continued the carving at night. The legend expresses the buildings’ spiritual importance and the astonishment they inspire. It is not, however, a practical explanation of their construction.

Archaeological and architectural studies increasingly suggest that the complex did not emerge as a single project completed entirely during Lalibela’s reign. Differences in style, tool marks, layout and modification indicate several building phases. Some spaces may originally have served non-church purposes before being altered for Christian worship. The Metropolitan Museum of Art summarises the scholarly position as a sequence of four or five phases extending roughly from the seventh to the thirteenth century, although the dating of individual structures remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/40388317" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">archaeology context for the rock-hewn medieval churches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">archaeology context for the rock-hewn medieval churches</span></span></span>

This does not mean that King Lalibela had no part in the site. He may have sponsored major alterations, connected older monuments into a planned sacred landscape or transformed an existing centre into a royal pilgrimage city. Medieval rulers often received credit for complexes that incorporated earlier work and continued to change after their deaths.

The remaining puzzle is therefore not “how could anyone carve stone?” Quarrying and rock-cut architecture were established skills in the region. The harder questions concern sequence, purpose and organisation: which spaces came first, how many generations worked there, and how an evolving collection of monuments became remembered as one king’s inspired creation. Further archaeological study may refine that story, but the site’s active religious use rightly limits destructive investigation.

## Why did Aksum’s greatest stone monument fall?

Aksum’s royal cemetery contains immense carved stelae, upright stone monuments designed to resemble multi-storey buildings. They probably marked elite burials during the kingdom’s height in the third and fourth centuries. The largest surviving upright example rises more than 23 metres, while the largest attempted monument lies broken on the ground and was originally about 33 metres long. UNESCO describes it as possibly the largest monolithic stele ever attempted in the ancient world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

A familiar explanation says that the giant monument broke while it was being raised. This is plausible at first glance: the stone was extraordinarily heavy, and hauling it upright without modern machinery would have required precise control of ropes, ramps, supports and foundations. A catastrophic failure during erection could explain why it never stood.

Yet archaeological analysis has challenged that simple version. Researchers studying the stelae and their foundations have argued that some technical details are difficult to reconcile with an immediate erection accident. Alternative reconstructions suggest that the monument may have stood before falling because of foundation movement, structural instability, deliberate toppling or seismic activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.openedition.org/palethnologie/5778?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.openedition.org">[OpenEdition Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.openedition.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on openedition.org.</span></span></span>

The smaller Stele 2 demonstrates that Aksumite engineers were capable of raising enormous monuments. It was later removed by Fascist Italy in 1937, returned to Ethiopia in 2005 and re-erected under a UNESCO-supported operation completed in stages in 2008. Excavation associated with that project suggested that Stele 2 itself may have remained upright for several centuries before toppling, cautioning against the assumption that every fallen monument failed during construction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/350" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The fall of the largest stele has sometimes been linked symbolically to Aksum’s conversion to Christianity, as though the collapse marked the end of an older royal funerary culture. That is an intriguing interpretation, but evidence for a direct causal connection is weak. What can be said securely is that monumental stele building ceased as Christian forms of commemoration became dominant.

The mystery endures because several explanations remain mechanically possible and the original event left no known written account. It is a genuine archaeological problem rather than evidence of lost technology. Aksum’s builders clearly possessed remarkable organisational and engineering knowledge; the broken stone reveals that even such expertise could meet geological or structural limits.

## How old are the Garima Gospels?

The illustrated Gospel books preserved at the Abba Garima Monastery in northern Ethiopia were long thought by many outside scholars to date from around the late first millennium or even the medieval period. Monastic tradition, by contrast, associated them with a saint said to have arrived in Ethiopia around the end of the fifth century.

Radiocarbon testing dramatically changed the discussion. Tests on manuscript material produced date ranges of approximately 390–570 for one volume and 530–660 for another. These results place the books within late antiquity and make the earlier manuscript one of the oldest surviving complete illustrated Christian Gospel books.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digital.kenyon.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1209&amp;context=perejournal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digital.kenyon.edu">[Kenyon Digital Scholarship]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digital.kenyon.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kenyon.edu.</span></span></span>

Scientific dating did not prove every part of the monastery’s story. Tradition says the saint completed a Gospel book in a single day after God prevented the sun from setting. Radiocarbon analysis can date parchment or associated organic material, but it cannot verify a miraculous working period or establish the identity of a scribe. Scholars must also distinguish the production of parchment, writing of text, painting of images and later rebinding, which need not all have happened at precisely the same time.

The manuscripts nevertheless overturned an older scholarly assumption that such sophisticated Christian book production must have reached Ethiopia substantially later or depended wholly on imported objects. Their texts, illustrations and preparation point instead to an active manuscript culture in or closely connected with the Aksumite world.

This is a mystery partly resolved. The books are no longer enigmatic because specialists cannot estimate their age; laboratory evidence has greatly narrowed it. What remains uncertain is where every component was produced, who wrote and painted the volumes, how their visual style travelled across the Red Sea and Nile worlds, and how the manuscripts survived for roughly fifteen centuries in a working monastery.

## What happened to the treasures taken from Maqdala?

In 1868, a British military expedition captured Emperor Tewodros II’s mountain stronghold at Maqdala. Tewodros died by suicide as the fortress fell. British troops then removed an enormous quantity of manuscripts, crowns, crosses, textiles, weapons, ecclesiastical objects and other property. Much of it was auctioned to members of the expedition on the nearby plain before entering royal, institutional and private collections. British Museum records openly identify objects as bought at the sale of the plunder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/about-us/british-museum-story/contested-objects-collection/maqdala-collection" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British MuseumMaqdala collectionThe collection includes important liturgical objects such as processional crosses, chalices and umbrella...</span></span></span>

The basic event is not mysterious: the seizure and dispersal are well documented. The archival puzzle concerns the full scale and present location of what was taken. Objects moved through military sales, dealers, inherited collections, libraries and museums. Some were catalogued vaguely or under outdated descriptions; others may remain unidentified in private hands.

The manuscripts are especially important. Tewodros had himself gathered books and sacred materials from churches, particularly around Gondar, because he intended Maqdala to serve as an imperial centre of learning. Their removal in 1868 therefore displaced objects that had already undergone an earlier internal transfer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1868" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishmuseum.org.</span></span></span>

The status of the sacred altar tablets is clearer than that of many other objects. The British Museum acknowledges holding eleven from Maqdala and recognises that they represent the Ark within Ethiopian Orthodox worship. Their continued retention has made them central to restitution demands because they are consecrated religious objects, not simply decorative works.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/about-us/british-museum-story/contested-objects-collection/maqdala-collection" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British MuseumMaqdala collectionThe collection includes important liturgical objects such as processional crosses, chalices and umbrella...</span></span></span>

The “lost treasure” element should not be exaggerated into a rumour of hidden gold. The real challenge is painstaking provenance research: matching expedition inventories, auction records, inscriptions, church histories and modern catalogues. Each identification can reconnect an object with the community, institution or sacred setting from which it was removed.

## Why did Lij Iyasu disappear from history?

Lij Iyasu, the designated successor of Emperor Menelik II, became Ethiopia’s effective ruler while still young but was never formally crowned emperor. In 1916, nobles and senior church figures deposed him, accusing him of abandoning the Christian monarchy and favouring Islam. His supporters disputed both the charges and the legitimacy of his removal.

Iyasu resisted capture for several years before being imprisoned. He escaped briefly in 1931 but was recaptured and confined more securely. When Italy invaded Ethiopia in 1935, Italian propaganda invoked him as a possible alternative ruler, making his continued existence a political danger to Emperor Haile Selassie. Soon afterwards, Iyasu’s death was announced.

The date, cause and circumstances of his death were not transparently documented, and his burial place was never publicly established. Rumours claimed that he was killed on imperial orders to prevent the Italians from using him. Other accounts say that he died naturally after years of imprisonment. The surviving evidence does not securely prove either version.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lij_Iyasu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lij Iyasu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lij Iyasu</span></span></span>

Several proposed burial locations have circulated, including secret interment in or near religious institutions. None has been confirmed through a publicly documented identification of remains. This absence matters because Iyasu’s posthumous reputation remained politically charged. A known grave or official record might have encouraged commemoration, dynastic claims or renewed debate over the 1916 deposition.

His disappearance from official history was almost as important as his physical fate. The victorious political order portrayed him as an apostate and traitor, while later admirers recast him as a reformer committed to religious inclusion. Both images can oversimplify a young and politically vulnerable ruler operating during the First World War.

Iyasu’s case is among Ethiopia’s strongest unresolved personal mysteries because the uncertainty concerns a real prisoner whose death could have been recorded. The silence was not created by the remoteness of antiquity but by twentieth-century political secrecy.

## Was Haile Selassie murdered?

Emperor Haile Selassie was overthrown by the Derg military regime in September 1974 and held at the imperial palace. The government announced his death on 27 August 1975, attributing it to complications following illness or medical treatment. Because the deposed emperor died in custody and no transparent public investigation followed, suspicions of murder arose immediately.

Accounts later associated with members of the regime alleged that he had been strangled or smothered on orders from senior officials. These claims fit the Derg’s wider record of political killing, but the precise chain of command and method remain disputed. There was no independent post-mortem examination available to settle the matter.

In 1992, after the fall of the Derg, remains identified as Haile Selassie’s were discovered beneath part of the palace complex. They were transferred to a church and, after prolonged disagreement over the form and political meaning of the ceremony, reburied at Holy Trinity Cathedral in Addis Ababa in November 2000.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/27141174" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

The discovery confirmed that the regime had concealed his burial, strengthening doubts about its original account. It did not, by itself, establish the cause of death. Statements that he was certainly strangled go beyond what the publicly available forensic evidence can demonstrate. Conversely, accepting the Derg’s natural-death explanation at face value would ignore the secret burial and the political circumstances of his detention.

Haile Selassie’s death remains debated because it sits at the intersection of evidence and symbolism. To supporters, it was the clandestine murder of Ethiopia’s last emperor. To the revolutionaries who overthrew him, his removal represented the end of an unequal imperial system. For Rastafarians abroad, some of whom regarded him as divine, even his reported death could be interpreted through religious belief rather than conventional biography.

The most responsible verdict is that death in custody is established, official concealment is established, and homicide is a serious and plausible explanation. The exact manner of death and individual responsibility have not been proved to the standard that an independent contemporary investigation might have achieved.

## What the mysteries reveal about Ethiopian history

These cases survive for different reasons. The Ark cannot be inspected because religious authority forbids it. The Queen of Sheba stands at the meeting point of ancient commerce and later royal ideology. Lalibela and Aksum are difficult to reconstruct because monuments evolved, collapsed and left few explanatory texts. Iyasu and Haile Selassie became mysteries because states controlled information about defeated rulers. Maqdala’s objects became an archival puzzle because war scattered them across institutions and private collections.

They also show why Ethiopian history is easily distorted by outsiders. The country’s antiquity and distinctive Christian traditions have repeatedly attracted claims about secret biblical relics, vanished civilisations and impossible engineering. Such narratives often overlook the more interesting reality: Ethiopian societies possessed their own skilled builders, manuscript workshops, trading networks, political institutions and historical traditions. No lost super-civilisation is required to explain Aksum’s monuments or Lalibela’s churches.

At the same time, dismissing every tradition as fantasy would be equally misleading. The unexpectedly early dates of the Garima Gospels demonstrate that monastic memory can preserve a genuinely ancient core even when miraculous details cannot be tested. Archaeology has also confirmed the scale and international reach of the Aksumite kingdom, making older depictions of Ethiopia as isolated increasingly untenable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/ethiopia-and-the-world-3301500-ce/9050009D75ED529C859364EE3308EF0B" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

The best Ethiopian mysteries therefore reward a middle position: respect traditions without treating them automatically as literal records; use archaeology and science without assuming they can answer theological questions; and recognise that missing evidence may reflect warfare, censorship, restricted access or the dispersal of archives. Their value lies not simply in whether they can be “solved”, but in what the search reveals about power, faith and the survival of memory across one of Africa’s longest recorded histories.
