## Introduction

[Zimbabwe]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-zimbabwes/' | relative_url }})'s past was not only shaped by ancient kingdoms and changing societies. It was also reshaped by colonial rule, which influenced which discoveries were celebrated, which stories were dismissed, and even which archaeological evidence survived. Few examples illustrate this more clearly than [Great Zimbabwe]({{ 'great-zimbabwe/' | relative_url }}), where European settlers promoted the false idea that Africans could not have built the country's most famous stone city. Those claims were reinforced through museums, official publications and destructive excavations that damaged evidence still needed by archaeologists today. Although modern research has firmly established the African origins of Great Zimbabwe, the effects of colonial interpretations continue to influence public memory, museum collections and debates over the return of cultural heritage.

## Who Controlled the Story of Zimbabwe's Past?

Colonial governments understood that history could be politically useful. If African societies could be portrayed as lacking sophisticated states or monumental architecture before European conquest, colonial rule appeared easier to justify. Archaeology therefore became more than a scientific discipline: it became part of a wider struggle over legitimacy and identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/15127" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk">[St Andrews Research Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Andrews Research RepositoryThe historical development of Zimbabwe&#x27;s museums and monumentsJanuary 1, 1996...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1996</span></span></span>

In Southern Rhodesia, later Zimbabwe, official institutions often promoted interpretations that placed the country's greatest monuments outside African history. Early European visitors suggested that Great Zimbabwe had been built by Phoenicians, ancient Arabs, biblical peoples or other foreign civilisations. These ideas were presented despite the absence of convincing archaeological evidence and reflected contemporary racial assumptions rather than careful investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1429/the-impact-of-prejudice-on-the-history-of-great-zi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldhistory.org">[worldhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World History EncyclopediaThe Impact of Prejudice on the History of Great Zimbabwe - World History EncyclopediaAugust 19, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2019</span></span></span>

The result was a lasting historical distortion. Instead of asking how African societies created such an impressive centre, colonial writers spent decades asking which outsiders supposedly built it. This diverted attention away from genuine historical questions about politics, trade, religion and urban development.

## Foreign-Builder Myths at Great Zimbabwe

The mystery surrounding Great Zimbabwe was, in many respects, manufactured rather than discovered.

Nineteenth-century explorer Karl Mauch associated the ruins with the biblical Queen of Sheba, while later investigators such as Theodore Bent argued that the architecture must have been the work of Mediterranean or Middle Eastern peoples. These theories relied largely on assumptions that monumental stone architecture was beyond the abilities of local African communities. Imported trade goods from Asia and the Middle East—which actually demonstrated participation in international trade networks—were instead misused as supposed proof of foreign builders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1429/the-impact-of-prejudice-on-the-history-of-great-zi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldhistory.org">[World History Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World History EncyclopediaThe Impact of Prejudice on the History of Great Zimbabwe - World History EncyclopediaAugust 19, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2019</span></span></span>

Professional archaeological work gradually dismantled these claims. David Randall-MacIver, and especially Gertrude Caton-Thompson's carefully planned excavations in 1929, showed that the settlement belonged within a local African cultural sequence. Later radiocarbon dating confirmed a medieval chronology consistent with indigenous Shona-speaking societies rather than ancient biblical kingdoms. Even so, colonial authorities and many settler publications resisted these findings because they conflicted with political narratives supporting white rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/great-zimbabwe-2005-01/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scientificamerican.com">[scientificamerican.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scientificamerican.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scientific American Great Zimbabwe &#124; Scientific American</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientific American Great Zimbabwe &#124; Scientific American</span></span></span>

The episode remains one of archaeology's clearest examples of how political ideology can shape historical interpretation long after contrary evidence has emerged.

## Looting, Excavation and Destroyed Evidence

Colonial influence extended beyond interpretation. It also affected the physical survival of archaeological evidence.

During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Great Zimbabwe attracted treasure hunters, collectors and poorly trained investigators seeking gold or spectacular discoveries. Excavation methods often ignored the careful recording of soil layers, artefacts and building sequences that modern archaeology depends upon.

Richard Nicklin Hall's work became particularly controversial. His programme of clearing what he regarded as the "filth" of later African occupation removed large volumes of archaeological deposits. Valuable stratigraphic evidence—the layered record showing how the site developed over time—was permanently destroyed. Artefacts judged unimportant were discarded, while attention focused on objects thought capable of supporting theories of foreign origins. Later archaeologists repeatedly noted that this damage made reconstructing the site's history far more difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1429/the-impact-of-prejudice-on-the-history-of-great-zi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldhistory.org">[worldhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World History EncyclopediaThe Impact of Prejudice on the History of Great Zimbabwe - World History EncyclopediaAugust 19, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2019</span></span></span>

The consequences extended beyond Great Zimbabwe itself. Once archaeological layers have been removed without proper recording, the information they contain cannot be recreated. Some of the remaining mysteries surrounding the site's growth, chronology and social organisation persist partly because evidence was lost during these early excavations rather than because it never existed.

## How Official Myths Shaped Public Memory

Colonial narratives were reinforced through museums, guidebooks and education.

Museums in colonial Rhodesia frequently reflected government priorities, presenting interpretations that elevated European civilisation while minimising African achievement. Great Zimbabwe and the Matobo Hills became politically sensitive heritage sites where interpretations were expected to support the broader colonial worldview. By the mid-twentieth century, museum displays increasingly aligned with settler politics as African nationalist movements challenged colonial authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/15127" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk">[St Andrews Research Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Andrews Research RepositoryThe historical development of Zimbabwe&#x27;s museums and monumentsJanuary 1, 1996...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1996</span></span></span>

Even after professional archaeologists had rejected the foreign-builder theories, simplified versions continued to appear in tourist literature and popular culture. False ideas often survive because they are dramatic, familiar and politically useful. The persistence of these myths demonstrates that correcting historical evidence can take far longer than discovering it.

## Repatriation and the Recovery of Memory

Zimbabwe's independence in 1980 marked an important shift in reclaiming its past.

The country deliberately adopted the name "Zimbabwe" from the ancient stone city, making Great Zimbabwe a national symbol rather than a colonial curiosity. The famous carved Zimbabwe Birds, long removed from their original setting during the colonial period, became central national emblems and feature on the country's flag, coat of arms and currency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1429/the-impact-of-prejudice-on-the-history-of-great-zi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldhistory.org">[World History Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World History EncyclopediaThe Impact of Prejudice on the History of Great Zimbabwe - World History EncyclopediaAugust 19, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2019</span></span></span>

Recovery has involved more than national symbols. Zimbabwean archaeologists, historians and heritage professionals have increasingly incorporated local knowledge, oral traditions and community participation into research. Earlier archaeological practice often excluded neighbouring communities from interpreting sites that formed part of their own cultural landscape. Recent approaches seek to restore those voices alongside scientific investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/blog/reclaiming-great-zimbabwes-past-to-learn-lessons-for-the-future/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebritishacademy.ac.uk">[The British Academy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebritishacademy.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thebritishacademy.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Questions of repatriation also remain important. Some artefacts removed during the colonial period have been returned, while others remain in overseas museums and collections. These debates concern not only ownership but also who has the authority to tell Zimbabwe's history.

## Why This Matters for Zimbabwe's Historical Mysteries

Colonial rule did not simply create mistaken theories—it altered the evidence available for future generations.

Many unanswered questions about Great Zimbabwe concern its political organisation, religious practices and changing settlement patterns. Some uncertainty is natural because medieval societies rarely left written records. Yet other gaps exist because archaeological deposits were disturbed, artefacts dispersed and African interpretations deliberately marginalised during the colonial era.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1429/the-impact-of-prejudice-on-the-history-of-great-zi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldhistory.org">[worldhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World History EncyclopediaThe Impact of Prejudice on the History of Great Zimbabwe - World History EncyclopediaAugust 19, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2019</span></span></span>

Understanding this distinction is essential. The enduring mystery is not whether Africans built Great Zimbabwe—that question has been resolved by decades of archaeological research. The more revealing mystery is how a false narrative gained such influence, survived despite contradictory evidence and permanently reshaped the historical record. Zimbabwe's experience shows that history can be distorted not only by what is forgotten, but also by who controls the story.
