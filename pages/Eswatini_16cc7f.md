## Lion Cavern and the people who mined colour

The most important archaeological enigma in Eswatini lies at Ngwenya, near the country’s north-western border. Within the ancient iron-rich landscape is Lion Cavern, where Stone Age people deliberately extracted ochre tens of thousands of years ago. Ochre is a mineral pigment, commonly red, yellow or brown, which could be ground into powder and used for colouring objects, bodies or other materials.

Ngwenya has long been promoted as the world’s oldest mine. Early excavations and radiocarbon tests produced dates of roughly 43,000 years, but the simple slogan concealed several uncertainties. Charcoal dates an episode of burning rather than the act of mining itself, and later industrial extraction disturbed parts of the site. It was therefore reasonable to ask whether Lion Cavern represented sustained organised mining, occasional collection from a natural exposure, or several unrelated episodes separated by long intervals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5421/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreNgwenya MinesThis mine is known to be one of the oldest mine in the world. In 1964 charcoal nodules from the...</span></span></span>

Research published in *Nature Communications* in 2024 considerably strengthened the mining interpretation. Scientists compared 173 ochre samples from 15 Stone Age sites in and around modern Eswatini, examining their chemical composition to identify likely geological sources. The study found evidence that people repeatedly selected particular varieties of ochre, transported them across the landscape and maintained recognisable practices over many generations. New dating work placed intensive extraction at Lion Cavern at about 48,000 years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatiniby BL MacDonald · 2024 · Cited by 14 — Eswatini also hosts Lion Cavern, a high-e...</span></span></span>

That evidence resolves one question but opens others. Archaeology can show that people removed mineral material and carried it elsewhere; it cannot directly recover their language, identity or intended meaning. Some ochre may have served practical purposes, such as preparing hides or colouring compounds. Some may have been used for personal decoration, art or ceremonies. Chemical matching also suggests movement and exchange, but it does not reveal whether ochre was traded, gifted, collected during seasonal journeys or carried by migrating communities.

The modern mystery is therefore not whether very early mining occurred. The evidence for purposeful extraction is now strong. The unresolved questions concern the social world behind it:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was access to Lion Cavern controlled by particular families or communities?
* Did distinctive colours carry different meanings?
* How far did knowledge of the deposit travel?
* Were mining techniques taught continuously, or was the cavern repeatedly rediscovered?
* What relationship, if any, exists between Stone Age practices and much later cultural uses of ochre?

</div>

Claims that the mine belonged straightforwardly to one modern ethnic group go beyond what the evidence can establish. The site is tens of thousands of years older than present national borders and recorded political identities. Lion Cavern belongs securely to Eswatini’s archaeological heritage, but the precise identity of its first miners remains unknown.

## The fire that erased part of Ngwenya’s record

The uncertainty surrounding Lion Cavern became more serious in September 2018, when the Ngwenya visitor centre burned down. The centre had displayed ancient mining tools and other material associated with the site. Reports agree that the building was destroyed and that it had still not been rebuilt several years later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://landportal.org/news/2021/06/reopening-eswatini%E2%80%99s-controversial-ngwenya-mine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landportal.org">[landportal.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landportal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land PortalReopening eSwatini&#x27;s controversial Ngwenya Mine - Land Portal26 Oct 2020 — Despite this, a visitor centre that caught fire and...</span></span></span>

Publicly accessible accounts are less consistent about exactly what was lost. Some state broadly that the artefacts in the centre were destroyed; others mention tools without providing a complete catalogue, photographs, accession numbers or a detailed conservation assessment. That distinction matters. An object destroyed by fire may still have been previously measured, photographed, drawn or sampled. Conversely, an object described merely as “lost” might have survived elsewhere if it had been moved before the blaze.

The destruction therefore created an archival puzzle as well as a heritage tragedy. A definitive account would require a pre-fire inventory, insurance or incident records, excavation catalogues and confirmation of which objects were originals rather than replicas. Without that documentation in an easily accessible public form, dramatic online claims about the fire should be treated cautiously.

There is no sound evidence that the blaze was deliberately set to erase archaeological discoveries. The stronger conclusion is less sensational but still troubling: inadequate public documentation has made it difficult to determine the precise scale of the loss. At a site important to the study of early human technology, uncertainty about the surviving collection reduces the ability of future researchers to check older interpretations.

Ngwenya has also been caught between conservation and modern mineral exploitation. It was placed on Eswatini’s tentative list for UNESCO World Heritage status in 2008, while proposals to reopen commercial mining generated concerns about pollution, working conditions and damage to the wider cultural landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://landportal.org/news/2021/06/reopening-eswatini%E2%80%99s-controversial-ngwenya-mine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landportal.org">[Land Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landportal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land PortalReopening eSwatini&#x27;s controversial Ngwenya Mine - Land Portal26 Oct 2020 — Despite this, a visitor centre that caught fire and...</span></span></span> The result is an unusual double mystery: archaeologists are still reconstructing what happened there 48,000 years ago, while historians must also reconstruct what became of material recovered during the twentieth century.

## Why poison shadows Eswatini’s royal history

Stories of poisoning recur in accounts of Eswatini’s royal succession. They should not all be treated as equally reliable. In a political system where succession could redistribute authority among royal households, an unexpected death naturally encouraged suspicion. Yet restricted access to royal affairs, limited medical records and later retelling make it difficult to separate documented investigation from political accusation.

### The death of Crown Prince Ludvonga

After King Mswati II died in 1868, Ludvonga was selected as the future ruler. He died before taking effective power, probably in 1872. Historian D. Hugh Gillis described his death as occurring in “mysterious circumstances”, while Philip Bonner’s study of nineteenth-century Swazi politics emphasises that it left the kingdom exposed to internal division and external pressure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sahistory.org.za/sites/default/files/archive-files3/philip_bonner_kings_commoners_and_concessionairbook4you.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sahistory.org.za">[South African History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sahistory.org.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sahistory.org.za.</span></span></span>

Later summaries frequently identify poisoning as the cause. The difficulty is that accessible evidence does not provide a contemporary medical examination, a named perpetrator or a securely documented judicial process. It is therefore safest to describe poisoning as a historical suspicion rather than an established fact.

Ludvonga’s death mattered because it forced another succession decision. His mother adopted Mbandzeni, another son of Mswati II, into her household so that he could become king. The episode illustrates why rumours surrounding royal deaths could endure: an unexplained death did not merely remove an individual but altered the balance between families, councillors and regional factions.

### King Bhunu’s sudden death

King Bhunu died in December 1899 at about 23 years of age, reportedly during the period of the annual royal ceremony. His death was initially withheld from the wider population until the ceremony had concluded. Later accounts include speculation that he was poisoned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhunu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Secrecy surrounding the announcement was not itself proof of murder. Royal ritual and political stability could provide reasons for delaying news of a king’s death, particularly at a moment when the region was entering the South African War. Bhunu left an infant son, the future Sobhuza II, and a long regency followed.

The circumstances nevertheless invited suspicion. He was young; his death was sudden; information was controlled; and the succession had enormous political consequences. No publicly available evidence identifies a toxin or establishes responsibility. Bhunu’s death remains mysterious in the strict historical sense, but the mystery is the absence of a demonstrable cause, not evidence of a proven assassination.

### The accusations after Sobhuza II died

The poisoning controversy after the death of King Sobhuza II in August 1982 is better documented because contradictory claims appeared in contemporary news reports. In October, United Press International reported that two royal sons had been arrested amid allegations that they had poisoned their father with a preparation supplied through a traditional practitioner. The following day, Prime Minister Mabandla Dlamini publicly called the story a fabrication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.upi.com/Archives/1982/10/17/Royal-family-suspects-two-sons-poisoned-King-Sobhuza/7190403675200/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upi.com">[Upi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Royal family suspects two sons poisoned King Sobhuza</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Royal family suspects two sons poisoned King Sobhuza</span></span></span>

Those opposing statements establish that a serious political allegation existed. They do not establish that Sobhuza was poisoned. No publicly cited toxicology findings or transparent trial proved the accusation. The reports emerged during a tense succession, when the identity of the future king had not been publicly settled and rival groupings were competing for influence around the regency.

The years following Sobhuza’s death were marked by the removal of Queen Regent Dzeliwe, the rise of Queen Regent Ntfombi and struggles within the royal advisory council. Further poison and coup allegations accompanied the political conflict. A 1987 account in *Time* described accusations involving princes, officials and an alleged attempt to poison a royal figure abroad, alongside detentions without trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/archive/6710234/swaziland-in-the-kingdom-of-fire-eyes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">swaziland in the kingdom of fire eyes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">swaziland in the kingdom of fire eyes</span></span></span>

This atmosphere explains why poison became part of public memory. It provided a language for discussing concealed political struggle in a system where decisions were made within restricted royal institutions. The best evidence supports neither a sweeping conspiracy nor the dismissal of every allegation. It supports a narrower judgement: accusations were real and politically consequential, but the death of Sobhuza II has not been publicly demonstrated to have resulted from poisoning.

## The incomplete record of apartheid’s secret war

During the later decades of apartheid, Eswatini became both a refuge and a dangerous transit zone for members of South African liberation movements. Its geographical position made it vulnerable. South African security units could cross or manipulate the border, while the government of what was then Swaziland faced intense pressure from its much larger neighbour.

Assassinations, abductions, ambushes and parcel bombs took place on Eswatini’s territory. South African History Online records, for example, that African National Congress figures Cassius Make and Paul Dikeledi were killed in a taxi ambush in July 1987, together with a Mozambican national.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sahistory.org.za/article/umkhonto-wesizwe-mk-timeline" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sahistory.org.za">[South African History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sahistory.org.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">umkhonto wesizwe mk timeline</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">umkhonto wesizwe mk timeline</span></span></span> Other operations involved covert police units, informers and agents whose roles were exposed only years later.

South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission confirmed that apartheid operatives carried out killings and abductions in neighbouring countries, including Swaziland. Amnesty proceedings disclosed specific operations and granted amnesty to some participants who made the required disclosures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/media/pr/2000/pr000602a.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on justice.gov.za.</span></span></span>

Yet the process did not provide a complete accounting. An ANC submission listed 52 members of its armed wing believed to have been killed by apartheid security forces in Swaziland, while amnesty applications reportedly covered only 14 targeted killings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sahistory.org.za/article/umkhonto-wesizwe-mk-exile" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sahistory.org.za">[South African History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sahistory.org.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">South African History Onlineu Mkhonto we Sizwe (MK) in exile</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">South African History Onlineu Mkhonto we Sizwe (MK) in exile</span></span></span> The difference does not prove that all remaining deaths were committed by the same units. It does show the scale of the unresolved record.

Several factors keep these cases open:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Operatives applied for amnesty only when they believed disclosure served their interests.
* Some commanders or participants were dead by the time hearings began.
* Covert units used false identities, intermediaries and informers.
* Local witnesses may have feared both South African agents and political repercussions inside Swaziland.
* Operational records could be destroyed, concealed or written to obscure responsibility.
* Rival liberation groups and internal suspicions sometimes complicated later reconstruction.

</div>

One particularly revealing case involved plans to conceal explosives inside a manuscript intended for delivery in Swaziland. Truth and Reconciliation Commission records describe an ANC courier being injured when the package exploded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/media/pr/2000/pr000721a.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on justice.gov.za.</span></span></span> Such methods were designed to blur the chain between planner, manufacturer, courier and victim.

The enduring mystery is therefore not whether apartheid South Africa violated Eswatini’s sovereignty; official proceedings and former operatives confirmed that it did. The unanswered questions are who authorised every operation, which deaths belong to which covert programme, how much local assistance was provided and how many victims remain absent from the recognised record.

## What the evidence supports

Eswatini’s unresolved history is marked by three different kinds of uncertainty.

At Lion Cavern, the uncertainty comes from extreme age. Scientific analysis can reconstruct sources, dates and movement patterns, but the miners left no written account of their intentions. The latest research makes the site more historically important while showing how much remains unknowable.

In royal history, uncertainty comes from restricted information and political rivalry. Ludvonga and Bhunu died young in circumstances that later generated poisoning stories. Sobhuza II’s death produced contemporary allegations and official denials. In none of these cases does the available public evidence justify presenting murder as proven.

In the apartheid-era cases, uncertainty was deliberately manufactured. Covert units concealed their actions, and later truth proceedings uncovered only the operations for which evidence or testimony survived. The resulting gaps concern real victims and documented state violence, not folklore.

These distinctions are essential. An unexplained death is not automatically an assassination; a destroyed collection is not evidence of a cover-up; and a traditional story is not worthless merely because it cannot be verified literally. Eswatini’s strongest historical mysteries endure precisely where archaeology, oral memory, political secrecy and incomplete archives overlap. Their value lies not in forcing a dramatic solution, but in showing how historical knowledge is created—and how easily part of it can disappear.
