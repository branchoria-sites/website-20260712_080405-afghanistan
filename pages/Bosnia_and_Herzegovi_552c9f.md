## Introduction

Some puzzles can be narrowed by documents, archaeology or DNA. Others survive because evidence was destroyed, witnesses died, political interests shaped the record or later legends replaced uncertainty with certainty. The clearest approach is therefore to separate genuine unanswered questions from claims that have already been tested and rejected. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, that distinction matters because mysteries about the past frequently become arguments about identity, responsibility and ownership in the present.

## The Sarajevo Haggadah’s missing journey

The [Sarajevo Haggadah]({{ 'haggadah/' | relative_url }}) is a richly illustrated Jewish book used for the Passover meal. It was produced on parchment in northern Spain during the second half of the fourteenth century, but much of its route from medieval Iberia to nineteenth-century Sarajevo remains unknown. Marginal notes and other physical evidence indicate that it passed through Italy after the expulsion of Jews from Spain, yet no continuous chain of owners or locations has been recovered. The National Museum of Bosnia and Herzegovina acquired it in 1894 from the Sarajevo family of Joseph Kohen. What can be said securely is that it originated in the Sephardic Jewish world of medieval Spain, later appeared in Italy and ultimately entered Sarajevo’s museum collection. ## Who stood behind the Sarajevo assassination?

The basic events of 28 June 1914 are firmly established. During an official visit to Sarajevo, the Austro-Hungarian heir Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie were shot and killed by Gavrilo Princip, a young Bosnian Serb associated with the revolutionary movement Young Bosnia. Earlier that day another conspirator had thrown a bomb at the royal procession. The murders became the immediate trigger for the July Crisis, although the wider European war that followed was a political choice rather than an automatic consequence of the shots.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/sarajevo-incident-1-1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net">[1914-1918-Online (WW1) Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on 1914-1918-online.net.</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery concerns responsibility beyond the young assassins. Weapons, training and assistance came through clandestine networks connected with Serbian military intelligence. Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, was both a senior intelligence officer and a leading figure in the secretive Black Hand organisation. This establishes involvement by Serbian state officials, but not necessarily approval by the Serbian government as a whole.

Many historians distinguish between rogue or semi-independent military conspirators and the civilian government of Prime Minister Nikola Pašić. The available evidence suggests that the plot was organised largely without formal government authorisation. Pašić may have received fragmentary warnings and apparently attempted to tighten border controls, but surviving records do not prove that he knew the complete plan, could identify the assassins or deliberately allowed the attack to proceed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/civilian-and-military-power-south-east-europe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net">[1914-1918-Online (WW1) Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on 1914-1918-online.net.</span></span></span>

The uncertainty was deepened by later events. Apis was executed after the politically manipulated Salonika Trial in 1917, while records and testimony became entangled in struggles between Serbia’s military and civilian leadership. The trial was later overturned, but that did not restore a complete documentary trail. Scholars still disagree over how much Apis personally directed, what Pašić knew and when he knew it. The claim that “Serbia” ordered the assassination therefore compresses several competing institutions into one actor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/black-hand/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net">[1914-1918-Online (WW1) Encyclopedia-1918-Online (WW1) Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">black hand</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">black hand</span></span></span>

A second layer of myth concerns chance. Popular retellings often claim that Princip encountered the archduke only because the royal driver took a wrong turn and stopped directly beside him. The route was indeed confused after the earlier bombing, and the car slowed while attempting to correct its course. Yet portraying the encounter as an almost impossible accident overlooks the fact that several armed conspirators had been positioned along the route. The assassination succeeded through a mixture of preparation, poor security and contingency, not through one miraculous coincidence.

The mystery matters because interpretations of Princip remain politically charged. He has been remembered variously as an anti-imperial freedom fighter, a Yugoslav revolutionary and a terrorist. Those labels reflect later national memories as much as the surviving evidence. The unresolved question is not whether Princip fired the shots, but how far the conspiracy extended and how responsibility should be divided between individuals, underground organisations and state institutions.

## What do the medieval tombstones mean?

Across Bosnia and Herzegovina stand thousands of large medieval stone tombstones, many carved with crosses, stars, crescents, human figures, weapons, hunting scenes and circular dances. They date mainly from the twelfth to the sixteenth centuries and form one of the country’s most distinctive archaeological landscapes. UNESCO recognises selected burial grounds in Bosnia and Herzegovina and neighbouring states as a transnational World Heritage property.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1504/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

For generations, the stones were often explained as monuments belonging exclusively to the medieval Bosnian Church, which its opponents accused of heresy. That interpretation suited the idea of an isolated religious community with a unique visual code. The archaeological record is less tidy. UNESCO notes that the tombstones were used by members of all three principal medieval Christian communities in the region: Catholic, Orthodox and the Bosnian Church. They cannot reliably be assigned to one faith simply from their form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1504/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Their imagery is also resistant to a single decoding system. A carved rider may represent the deceased’s social rank, an idealised aristocratic identity or a scene with religious meaning. A raised hand may be a greeting, a declaration of authority, a prayer or a symbolic boundary between the living and the dead. Circular dances can be read as scenes of communal life, ritual or an imagined afterlife. Short inscriptions sometimes name the dead or warn visitors not to disturb the grave, but most stones offer no explanatory text.

More speculative theories identify astronomical maps, secret heretical teachings or even records of celestial events in the carvings. One study has proposed that certain motifs might preserve a memory of the supernova observed in 1054, but this remains a hypothesis rather than an accepted decipherment. Similar stars, circles and crescents occur in many medieval artistic traditions and do not automatically represent a specific astronomical observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The most plausible conclusion is that the symbols drew on several overlapping traditions: Christianity, local status display, funerary custom, workshop conventions and wider Mediterranean imagery. Their mystery is genuine because medieval Bosnians left too little written explanation, not because the stones necessarily encode one lost doctrine. Modern attempts to claim them for a single nation or confession can obscure their strongest historical message: they emerged from a shared and culturally mixed medieval landscape.

## The “Bosnian pyramids” and a manufactured mystery

In 2005, businessman Semir Osmanagić announced that hills near Visoko were enormous human-made pyramids built by an unknown ancient civilisation. The most prominent, Visočica hill, was promoted as the “Pyramid of the Sun”, accompanied by claims about precise orientation, artificial concrete, underground tunnels and an age exceeding that of Egypt’s pyramids.

These assertions created an international media story and a successful tourism attraction. They also offered an emotionally powerful narrative: Bosnia and Herzegovina, recently associated abroad with war, could instead possess the world’s oldest monumental civilisation. The claim’s appeal therefore cannot be understood only as an argument about geology. It also concerns economic revival, national pride and dissatisfaction with established academic authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/48740784" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

The scientific assessment, however, is not evenly balanced. Geologists and archaeologists have identified the hills as natural formations shaped by sedimentary layers, tectonic processes and erosion. Flat, fractured rock surfaces presented as paving or concrete are consistent with naturally bedded conglomerate and sandstone. No secure archaeological context has produced the infrastructure expected from a civilisation capable of constructing gigantic pyramids: there are no convincing building layers, tools, settlements, inscriptions or datable construction deposits.

Claims about tunnels do not change that assessment. Some underground passages in the wider district may be old mines, drainage works or modern excavations, but their existence does not demonstrate a prehistoric pyramid complex. A tunnel near a hill is not evidence that the hill itself was built. Archaeology depends on controlled excavation, recorded layers and associations between objects and structures, rather than isolated measurements or visually suggestive shapes.

Professional archaeologists have also warned that excavation and reshaping may damage authentic heritage around Visoko, including remains associated with medieval Bosnia. The controversy is therefore not simply harmless folklore. A dramatic but unsupported ancient past can divert attention and money from genuine sites whose evidence is less spectacular but historically secure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_pyramid_claims" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bosnian pyramid claims</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bosnian pyramid claims</span></span></span>

The fair conclusion is that the hills remain culturally significant, but not as unexplained pyramids. They are a case study in how a modern mystery is manufactured: begin with a landscape that resembles a familiar monument, interpret natural features as construction, present academic rejection as proof of suppression, and build a self-sustaining tourism economy around continued uncertainty. The social phenomenon is real. The prehistoric pyramid claim is not supported by credible archaeological evidence.

## The people still missing from the war

The gravest unresolved mystery in Bosnia and Herzegovina is not ancient. When the 1992–95 war ended, more than 31,000 people were estimated to be missing. By June 2025, approximately 23,500 had been accounted for, leaving about 7,500 still missing. Bosnia and Herzegovina has achieved one of the world’s highest rates of post-conflict identification, but every remaining case represents a family without a confirmed death, burial place or complete account of what happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/bosnia-and-herzegovina/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">bosnia and herzegovina</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">bosnia and herzegovina</span></span></span>

The missing are not unexplained in the supernatural sense. Many were civilians or prisoners killed during ethnic cleansing, detention, forced removal and mass execution. What remains unknown is the precise fate and location of particular individuals. In some cases, perpetrators concealed bodies in remote graves. Around Srebrenica, remains were moved from primary burial sites into secondary and sometimes tertiary graves in an effort to hide evidence. Heavy machinery broke and scattered bodies, leaving parts of one person in several locations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2016/dec/06/bringing-up-the-bodies-bosnia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Bringing up the bodies in Bosnia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">First-person testimonies from survivors like Zijad and Fikret Bačić convey the profound personal toll of the atrocities, including mass e...</span></span></span>

This concealment transformed identification into an immense forensic problem. Early investigations relied on clothing, personal possessions, dental records and physical characteristics. From 2001, large-scale DNA matching compared samples from bones with voluntary reference samples supplied by relatives. The method produced thousands of identifications and allowed separated remains to be reassociated with the same individual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/stocktaking-2025.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icmp.int.</span></span></span>

Yet science cannot locate a grave without information. Investigators still depend on witnesses, wartime records, aerial imagery and people willing to reveal burial sites. Political obstruction, denial, fear of prosecution and the death of witnesses all reduce the chances of new discoveries. Urban development and changes to the landscape can further erase traces. Even where remains have been recovered, degraded DNA or the absence of living close relatives may prevent a confirmed identification.

The search also raises painful choices. A family may receive only a few identified bones and must decide whether to hold a burial or wait in the hope that more remains will be found. Later discoveries can require a grave to be reopened. The technical success of DNA identification therefore does not produce a simple emotional ending.

Progress continues. ICMP reported that its work with Bosnian authorities between 2020 and 2025 contributed to hundreds of new identifications and the reassociation of previously identified remains. But the declining number of new graves and usable leads means that the last cases may be the hardest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/news/icmps-support-for-bosnia-and-herzegovina-in-locating-missing-persons-from-the-conflicts-of-the-1990s-key-results-over-the-last-five-years/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s Support for Bosnia and Herzegovina in Locating</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s Support for Bosnia and Herzegovina in Locating</span></span></span>

These disappearances should not be treated as a conventional mystery entertainment. They are unfinished human-rights cases and, in many instances, evidence of war crimes. Establishing the truth serves several purposes at once: returning names to the dead, protecting families’ legal rights, supporting criminal accountability and resisting attempts to deny documented atrocities.

## Why Bosnia’s mysteries remain contested

The country’s historical enigmas persist for different reasons. The Sarajevo Haggadah has missing centuries because ordinary ownership records did not survive migration and persecution. The Sarajevo assassination remains disputed because clandestine networks overlapped with state institutions and later political trials distorted the record. Medieval tombstones are difficult to interpret because images survived without explanatory texts. War victims remain missing because evidence was deliberately concealed.

These cases should not be placed on the same evidential level. The route of a manuscript is genuinely incomplete. The degree of Serbian governmental foreknowledge in 1914 is historically debatable. The meanings of many tombstone carvings are open to interpretation. The claim that Visoko contains prehistoric pyramids has been tested and rejected by mainstream archaeology and geology. The fate of thousands of wartime missing persons is unresolved individually, although the broader system of killing and concealment is extensively documented.

What links them is the way uncertainty enters public memory. Gaps can invite careful investigation, but they can also be filled by nationalist certainty, conspiracy claims or comforting legends. Bosnia and Herzegovina’s past is most revealing when those gaps are left honestly defined: what is known, what can reasonably be inferred, what remains disputed and what the evidence does not support.
