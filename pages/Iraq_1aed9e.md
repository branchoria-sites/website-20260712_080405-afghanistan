## Were the Hanging Gardens really in Babylon?

The [Hanging Gardens]({{ 'hanging-gardens/' | relative_url }}) of Babylon are traditionally counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. Classical writers described an extraordinary terraced garden sustained by an ingenious water-lifting system, usually associating it with Babylon and, in later tradition, King Nebuchadnezzar II. Yet archaeologists have found no structure at Babylon that can be securely identified as the gardens, and surviving Babylonian royal inscriptions do not clearly describe such a monument.

This absence does not prove that the gardens never existed. Much of ancient Babylon has been damaged, rebuilt or remains difficult to excavate. Classical accounts were also written centuries after the supposed construction, often by authors who may never have visited Mesopotamia. The problem is therefore not simply that the gardens are “missing”, but that the surviving descriptions are late, inconsistent and difficult to match with the physical remains.

A prominent alternative proposed by Assyriologist Stephanie Dalley places the gardens at Nineveh, near modern Mosul, rather than Babylon. The Assyrian king Sennacherib described elaborate palace gardens and major hydraulic works there. Remains of canals and aqueducts demonstrate that his engineers transported water over considerable distances, while his inscriptions celebrate technological innovations used in his royal landscape. These details resemble important parts of the later Greek descriptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanging_Gardens_of_Babylon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hanging Gardens of Babylon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hanging Gardens of Babylon</span></span></span>

The Nineveh theory is plausible but not universally accepted. It requires explaining how later writers transferred the Wonder from one famous Mesopotamian capital to another. Ancient place names and royal traditions could become confused, especially when repeated by distant authors, but no surviving inscription simply declares that Sennacherib built the Wonder later known as the Hanging Gardens.

The safest conclusion is that spectacular royal gardens certainly existed in ancient Iraq, including Sennacherib’s at Nineveh. Whether those gardens were the original source of the famous Wonder remains debated. The popular image of a single, securely identified monument at Babylon goes further than the surviving evidence allows.

## What happened in the death pits of Ur?

Excavations directed by Leonard Woolley at the Royal Cemetery of Ur in the 1920s and 1930s uncovered some of the most remarkable burials ever found in Mesopotamia. Several tomb complexes contained a principal burial accompanied by guards, musicians, attendants, animals and rich objects made from gold, silver, shell and lapis lazuli. In the so-called Great Death Pit, dozens of bodies had apparently been arranged in ordered rows.

Woolley believed that the attendants had willingly entered the tomb ceremonies, drunk poison from cups and died peacefully so that they could continue serving a royal master in the afterlife. The neat arrangement of the bodies and the survival of elaborate headdresses appeared to support a formal, carefully controlled ritual rather than a chaotic massacre.

Later research made this picture considerably darker. Examination of surviving skulls has identified injuries consistent with deliberate blows from a heavy, pointed object. Some bodies may also have been heated or treated after death, perhaps to delay decomposition during lengthy funeral ceremonies. This suggests that at least some attendants were killed directly and then prepared for display or burial, rather than calmly drinking poison together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Cemetery_at_Ur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Royal Cemetery at Ur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Royal Cemetery at Ur</span></span></span>

Important questions nevertheless remain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* Were all the attendants killed, or did methods vary between burials?
* Were they servants, soldiers, musicians, family members or ritual specialists?
* Did they participate voluntarily under the beliefs of their society, or were they coerced?
* Were the principal occupants truly kings and queens, or high-ranking priests playing divine roles?

</div>

The excavation methods of Woolley’s era limit what can now be established. Many skeletons were poorly preserved, and only selected remains were retained for later study. Modern techniques such as DNA analysis, isotope testing and detailed forensic imaging therefore cannot be applied to the entire burial population.

The mystery is no longer whether unusual deaths occurred. The physical evidence strongly supports organised killing in at least some tombs. What remains uncertain is the meaning of the ceremony: royal sacrifice, religious performance, political theatre or a combination of all three.

## Was the Baghdad Battery an ancient electrical device?

The object popularly known as the Baghdad Battery consists of a small ceramic vessel containing a copper cylinder and an iron rod. It was reportedly found near Baghdad and is generally associated with the Parthian or early Sasanian period, although the exact archaeological context was inadequately documented. In the 1930s, museum director Wilhelm König suggested that the arrangement might have functioned as a primitive electrical cell.

Modern replicas filled with acidic liquids can produce a small voltage. This has encouraged claims that people in ancient Iraq used electricity for electroplating, pain relief or religious effects. The object has consequently become a favourite example in books and programmes about supposedly lost ancient technology.

Producing electricity in a reconstruction, however, does not prove that electricity was the original purpose. No securely identified wires, connected devices, operating instructions or workshops have been found. Proposed examples of ancient electroplating can generally be explained by established metalworking methods. The vessel’s design is also inconvenient for repeated electrical use: the copper component is difficult to access as a terminal, while the arrangement provides no obvious way to connect several cells efficiently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sino-platonic.org/complete/spp377_baghdad_battery.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sino-platonic.org">[sino-platonic.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sino-platonic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Baghdad Battery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 3, 2026 — by A Bazes · Cited by 1 — Were the Baghdad Battery&#x27;s only source of voltage the reaction between iron and copper, then...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 3, 2026</span></span></span>

Other suggested functions include storage for sacred or written material, although these interpretations are also uncertain. Because the findspot and associated objects were not scientifically recorded to modern standards, its original use may never be recoverable.

The balanced judgement is that the vessel could generate a weak current if supplied with a suitable liquid, but there is no persuasive evidence that its makers understood or intended this effect. Calling it a “battery” quietly assumes the very point that remains unproved.

## Where did Iraq’s stolen antiquities go?

When Baghdad fell in April 2003, the Iraq Museum was looted. Early reports claimed that about 170,000 objects had disappeared, but subsequent investigations showed that this figure confused the museum’s wider registered holdings with the number actually stolen. The best-supported estimate is approximately 15,000 missing objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/04-05_Iraq_Museum.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isac.uchicago.edu">[uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isac.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">04 05 Iraq Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">04 05 Iraq Museum</span></span></span>

The theft was not a single, easily explained event. Different parts of the museum appear to have been entered by different groups. Some people stole or smashed objects opportunistically. Other thieves seem to have known the building, storage systems or value of particular collections. Especially damaging losses included thousands of cylinder seals: small engraved objects that are portable, valuable and difficult to trace once separated from their records.

By 2021, UNESCO reported that the Iraqi authorities had recovered about 4,300 of the roughly 15,000 museum objects. Other official and investigative estimates vary according to date, classification and whether recovered pieces have been formally verified. The Federal Bureau of Investigation has continued to list thousands of Iraqi artefacts as missing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-supports-iraq-national-museum-protect-and-preserve-its-collection" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">supports iraq national museum protect and preserve its collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">supports iraq national museum protect and preserve its collection</span></span></span>

The larger mystery extends beyond the museum. Archaeological sites across southern Iraq were heavily looted, often leaving landscapes pitted with illegal excavations. Objects taken directly from the ground are especially difficult to identify because they may never have been photographed, catalogued or even known to scholars. Once sold through layers of dealers and false ownership histories, their Iraqi origin can be obscured. The destruction of excavation context also removes information about date, use and association, even when an object is eventually recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isac.uchicago.edu/museum-exhibits/special-exhibits/oriental-institute-university-chicago-examine-looting-iraq-nation-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isac.uchicago.edu">[Ancient Cultures Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isac.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">oriental institute university chicago examine looting iraq nation 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">oriental institute university chicago examine looting iraq nation 0</span></span></span>

There is no evidence that all the missing objects entered one secret collection or followed a single organised route. Some were hidden locally, some were returned voluntarily, some were intercepted abroad and others probably remain in private hands. The true unresolved question is not simply where the artefacts are, but how many unrecorded objects were removed from archaeological sites and can now never be recognised with certainty.

## The treasure of Nimrud: a mystery that was partly solved

The royal tombs at Nimrud, excavated by Iraqi archaeologists in the late 1980s, contained hundreds of extraordinary pieces of gold jewellery, vessels, ornaments and precious stones associated with Assyrian queens. Political isolation and international sanctions meant that the discovery was initially far less widely known than its importance deserved.

After the Gulf War, the treasure disappeared from public view. When government buildings and museums were looted in 2003, many observers feared that it had been stolen or destroyed. The uncertainty became one of the most dramatic cultural mysteries of the invasion.

In June 2003, investigators opened flooded vaults beneath Iraq’s Central Bank and found the main treasure stored in waterlogged crates. Iraqi officials had secretly placed it there for protection years earlier. The survival of the vault through bombing, fire, flooding and the collapse of state security was extraordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2003/6/7/iraqi-treasures-found-safe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aljazeera.com">[Al Jazeera]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aljazeera.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Al Jazeera Iraqi treasures found safe &#124; News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Al Jazeera Iraqi treasures found safe &#124; News</span></span></span>

That discovery did not resolve every problem connected with Nimrud. Some less celebrated excavation material and documentation stored elsewhere was lost or dispersed. The tombs had already been disturbed in antiquity, complicating efforts to identify individual queens and reconstruct the original arrangement of bodies and jewellery. Later destruction at Nimrud erased further archaeological evidence, making the surviving excavation records even more important.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/misc-2016-Nimrud-Queens-Tombs-web.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isac.uchicago.edu">[Ancient Cultures Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isac.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">misc 2016 Nimrud Queens Tombs web</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">misc 2016 Nimrud Queens Tombs web</span></span></span>

The case is useful because it shows how quickly absence becomes a story of theft. The treasure really was missing from public knowledge, but it was not in an international criminal collection. It had been concealed by Iraqi officials, and secrecy intended to protect it also created the mystery surrounding its fate.

## Who owns the Iraqi Jewish Archive?

In May 2003, American personnel entered the flooded basement of Iraq’s intelligence headquarters in Baghdad and found more than 2,700 books and tens of thousands of documents connected with Iraq’s Jewish community. The collection included religious works, school records, communal papers, correspondence and personal material. Many items were waterlogged and moulding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ijarchive.org/s/iraqi-jewish-archive/page/home" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ijarchive.org">[ijarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ijarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ijarchive.org.</span></span></span>

With the agreement of Iraqi authorities, the material was transported to the United States for conservation. Specialists froze, dried, cleaned, repaired and digitised the documents, making much of the collection publicly accessible. The rescue is well documented; the deeper mystery concerns how the material entered the intelligence building and who should now possess it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/publications/prologue/2013/fall-winter/ija.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Preserving the &quot;Iraqi Jewish Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Preserving the &quot;Iraqi Jewish Archive</span></span></span>

Iraq maintains that the archive is part of its national documentary heritage and was removed under an agreement requiring its return. Critics argue that many books and papers were originally owned by Jewish individuals, schools and religious institutions whose community was driven into exile. Returning the physical collection to the Iraqi state, they contend, would place confiscated property in the hands of the successor to the institutions that seized it.

The archive therefore presents several competing claims:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **State custody:** The records were found in Iraq and document an Iraqi community, giving them national historical importance.
* **Community ownership:** Many items appear to have originated with Jewish organisations and families rather than the government.
* **Individual property:** Names, inscriptions and school records may link particular materials to identifiable owners or descendants.
* **Public access:** Conservation and digitisation have created a separate question about where the objects can be most safely preserved and studied.

</div>

This is not merely a legal quarrel over old books. Iraq’s Jewish population was once one of the world’s oldest Jewish communities, with roots extending back more than two millennia. Its rapid displacement in the twentieth century left religious buildings, property and archives vulnerable to confiscation or abandonment. The basement discovery preserved evidence of that vanished communal world while reopening the unresolved question of who has the right to speak for it.

## Did Gertrude Bell die by accident or suicide?

Gertrude Bell was a British traveller, intelligence officer, political adviser and archaeologist who played an influential role in the establishment of the Iraqi monarchy after the First World War. She also helped create the institution that became the Iraq Museum. On 12 July 1926, she was found dead at her Baghdad home after taking an overdose of sleeping medication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gertrudebell.ncl.ac.uk/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gertrudebell.ncl.ac.uk">[gertrudebell.ncl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gertrudebell.ncl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ncl.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The immediate cause of death is not seriously disputed. The uncertainty concerns intention.

Evidence sometimes used to support an accidental overdose includes her instruction that a servant should wake her the following morning. Sleeping medicines of the period were dangerous, and Bell was physically unwell. An unintended fatal dose is therefore entirely possible.

The suicide interpretation draws on her deteriorating health, periods of depression, political marginalisation and recent personal losses. Shortly before her death, she reportedly made arrangements for the care of her dog and wrote in a melancholy manner about loneliness and the future. Later biographers have often regarded suicide as likely, although the surviving evidence does not establish it conclusively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eleanorscottarchaeology.com/els-archaeology-blog/2017/3/17/the-death-of-gertrude-bell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eleanorscottarchaeology.com">[ELEANOR SCOTT ARCHAEOLOGY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eleanorscottarchaeology.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ELEANOR SCOTT ARCHAEOLOGYThe Death Of Gertrude Bell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ELEANOR SCOTT ARCHAEOLOGYThe Death Of Gertrude Bell</span></span></span>

The case has also been shaped by changing attitudes. Earlier accounts tended to avoid explicit discussion of suicide, partly out of concern for Bell’s family and reputation. Later treatments have sometimes moved too far in the opposite direction, presenting intention as settled fact.

No known note explains her actions, and the available circumstantial evidence points in both directions. Her death is best described as a fatal overdose of uncertain intent. Its place in Iraqi history comes not from the manner of death alone but from its timing: Bell died only a few years after helping construct a new political order whose stability she had already begun to doubt.

## Why so many Iraqi mysteries remain unresolved

Iraq’s historical record is exceptionally rich, but it is not continuous. Ancient cities were repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt. Early excavators sometimes valued spectacular objects more than skeletal, environmental or contextual evidence. Modern wars damaged museums, archives and archaeological sites. Looters removed objects without recording where they came from, while political secrecy concealed collections or restricted scholarly access.

These conditions create several different kinds of mystery:

* **Missing evidence**, as with looted museum objects whose present locations are unknown.
* **Ambiguous evidence**, as with the Baghdad vessel, whose physical form permits several interpretations.
* **Incomplete excavation**, as at Ur, where modern forensic questions cannot be answered for every body.
* **Confused tradition**, as with the possible transfer of the Hanging Gardens from Nineveh to Babylon. * **Contested ownership**, as with the Iraqi Jewish Archive.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Jewish_Archive" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Iraqi Jewish Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Iraqi Jewish Archive</span></span></span> * **Uncertain intention**, as in Gertrude Bell’s fatal overdose.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/gertrude-bell-archive" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Gertrude Bell Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Gertrude Bell Archive</span></span></span>

The strongest explanations are usually those that account for both the evidence and its limitations. The Hanging Gardens may have grown from real Assyrian engineering, but that does not make the Nineveh identification certain. The Baghdad vessel can produce voltage, but capability is not proof of purpose. The attendants at Ur were probably killed, but their beliefs and degree of choice remain inaccessible. Thousands of antiquities are genuinely missing, yet claims of a single mastermind or hidden hoard oversimplify a fragmented history of theft, concealment and trafficking.

Iraq’s enduring enigmas matter because they expose the difference between possessing the past and understanding it. An artefact without its findspot, a document without an owner and a death without testimony can all survive physically while losing the information needed to interpret them. The mystery often lies not in something supernatural, but in the historical path by which evidence was broken apart.
