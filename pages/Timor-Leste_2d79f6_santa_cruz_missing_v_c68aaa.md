## Introduction

The Santa Cruz massacre is one of the best-documented atrocities of the Indonesian occupation of [Timor-Leste]({{ 'what-remains-unresolved-in-timor-lestes/' | relative_url }}), yet it also contains one of the country's most enduring historical mysteries: what became of many of the people who disappeared after the shooting. On 12 November 1991, Indonesian security forces opened fire on a peaceful funeral procession at Santa Cruz Cemetery in Dili. The attack was witnessed by foreign journalists and secretly filmed, ensuring that the violence could not be denied. Even so, the exact number of people killed, the fate of many who were detained afterwards, and the locations of numerous victims' remains have never been fully established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/ASA21/023/1991/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International East Timor: The Santa Cruz massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalEast Timor: The Santa Cruz massacre - Amnesty InternationalNovember 14, 1991...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 1991</span></span></span>

The uncertainty is not primarily about whether the massacre happened. That is firmly established. The mystery lies in the incomplete record left behind by military secrecy, hurried burials, disappearances after arrest, and decades of limited access to official records and grave sites.

## How the funeral procession became a mass shooting

The procession began as a memorial for Sebastião Gomes, a young independence supporter who had been killed by Indonesian forces during an earlier confrontation at Motael Church. Thousands of mourners walked peacefully through Dili towards Santa Cruz Cemetery carrying banners and chanting in favour of East Timorese self-determination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/asa570042011en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalTimor-Leste: Santa Cruz massacre – still waiting for justice 20 years laterJune 9, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 9, 2021</span></span></span>

As the crowd entered the cemetery, Indonesian troops and police moved into position. Witnesses consistently reported that soldiers opened fire without meaningful warning. Many victims were trapped by the cemetery walls while others were shot as they tried to escape. The attack lasted only minutes, but it transformed an act of remembrance into one of the defining massacres of the occupation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/ASA21/023/1991/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International East Timor: The Santa Cruz massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalEast Timor: The Santa Cruz massacre - Amnesty InternationalNovember 14, 1991...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 1991</span></span></span>

The massacre marked a turning point because it occurred in front of international observers. Unlike many earlier killings during the occupation, this one could not easily be concealed.

## What witnesses and filmed evidence established

The Santa Cruz massacre became internationally known largely because several foreign journalists were present. British cameraman Max Stahl secretly filmed the shooting, while American journalists Allan Nairn and Amy Goodman witnessed the attack directly and were themselves assaulted by Indonesian troops. Stahl successfully smuggled the videotape out of East Timor, allowing television audiences around the world to see soldiers firing into an unarmed crowd.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/resource/blaming-the-victims-the-12-november-1991-massacre-in-dili-east-timor-and-the-response-of-the-indonesian-government/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsBlaming the victims: the 12 November 1991 massacre in Dili, East Timor, and the response of the Indone...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: november 1991</span></span></span>

The footage confirmed several key facts:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the procession was overwhelmingly peaceful before the shooting;
* Indonesian security forces used automatic weapons against civilians;
* many victims were fleeing rather than attacking soldiers;
* the violence continued even after panic spread through the cemetery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/resource/blaming-the-victims-the-12-november-1991-massacre-in-dili-east-timor-and-the-response-of-the-indonesian-government/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsBlaming the victims: the 12 November 1991 massacre in Dili, East Timor, and the response of the Indone...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: november 1991</span></span></span>

</div>

This visual evidence effectively ended attempts to dismiss the event as a minor disturbance. Although Indonesian authorities acknowledged that deaths had occurred, they initially claimed security forces had responded to violent rioters. Eyewitness testimony and the surviving film strongly contradicted that version of events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/ASA21/023/1991/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International East Timor: The Santa Cruz massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalEast Timor: The Santa Cruz massacre - Amnesty InternationalNovember 14, 1991...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 1991</span></span></span>

## Why the death toll remains uncertain

One of the central historical puzzles is the number of people who actually died.

Immediately after the massacre, Indonesian authorities reported a much lower death toll than independent observers. Human rights organisations initially estimated between about 50 and 100 people had been killed, while later investigations suggested the true figure may have been substantially higher once those who disappeared after arrest or died from injuries were included.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/ASA21/023/1991/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International East Timor: The Santa Cruz massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalEast Timor: The Santa Cruz massacre - Amnesty InternationalNovember 14, 1991...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 1991</span></span></span>

Several factors explain why no universally accepted total exists.

First, many wounded people were removed by the military before independent investigators could document them. Second, numerous participants were arrested in the following hours and days, making it difficult to distinguish those killed during the shooting from those who later disappeared in custody. Third, families often feared reporting missing relatives because doing so could expose them to further reprisals during the occupation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/ASA21/024/1991/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International East Timor: After the massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalEast Timor: After the massacre - Amnesty International...</span></span></span>

Rather than reflecting disagreement over whether killings occurred, the competing estimates reflect incomplete documentation under conditions of military control.

## Why the locations of many bodies remain uncertain

The most enduring mystery concerns the fate of many victims after the shooting ended.

Human rights investigations gathered testimony that some bodies were rapidly removed by security forces before relatives could recover them. Other detainees reportedly disappeared after arrest and were believed to have been killed later. Amnesty International documented allegations that some prisoners died in custody and were buried in unmarked graves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/ASA21/024/1991/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International East Timor: After the massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalEast Timor: After the massacre - Amnesty International...</span></span></span>

These accounts have never been fully resolved because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* many military records remain unavailable or were never preserved;
* witnesses died before giving complete testimony;
* some suspected burial locations were altered over time;
* comprehensive forensic investigations of every suspected grave have never been possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/east-timor/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">East Timor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">East Timor - International Commission on Missing Persons...</span></span></span>

</div>

As a result, numerous families have never received confirmed information about where relatives were buried or precisely how they died.

## Investigations and what they could—and could not—prove

Several investigations examined the massacre.

Indonesia established official inquiries soon after the event, but critics argued that these investigations accepted official military explanations too readily and failed to establish full accountability. Independent reviews, including a fact-finding mission by the International Commission of Jurists, relied heavily on eyewitness testimony and surviving film evidence to challenge the government's account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/resource/blaming-the-victims-the-12-november-1991-massacre-in-dili-east-timor-and-the-response-of-the-indonesian-government/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of JuristsBlaming the victims: the 12 November 1991 massacre in Dili, East Timor, and the response of the Indone...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: november 1991</span></span></span>

After Timor-Leste's independence, the Commission for Reception, Truth and Reconciliation (CAVR) documented the massacre within the broader pattern of human rights abuses committed during the occupation. It concluded that enforced disappearances and unlawful killings formed part of a much wider system of violence and recommended continued efforts to identify the missing and investigate grave sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/east-timor/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">East Timor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">East Timor - International Commission on Missing Persons...</span></span></span>

International forensic specialists have since assisted in exhumations at a number of conflict-related burial sites in Timor-Leste. These efforts have helped identify some victims of the occupation, but they have not resolved every disappearance connected with Santa Cruz itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/east-timor/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">East Timor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">East Timor - International Commission on Missing Persons...</span></span></span>

## The unresolved legacy of Santa Cruz
The Santa Cruz massacre occupies an unusual place in modern history. Unlike many historical atrocities, the shooting itself was captured on film and witnessed by international journalists. The mystery therefore does not concern whether the massacre occurred but what happened afterwards.

Questions remain about the precise number of people who died, the identities of everyone who disappeared, where many victims were buried, and whether all relevant military records still exist. These unresolved issues continue to affect surviving families, many of whom have never been able to recover relatives' remains or obtain a complete official account of their fate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/east-timor/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[icmp.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">East Timor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">East Timor - International Commission on Missing Persons...</span></span></span>

For Timor-Leste, Santa Cruz is remembered both as a tragedy and as a turning point. The images from the cemetery drew unprecedented international attention to the occupation, but the missing victims remain a reminder that even well-documented atrocities can leave unanswered questions that persist for generations.
