## Introduction

The Meroitic language presents one of [Sudan]({{ 'what-secrets-still-hide-in-sudans-past/' | relative_url }})'s greatest historical puzzles because scholars can **read its script but still cannot fully understand the language**. This distinction is crucial. Since the early twentieth century, researchers have known the sound values of the Meroitic signs well enough to pronounce many inscriptions and identify names, titles and recurring formulas. What remains elusive is the meaning of much of the vocabulary and grammar. As a result, thousands of inscriptions left by the Kingdom of Kush can be read aloud but not translated with confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119399919.eahaa00443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Meroitic Language</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeroitic Language - Rilly - Major Reference Works - Wiley Online LibraryOctober 28, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2021</span></span></span>

The mystery matters far beyond linguistics. Kush was one of Africa's great ancient kingdoms, ruling much of the Middle Nile and, for a time, Egypt itself. Yet historians still rely heavily on archaeology and foreign writers because Kush's own written voice remains only partly accessible. Every advance in understanding Meroitic has the potential to reshape what we know about Sudan's ancient political history, religion, economy and society.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/35472/chapter-abstract/303803188" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicLanguage and Writing in the Kingdom of Meroe &#124; The Oxford Handbook of Ancient Nubia &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 13, 2021</span></span></span>

## How the script was deciphered

The first major breakthrough came not from translating the language but from identifying how its writing system worked.

Around 1909–1911, the British Egyptologist Francis Llewellyn Griffith compared Meroitic inscriptions with Egyptian names and titles that appeared in recognisable historical contexts. By matching royal names and place names known from Egyptian sources, he established the phonetic values of most Meroitic characters. This achievement was comparable to learning how to pronounce every letter of an unfamiliar alphabet without yet knowing the language written in it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-11/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unicode.org">[Unicode]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unicode.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Chapter 11 – Unicode 17.0.0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chapter 11 – Unicode 17.0.0...</span></span></span>

Meroitic exists in two closely related scripts:

* **Hieroglyphic Meroitic**, used mainly on temples and royal monuments.
* **Cursive Meroitic**, the everyday script used for administrative documents, funerary inscriptions, graffiti and other practical texts.

Both scripts represent the same language, and both were derived in appearance from Egyptian writing while functioning very differently. Unlike Egyptian hieroglyphs, Meroitic writing is largely an alphabetic or alphasyllabic system with a relatively small set of signs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode17.0.0/core-spec/chapter-11/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unicode.org">[Unicode]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unicode.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Chapter 11 – Unicode 17.0.0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chapter 11 – Unicode 17.0.0...</span></span></span>

This means the script itself is no longer the mystery. The challenge lies in understanding the language encoded by those symbols.

## Why reading the signs is not enough

A useful comparison is hearing someone read an unfamiliar language aloud. You might recognise individual sounds without understanding a single sentence.

That is effectively the situation with Meroitic. Researchers can transliterate inscriptions into the Roman alphabet with considerable confidence, but pronunciation alone reveals little unless the meanings of words and grammatical structures are known.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119399919.eahaa00443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Meroitic Language</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeroitic Language - Rilly - Major Reference Works - Wiley Online LibraryOctober 28, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2021</span></span></span>

Several obstacles combine to keep the language only partly understood.

**There is no Meroitic equivalent of the Rosetta Stone.** Egyptian hieroglyphs became decipherable because scholars possessed a long bilingual inscription presenting the same text in different languages. No comparable bilingual document has been discovered for Meroitic, depriving researchers of the most direct route to translation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119399919.eahaa00443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Meroitic Language</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeroitic Language - Rilly - Major Reference Works - Wiley Online LibraryOctober 28, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2021</span></span></span>

**The language has no living descendants that can simply be consulted.** Meroitic disappeared by the fourth or fifth century AD, leaving no continuous literary tradition. Although modern linguistic research has made substantial progress in identifying its broader language family, there is no uninterrupted chain of speakers preserving its vocabulary or grammar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/35472/chapter-abstract/303803188" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicLanguage and Writing in the Kingdom of Meroe &#124; The Oxford Handbook of Ancient Nubia &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 13, 2021</span></span></span>

**Most surviving texts are repetitive.** Of roughly 2,000 known inscriptions, many are funerary texts following highly formulaic patterns. Such texts are valuable for identifying recurring words and grammatical endings, but they offer limited insight into everyday vocabulary or complex historical narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119399919.eahaa00443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Meroitic Language</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeroitic Language - Rilly - Major Reference Works - Wiley Online LibraryOctober 28, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2021</span></span></span>

## What scholars can translate with confidence

Despite popular descriptions of Meroitic as "undeciphered", important parts of the language are understood.

Researchers can identify many:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* royal names and family relationships;
* divine names;
* place names;
* common royal titles;
* some funerary expressions;
* grammatical endings that indicate relationships between words.

</div>

Because these elements recur across many inscriptions, scholars can often determine their functions even when the surrounding text remains uncertain. Archaeological context also helps. If an inscription appears beside a temple offering or on a royal tomb, repeated expressions can often be interpreted with reasonable confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/meroitic-language-and-writing-system/D1534C93DCCA955D82E0594CE92598FC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment The Meroitic Language and Writing System</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Meroitic Language and Writing System...</span></span></span>

Even so, confidence varies greatly from text to text. Short dedications may be understood fairly well, while longer historical inscriptions can still contain large sections whose precise meanings remain debated.

## Has the language family been identified?

One of the biggest advances of the past two decades has been the growing scholarly consensus that Meroitic belongs to the Northern East Sudanic branch of the wider Nilo-Saharan language grouping.

This identification, developed most prominently through the work of Claude Rilly, has allowed linguists to compare Meroitic systematically with related languages spoken in parts of Sudan, Chad and Eritrea. These comparisons have improved understanding of grammar and suggested meanings for additional words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119399919.eahaa00443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Meroitic Language</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeroitic Language - Rilly - Major Reference Works - Wiley Online LibraryOctober 28, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2021</span></span></span>

However, identifying a language family is not the same as translating texts. English and Hindi both belong to the Indo-European family, yet knowing that relationship alone would not enable someone to translate Shakespeare into Hindi. Likewise, recognising Meroitic's probable relatives narrows the search but does not unlock its complete vocabulary.

## Why archaeology alone cannot solve the mystery

Every new excavation produces additional inscriptions, but new texts do not automatically produce new translations.

Many discoveries simply repeat familiar religious or funerary formulas. What scholars hope to uncover are unusual documents such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* long historical narratives;
* administrative records;
* legal texts;
* private letters;
* or, ideally, a bilingual inscription.

</div>

Any of these could provide fresh vocabulary in contexts where meaning is easier to infer. A genuine bilingual text remains the most coveted discovery because it could immediately confirm or overturn many existing hypotheses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/meroitic-language-and-writing-system/D1534C93DCCA955D82E0594CE92598FC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment The Meroitic Language and Writing System</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Meroitic Language and Writing System...</span></span></span>

This explains why archaeological work at Kushite sites continues to matter for linguistics as much as for history.

## Can computers decipher Meroitic?

Modern computing has opened new possibilities, but it has not solved the problem.

Researchers have built digital corpora of Meroitic texts and applied statistical methods, machine learning and cross-language comparison to identify recurring patterns that human readers might overlook. Recent computational studies aim to compare Meroitic with Egyptian and related African languages, creating tools that may gradually refine existing interpretations rather than producing a dramatic overnight breakthrough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://aclanthology.org/2025.alp-1.11/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aclanthology.org">[aclanthology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aclanthology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ACL Anthology Towards Ancient Meroitic Decipherment: A Computational Approach</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ACL AnthologyTowards Ancient Meroitic Decipherment: A Computational Approach - ACL Anthology...</span></span></span>

These methods are promising because they can process thousands of textual relationships simultaneously. Nevertheless, computers still depend on reliable linguistic evidence. Without enough securely translated words to train their models, artificial intelligence cannot simply infer an unknown language by itself.

## What a breakthrough could reveal about Kush

A fuller understanding of Meroitic would transform the study of ancient Sudan.

Historians might gain direct answers to questions that currently depend on archaeology or foreign observers, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* how Kushite kings described their own reigns;
* how government administration functioned;
* the details of religious beliefs and rituals;
* trade networks and taxation;
* succession disputes and political crises;
* the perspectives of ordinary people whose voices are largely absent today.

</div>

At present, the history of Kush is reconstructed from monuments, excavated objects and Greek, Roman and Egyptian accounts. Deciphering Meroitic more completely would allow the kingdom to speak in its own words rather than primarily through those of its neighbours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/35472/chapter-abstract/303803188" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[oup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicLanguage and Writing in the Kingdom of Meroe &#124; The Oxford Handbook of Ancient Nubia &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 13, 2021</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery endures

The enduring puzzle of Meroitic is not that scholars cannot read its writing. They can. The mystery lies in the gap between recognising sounds and understanding meaning.

More than a century after Griffith established the script's phonetic values, researchers have made genuine progress by identifying grammatical patterns, classifying the language within a broader African family and expanding the corpus of known inscriptions. Yet the absence of a bilingual text, the limited range of surviving documents and the extinction of the language continue to prevent a complete translation.

For Sudan's history, this means that thousands of inscriptions still preserve information that is visible but only partly accessible. The Kingdom of Kush left its own written record, but much of what it wished to say remains locked behind a language that scholars can pronounce far more easily than they can understand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119399919.eahaa00443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Meroitic Language</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeroitic Language - Rilly - Major Reference Works - Wiley Online LibraryOctober 28, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2021</span></span></span>
