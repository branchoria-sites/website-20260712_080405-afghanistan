## Introduction

Was Kumbi Saleh really the capital of the medieval Ghana Empire? The short answer is that nobody knows for certain. The ruined city in south-eastern [Mauritania]({{ 'mauritania/' | relative_url }}) is one of the most important archaeological sites in West Africa and has long been identified as the capital described by medieval Arabic writers. Yet despite decades of excavation, no inscription explicitly names the site as Ghana's royal capital. Archaeologists have uncovered an impressive Muslim trading town, monumental stone buildings and one of the earliest known large mosques in West Africa, but the royal settlement described in the written sources has never been securely identified. That gap between archaeology and historical texts has made Kumbi Saleh one of Mauritania's most enduring historical debates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter-abstract/553497129?login=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe Empire of Ghana &#124; Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History &#124; Oxford AcademicMarch 26, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2019</span></span></span>

## What medieval writers said about Ghana's capital

The mystery begins not with archaeology but with written descriptions from the medieval Islamic world. Merchants and geographers recorded accounts of the wealthy kingdom known as Ghana, famous for controlling parts of the trans-Saharan gold trade.

The most influential account came from the eleventh-century Andalusian scholar al-Bakri. Although he never visited the region himself, he gathered reports from merchants and travellers. He described Ghana's capital as consisting of two settlements about six miles (roughly ten kilometres) apart. One was a prosperous Muslim town with numerous mosques, merchants and judges. The other was the king's residence, known as Al-Ghaba, where royal ceremonies took place and traditional religious practices continued alongside a mosque for Muslim visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter-abstract/553497129?login=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe Empire of Ghana &#124; Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History &#124; Oxford AcademicMarch 26, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2019</span></span></span>

Later writers broadly reinforced the picture of a wealthy royal city but introduced complications. The twelfth-century geographer al-Idrisi described the royal city as lying beside a river and even mentioned a palace that he believed had been built in the early twelfth century. Whether this reflected a genuine move of the capital, confusion over geography or errors introduced during manuscript copying remains uncertain. Medieval writers often used the term "Nile" for major West African rivers, making precise locations difficult to reconstruct.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koumbi_Saleh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Koumbi Saleh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Koumbi Saleh</span></span></span>

These accounts provide invaluable historical evidence, but they also create the central puzzle: can the surviving ruins at Kumbi Saleh be matched confidently to the cities described in these texts?

## What excavations at Kumbi Saleh revealed

French researchers first identified the ruins in the early twentieth century, with major excavations beginning in the late 1930s and continuing through the second half of the twentieth century. They uncovered an unexpectedly large urban settlement built largely from local stone rather than mud brick, demonstrating a sophisticated and long-lasting town in the Sahel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/1546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Site archologique de Kumbi Saleh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSite archologique de Kumbi Saleh - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Archaeologists found evidence of:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Dense neighbourhoods with substantial stone houses.
* A large congregational mosque that was rebuilt and expanded over time.
* Imported goods linked to long-distance commerce.
* Occupation stretching from the late first millennium into roughly the thirteenth or fourteenth century, overlapping the period when Ghana flourished.
* Material evidence for participation in trans-Saharan trade networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/1546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Site archologique de Kumbi Saleh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSite archologique de Kumbi Saleh - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

</div>

These discoveries strongly support the idea that Kumbi Saleh was an important commercial centre connected to the Ghana Empire. The scale of the settlement fits what would be expected of a major trading city.

However, the excavations also exposed an important limitation. No inscription identifies the site as "Ghana" or names its ruler. Archaeology has revealed a flourishing city but has not produced the single piece of evidence that would settle the debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter-abstract/553497129?login=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe Empire of Ghana &#124; Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History &#124; Oxford AcademicMarch 26, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2019</span></span></span>

## Why the royal city may still be missing

The greatest difficulty is that the archaeological evidence matches only part of al-Bakri's description.

Researchers have convincingly identified a large Muslim settlement, but the separate royal city described in the medieval sources has never been located with certainty. This has led to several competing explanations.

One possibility is that archaeologists have excavated only part of a much larger urban landscape. The royal quarter may remain buried beyond the areas investigated or may have been destroyed by erosion, later occupation or centuries of stone reuse. Given the vast size of the archaeological zone, not every area has been examined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/1546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Site archologique de Kumbi Saleh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSite archologique de Kumbi Saleh - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Another possibility is that the royal settlement was located some distance away, exactly as al-Bakri described. If so, the surviving ruins may represent the Muslim commercial town, while the king's residence lies elsewhere and has yet to be identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koumbi_Saleh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Koumbi Saleh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Koumbi Saleh</span></span></span>

A third explanation questions the assumption that Kumbi Saleh was the capital at all. Some historians argue that the identification became accepted because the archaeological site fit many expectations, not because decisive proof existed. They suggest the true royal capital may have stood elsewhere within the wider Ghana Empire, with Kumbi Saleh functioning as one of its principal commercial centres instead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter-abstract/553497129?login=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe Empire of Ghana &#124; Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History &#124; Oxford AcademicMarch 26, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2019</span></span></span>

Finally, the capital itself may not have been permanent. Like many medieval African states, Ghana could have shifted its royal residence over time in response to political, military or commercial changes. If the capital moved, different medieval writers may have been describing different places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koumbi_Saleh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Koumbi Saleh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Koumbi Saleh</span></span></span>

## Why scholars remain cautious

The debate illustrates a broader challenge in African archaeology: matching written accounts to excavated places is rarely straightforward.

Several factors encourage caution:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Medieval Arabic writers depended largely on second-hand reports rather than personal observation.
* Place names changed over centuries and were copied through many manuscripts.
* Archaeological evidence often survives unevenly, especially in desert environments.
* Political capitals are not always archaeologically distinctive from major trading centres.
* The absence of inscriptions means identification must rely on circumstantial evidence rather than direct proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter-abstract/553497129?login=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe Empire of Ghana &#124; Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History &#124; Oxford AcademicMarch 26, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2019</span></span></span>

</div>

Modern scholarship therefore tends to avoid absolute statements. Rather than declaring the mystery solved, many researchers describe Kumbi Saleh as the strongest candidate for Ghana's capital while recognising that the evidence remains incomplete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter-abstract/553497129" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe Empire of Ghana &#124; Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History &#124; Oxford Academic...</span></span></span>

## Why the debate still matters

The argument over Kumbi Saleh is about far more than the name of one ruined city.

If the site is confirmed as Ghana's capital, it provides direct archaeological evidence for one of the earliest large states in West Africa and demonstrates the scale of urban life supported by trans-Saharan commerce. If it is not the capital, then one of medieval Africa's greatest royal cities still awaits discovery.

The debate also reshapes older assumptions about West African history. Excavations have shown that complex urban centres, monumental architecture and international trade flourished in the western Sahel long before European contact. Whether or not Kumbi Saleh proves to be the royal capital itself, it remains one of the most significant archaeological sites for understanding the political and commercial world of the Ghana Empire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/1546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Site archologique de Kumbi Saleh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSite archologique de Kumbi Saleh - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Today the strongest conclusion is also the most careful one: Kumbi Saleh is unquestionably a major medieval city linked to the Ghana Empire, but no inscription or decisive archaeological discovery has yet demonstrated beyond doubt that it was the royal capital described by medieval writers. Until new excavations or new evidence emerge, the "lost capital" question remains one of Mauritania's most important historical mysteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter-abstract/553497129?login=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe Empire of Ghana &#124; Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History &#124; Oxford AcademicMarch 26, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2019</span></span></span>
