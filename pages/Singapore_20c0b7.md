## Introduction

Some of these cases remain open because witnesses died, records were never created or physical evidence was lost. Others survive because folklore supplied a satisfying story where the archive could not. The strongest approach is therefore to separate three things: what contemporary evidence establishes, what later investigators reasonably infer, and what public memory has added. Viewed that way, Singapore’s mysteries reveal more than isolated puzzles. They expose the fragility of a small island’s historical record and the consequences of colonial development, war, rapid urban change and repeated retelling.

## What was written on the Singapore Stone?

The [Singapore Stone]({{ 'singapore-stone/' | relative_url }}) is the country’s oldest and most important undeciphered text. When British settlers arrived in 1819, a large inscribed sandstone boulder stood near the mouth of the Singapore River, close to the present-day Fullerton area. The mystery became far harder to solve in 1843, when the original boulder was blown apart to widen the river entrance. Lieutenant-Colonel James Low reportedly recovered three sizeable fragments and sent them to the Asiatic Society of Bengal, but only one is known to survive in Singapore today. Most of the inscription was therefore destroyed before photography, modern epigraphy or archaeological recording could preserve it properly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-21-issue-1-apr-jun-2025-origins-badang-strongman-singapore-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg">[nlb.gov.sg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uncovering the Origins of Badang the Strongmanby WL Gibson — This fragment, called the Singapore Stone, is on display at the Na...</span></span></span>

Scholars broadly agree that the script resembles Kawi, a writing system once used across parts of maritime Southeast Asia. The language is less certain. Old Javanese and Sanskrit remain leading possibilities, while Tamil connections have also been proposed. Even where individual characters appear recognisable, the fragment is too damaged and the surviving sequence too short to produce an agreed translation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/the-singapore-story-through-60-objects/networks-through-time/singapore-stone/story" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: roots.gov.sg">[roots.gov.sg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">roots.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Singapore StoneThe stone has been dated from the 10th to 14th centuries. Scholars have different views on the date and language of t...</span></span></span>

This uncertainty matters because the inscription might clarify Singapore’s place within the pre-modern trading and political networks of Java, Sumatra and the wider Indian Ocean. It could have recorded a royal order, a religious statement, a victory or a dedication. Those suggestions are plausible categories rather than translations: no scholar has established the text’s meaning.

The stone is sometimes connected to the legendary strongman Badang, who was said to have thrown a huge rock towards the river. That story belongs to literary and oral tradition, not to a verified account of the inscription’s creation. It remains culturally important, but it does not solve the archaeological puzzle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-21-issue-1-apr-jun-2025-origins-badang-strongman-singapore-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg">[BiblioAsia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uncovering the Origins of Badang the Strongmanby WL Gibson — This fragment, called the Singapore Stone, is on display at the Na...</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusion is therefore modest. The stone proves that pre-colonial Singapore possessed deeper written and regional connections than older colonial narratives admitted. What the text actually said may be permanently unrecoverable because the decisive evidence was destroyed.

## How many people died in the Sook Ching killings?

The Japanese occupation produced Singapore’s gravest unresolved historical accounting problem: the true human cost of the [Sook Ching]({{ 'sook-ching/' | relative_url }}) operation. From 21 February to 4 March 1942, Japanese forces ordered Chinese men, generally between the ages of 18 and 50, to report to screening centres. Those judged to be anti-Japanese or otherwise suspect were taken to execution sites around the island.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=cc4da337-3bcd-4f96-bdc6-5210646bdd90" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nlb.gov.sg">[NLB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nlb.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NLBOperation Sook Ching</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NLBOperation Sook Ching</span></span></span>

That basic outline is well established. The final death toll is not. Japanese post-war accounts cited about 6,000 deaths, while Singapore Chinese organisations and political leaders advanced much higher figures, sometimes reaching 70,000 or more. Historians have struggled to reconcile these estimates because the operation involved summary decisions, incomplete documentation, dispersed killing grounds and victims whose families often never learnt where they had died.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=cc4da337-3bcd-4f96-bdc6-5210646bdd90" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nlb.gov.sg">[NLB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nlb.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NLBOperation Sook Ching</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NLBOperation Sook Ching</span></span></span>

The missing names and graves are as important as the numerical dispute. Killings occurred at places including Changi, Punggol and coastal areas around Siglap and Bedok. Some burial grounds were discovered only through later construction or excavation. A major discovery of human remains in the Siglap area in 1962 helped drive the collection and ceremonial reinterment of victims’ remains at the Civilian War Memorial. Other sites may have been disturbed, built over or never identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sook_Ching" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sook Ching</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sook Ching</span></span></span>

No single estimate can be treated as a complete count. The lowest figure is difficult to reconcile with the number of known execution locations and survivor testimony, while the highest figures are also hard to demonstrate from surviving records. The uncertainty is not evidence that the massacre itself is doubtful. It reflects the methods of mass killing and the absence of a full victim register.

Sook Ching is therefore both a documented atrocity and an archival mystery. The unanswered questions concern scale, identity and location: precisely how many people were killed, who all of them were, and whether undiscovered or unmarked burial sites remain beneath the modern city.

## Why did two schoolboys disappear in 1986?

On 14 May 1986, 12-year-old friends Keh Chin Ann and Toh Hong Huat disappeared while making their way to Owen Primary School. They did not arrive for lessons and were never reliably seen again. Their case became known as that of the “McDonald’s boys” after the restaurant company offered a S$100,000 reward for information leading to their recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.straitstimes.com/singapore/very-rare-for-missing-person-cases-to-remain-unsolved" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: straitstimes.com">[The Straits Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">straitstimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Straits Times Man charged with murder of S&#x27;porean teen Felicia Teo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Straits Times Man charged with murder of S&#x27;porean teen Felicia Teo</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery begins with the lack of a confirmed crime scene. There was no established abduction point, no body, no verified ransom demand and no credible sighting that explained what happened. Searches extended beyond Singapore, and investigators considered the possibility that the boys had left the country, but no conclusive immigration or travel evidence emerged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonald%27s_boys_case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mc Donald&#x27;s boys case</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mc Donald&#x27;s boys case</span></span></span>

Several explanations circulated:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Running away:** Their families rejected this, noting that the boys had left savings and belongings behind. Nothing found later demonstrated a prepared departure.
* **Parental or family involvement:** Rumours arose from complicated family circumstances, but no public evidence established that a relative removed the boys.
* **Accidental death:** Suggestions that they drowned or entered a dangerous site have never been supported by recovered remains or physical evidence.
* **Abduction or trafficking:** This became one of the most persistent theories, partly because two children vanished together. Yet no verified demand, border record or later identification has substantiated it.
* **Murder:** Anonymous calls and sensational claims appeared after the disappearance, but none provided testable information or led investigators to the boys.

</div>

The distinction between possibility and evidence is crucial. Almost every major theory can explain one feature of the disappearance, but none explains the entire case with corroboration. Later stories about mutilated child beggars, secret rituals or overseas syndicates belong largely to rumour and retrospective speculation.

The boys’ disappearance became part of Singapore’s public memory because it contradicted ordinary assumptions about safety, surveillance and geographic scale. On a small island with controlled borders, it seemed inconceivable that two children could disappear completely. That intuition is emotionally powerful, but it is not evidence that an elaborate conspiracy occurred. The case remains unresolved because the first hours produced no decisive lead and later claims could not repair that absence.

## Who killed the four children at Geylang Bahru?

On the morning of 6 January 1979, four siblings aged between five and ten were killed inside their family’s one-room Housing and Development Board flat at Block 58, Geylang Bahru. Their parents, Tan Kuen Chai and Lee Mei Ying, had left early to operate their school transport service. When they returned, they found the children in the bathroom. The murders remain officially unsolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=d1137789-7c74-11e1-b0c4-0800200c9a66" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nlb.gov.sg">[NLB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nlb.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">article detail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">article detail</span></span></span>

The known circumstances suggest a deliberate attack rather than a burglary. The flat had not simply been ransacked, and investigators found no obvious sign that valuables were the motive. The suspected weapons, described in contemporary accounts as a chopping implement and a dagger, were not recovered. Police interviewed more than a hundred people but did not obtain evidence sufficient to charge anyone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=d1137789-7c74-11e1-b0c4-0800200c9a66" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nlb.gov.sg">[NLB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nlb.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">article detail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">article detail</span></span></span>

Much discussion has focused on whether the killer knew the household. An absence of obvious forced entry, familiarity with the family’s routine and the timing of the attack could point towards someone known to the victims or parents. Those details support an investigative direction, not an identification. Flats in densely populated estates were not sealed forensic environments, and a visitor could have been admitted for many innocent reasons.

Reports that the family later received a New Year card containing a disturbing reference to having “no offspring” have strengthened the theory of personal revenge. Yet the card’s authorship was never proved publicly. It might have come from the murderer, from someone with knowledge of the crime, or from a cruel person exploiting a widely reported tragedy.

The case illustrates the limits of late reinterpretation. Modern readers often assume that stored biological material could now produce a DNA match, but it is unclear what evidence survives, how it was collected or whether its chain of custody would support reliable testing. A reported new tip in 2021 renewed public attention, but no arrest or official resolution followed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geylang_Bahru_family_murders" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Geylang Bahru family murders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geylang Bahru family murders</span></span></span>

The most plausible broad explanation remains a targeted attack by someone with a grievance or knowledge of the family. That is still far from proof. Naming particular neighbours, relatives or business associates without charge or corroborated evidence turns an unresolved investigation into accusation. The historical record supports suspicion of a personal motive, but not the identity of the offender.

## Was there really a German girl on Pulau Ubin?

The German Girl Shrine on Pulau Ubin is a different kind of mystery. No lost inscription or police file lies at its centre. Instead, the puzzle concerns how a local sacred site acquired its modern identity.

The familiar story says that a German family operated a coffee plantation on Pulau Ubin at the outbreak of the First World War. When British personnel arrived to detain German nationals, the family’s daughter fled, fell from a cliff or into a quarry and died. Local workers supposedly found and buried her, and the grave later developed into a shrine. Her name is not known.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pulau-ubin.nparks.gov.sg/cultural-heritage/places-of-interest/german-girl-shrine/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pulau-ubin.nparks.gov.sg">[nparks.gov.sg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pulau-ubin.nparks.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">german girl shrine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">german girl shrine</span></span></span>

Parts of the setting are historically credible. German landholders were connected with property on Pulau Ubin, and British authorities did detain enemy nationals during the war. The difficulty is linking those facts to a specific dead girl. Archival research by writer William L. Gibson found no convincing contemporary record of the daughter, her death or the commonly described coffee plantation operating under the circumstances given in the legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pulau-ubin.nparks.gov.sg/cultural-heritage/places-of-interest/german-girl-shrine/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pulau-ubin.nparks.gov.sg">[Pulau Ubin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pulau-ubin.nparks.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">german girl shrine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">german girl shrine</span></span></span>

The shrine’s changing identity raises further doubt. A 1985 newspaper account reportedly associated the site with a Javanese princess rather than a German girl. The German narrative appeared in published form later, suggesting that the shrine may have supported more than one origin story before the best-known version became dominant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Girl_Shrine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">German Girl Shrine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">German Girl Shrine</span></span></span>

One alternative interpretation connects the shrine to older local traditions in which spiritually significant graves, natural features or termite mounds became places of protection and petition. Chinese worshippers could adopt and transform such a site, creating a layered practice that did not require a historically identifiable German burial. This would also explain why the shrine’s identity could shift while the sacred location endured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-17-issue-3-oct-dec-2021-ubins-german-girl-shrine/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg">[BiblioAsia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">vol 17 issue 3 oct dec 2021 ubins german girl shrine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">vol 17 issue 3 oct dec 2021 ubins german girl shrine</span></span></span>

The offerings now associated with the shrine, including cosmetics, dolls and other objects imagined as suitable for a young woman, reinforce the German girl’s presence in modern public memory. They do not independently prove that she existed. Ritual practice can make a story culturally real even when its biographical details remain unverified.

The fairest assessment is that a German connection to Pulau Ubin is documented, but the tragic daughter’s story is not securely established. The shrine may preserve a distorted memory of an actual death, a fusion of several traditions or a later narrative attached to an older sacred place. Its mystery lies less in identifying a ghost than in watching folklore, worship and local history reshape one another.

## What these mysteries reveal about Singapore

These cases persist for different reasons. The Singapore Stone was physically destroyed. Sook Ching was designed and conducted without a complete public record of its victims. The McDonald’s boys disappeared before investigators could establish a reliable scene. The Geylang Bahru murders left evidence but no provable offender. The German Girl Shrine accumulated stories faster than historians could verify them.

Together, they caution against treating every unanswered question as evidence of concealment. Missing information may result from violence, poor record-keeping, lost evidence, colonial priorities, urban redevelopment or the simple passage of time. In Singapore, rapid physical transformation has made this especially important: execution grounds, plantations, villages and crime scenes can disappear beneath roads, housing estates and landscaped parks while their stories continue circulating.

The strongest mysteries also change as the country changes. The Singapore Stone became a symbol of a pre-1819 past once dismissed or overlooked. Sook Ching’s unidentified dead became central to wartime remembrance. The vanished schoolboys and murdered siblings became warnings about the limits of security and investigation. The German Girl Shrine demonstrates how unofficial heritage can survive through practice even when its founding narrative cannot be proved.

None of these cases needs an exaggerated conspiracy to remain compelling. Their lasting power comes from the same unresolved question: how much of Singapore’s past can be recovered when the decisive inscription, witness, grave, object or document is no longer available?
