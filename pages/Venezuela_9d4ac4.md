## Did Simón Bolívar die of tuberculosis?

Simón Bolívar died on 17 December 1830 at the Quinta de San Pedro Alejandrino near Santa Marta, in what is now Colombia. He was 47, politically defeated, physically exhausted and preparing to leave South America. His French doctor, Alexandre Prosper Révérend, recorded a long decline marked by coughing, fever, weight loss and severe weakness. The post-mortem examination described extensive damage to Bolívar’s lungs, and tuberculosis became the accepted cause of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article-pdf/52/1/78/13802696/ciq071.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth - Oxford Academicby PG Auwaerter · 2011 · Cited by 12 — According to history books, tubercu...</span></span></span>

That conclusion is reasonable, but it was never as secure as a modern laboratory diagnosis. Révérend worked before bacteriology, chest imaging and reliable tests for tuberculosis. His report described what he could see rather than identifying a particular infectious organism. A later medical reassessment led by infectious-disease specialist Paul Auwaerter argued that parts of Bolívar’s clinical history and autopsy were not typical of terminal tuberculosis. The authors considered other possibilities, including a chronic fungal infection, and noted that arsenic exposure might have worsened his condition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article-pdf/52/1/78/13802696/ciq071.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth - Oxford Academicby PG Auwaerter · 2011 · Cited by 12 — According to history books, tubercu...</span></span></span>

Arsenic does not automatically imply murder. In the nineteenth century it appeared in medicines and could be absorbed from contaminated food, water or repeated treatments. Finding traces of it in remains would therefore be different from proving that someone deliberately administered a fatal dose.

The debate became politically charged under President Hugo Chávez, who repeatedly suggested that Bolívar had been poisoned by enemies. In July 2010, Venezuelan authorities opened the tomb in Caracas to examine the remains, confirm their identity and investigate the cause of death. The procedure transformed an old medical question into a public national drama involving forensic science, state ceremony and Chávez’s political use of Bolívar as a symbol of his own revolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2011/jul/26/hugo-chavez-liberation-hero-murdered" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hugo chavez liberation hero murdered</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianHugo Chávez claims Simon Bolívar was murdered not...July 26, 2011 — 26 Jul 2011 — Scientists found traces of toxins, includi...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 26, 2011</span></span></span>

The inquiry did not establish assassination. Venezuelan officials announced in 2011 that investigators could not prove death by unnatural causes or intentional poisoning. Toxic substances were reportedly detected, but their presence could not reveal when, why or in what quantity Bolívar had encountered them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csmonitor.com/World/Americas/Latin-America-Monitor/2011/0726/Venezuela-unable-to-determine-cause-of-Bolivar-s-death" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csmonitor.com">[Christian Science Monitor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csmonitor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“We could not establish the death was by non-natural means or by</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Christian Science MonitorVenezuela unable to determine cause of Bolivar&#x27;s deathJuly 26, 2011 — Now the results of the forensic analyses a...</span></span></span>

The fairest verdict is therefore narrower than either traditional certainty or conspiracy theory. Bolívar suffered from a serious, ultimately fatal illness involving his lungs. Tuberculosis remains plausible and may still be the best single explanation, but the surviving medical description is insufficient for an unquestionable diagnosis. Alternative natural diseases and harmful contemporary treatments cannot be excluded. There is no persuasive public evidence that he was murdered.

The mystery persists because Bolívar is more than a historical individual in Venezuela. Arguments about his body become arguments about ownership of the nation’s founding story. A diagnosis made in 1830 can consequently be challenged not only by doctors, but by later governments seeking a heroic victim, a betrayed revolutionary or a political ancestor.

## Where is Francisco de Miranda buried?

Francisco de Miranda’s disappearance is more literal. Venezuela knows where its great independence precursor died, but not where his body lies.

Born in Caracas in 1750, Miranda led an extraordinary transatlantic life. He fought in campaigns connected with the American and French revolutions, travelled widely through Europe and sought international support for the liberation of Spanish America. His meticulously organised papers, now known collectively as the *Colombeia*, are recognised by UNESCO as an exceptionally important documentary record of the independence era.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hispanic-anglosphere.com/individuals/miranda-francisco-de-1750-1816/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hispanic-anglosphere.com">[The Hispanic-Anglosphere...]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hispanic-anglosphere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">miranda francisco de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">miranda francisco de</span></span></span>

Miranda returned to Venezuela after the independence movement began in 1810 and became the leading military figure of the First Republic. When royalist forces advanced in 1812 and the republican position collapsed, he negotiated a capitulation. Younger patriots, including Bolívar, regarded this as treason. They detained Miranda before he could leave the country, and he passed into Spanish custody.

He was eventually imprisoned at the La Carraca naval arsenal near Cádiz, Spain. There he died on 14 July 1816, apparently from natural causes, before his legal case had reached a final conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_de_Miranda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Francisco de Miranda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francisco de Miranda</span></span></span>

The uncertainty begins after death. Miranda was buried without the honours later generations believed he deserved, commonly described as being placed in a communal grave. The site was not preserved in a way that would allow his remains to be securely identified and returned to Venezuela. His monument in the National Pantheon in Caracas is therefore a cenotaph: an honouring tomb without an authenticated body.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_de_Miranda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Francisco de Miranda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francisco de Miranda</span></span></span>

This is not an unsolved disappearance in the usual sense. There is no serious evidence that Miranda escaped, survived secretly or was buried under another identity. The mystery concerns the exact grave and the possibility of identification after more than two centuries.

Several obstacles make a solution unlikely. Prison burial records may be incomplete, the physical burial ground has changed, and communal graves can contain mixed or disturbed remains. Even if bones associated with La Carraca were recovered, identification would require a reliable chain of archaeological context and suitable genetic comparisons. A patriotic wish to find Miranda would not itself constitute evidence.

His missing body has nevertheless shaped Venezuelan public memory. Arturo Michelena’s 1896 painting of Miranda alone in his cell became one of the country’s defining historical images. It gives a visual form to abandonment: a cosmopolitan revolutionary who helped imagine an independent Spanish America, dying imprisoned by the old empire while estranged from the younger revolutionaries who inherited his cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://collections.arck-project.org/view/ARCK3D0000000038/pintura-de-miranda-en-la-carraca" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collections.arck-project.org">[The Arc/k Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collections.arck-project.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Arc/k Project Pintura de Miranda en La Carraca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Arc/k Project Pintura de Miranda en La Carraca</span></span></span>

The empty tomb consequently carries a historical tension. Venezuela celebrates Miranda as a founder, yet cannot physically recover him. His archive survived with remarkable richness; his body did not.

## Was El Dorado hidden beyond the Orinoco?

The most famous geographical mystery associated with Venezuela began as several different stories that Europeans gradually fused together. One concerned a gold-covered ruler or ritual in the Colombian Andes. Another concerned wealthy communities somewhere in the vast interior of the Guianas. By the late sixteenth century, explorers and mapmakers had transformed these reports into Manoa, a magnificent city supposedly standing beside a great inland body of water called [Lake Parime]({{ 'lake-parime/' | relative_url }}).

The Venezuelan connection came through the Orinoco. Spanish commander Antonio de Berrío searched through the region before Walter Raleigh seized him at Trinidad and gathered information about the interior. Raleigh then travelled into the Orinoco basin in 1595 and published an account presenting Guiana as a rich empire waiting to be discovered. He did not reach the alleged city or lake, but his descriptions gave European readers a compelling route towards them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://explorersweb.com/exploration-mysteries-lake-parime/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: explorersweb.com">[Explorersweb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">explorersweb.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">exploration mysteries lake parime</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">exploration mysteries lake parime</span></span></span>

Cartographers converted uncertain testimony into apparently precise geography. Maps from the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries placed an enormous Lake Parime south of the Orinoco, often with Manoa on its shore. The University of Pennsylvania’s digital map collection describes one influential map as combining genuine geographical information with fictional features derived from Raleigh’s El Dorado narrative. Once the lake appeared in respected atlases, later mapmakers repeatedly copied it, lending accumulated authority to an unverified claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dia.upenn.edu/en/content/CNT0183Y/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dia.upenn.edu">[dia.upenn.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dia.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on upenn.edu.</span></span></span>

This explains why the mystery lasted so long. Early European maps were not direct surveys of every feature they displayed. In poorly known regions, a feature might be based on an Indigenous account filtered through interpreters, an explorer’s commercial interests, an earlier printed map or a misunderstanding of seasonal conditions. Repetition could make speculation look like independent confirmation.

Raleigh’s claims also served political and personal purposes. He wanted English support for further expeditions and emphasised the wealth that might reward them. His second Guiana venture in 1617 ended disastrously: fighting occurred despite orders to avoid conflict with Spain, his son was killed, and expedition commander Lawrence Kemys took his own life. No golden city emerged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Parime" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lake Parime</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lake Parime</span></span></span>

By the turn of the nineteenth century, increasingly systematic exploration undermined the map tradition. Alexander von Humboldt’s investigations of the Orinoco and surrounding river systems led him to reject the great lake as mapped. He suggested that reports may have arisen from extensive seasonal flooding, which could make savannahs and connected waterways resemble a temporary inland sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elib.dlr.de/133510/1/Remote%20Sensing%20Archaeology%20-%20Searching%20for%20Lake%20Parime%20from%20Space-.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elib.dlr.de">[DLR Electronic Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elib.dlr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic Library Remote Sensing Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic Library Remote Sensing Archaeology</span></span></span>

Modern remote-sensing research has added an important qualification. Researchers have examined geological formations, satellite data, oral traditions and historical maps to ask whether some large prehistoric or periodically flooded body of water helped inspire Lake Parime. Such work does not validate Raleigh’s golden metropolis. It asks whether the fiction preserved a distorted memory of real hydrological conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elib.dlr.de/133510/1/Remote%20Sensing%20Archaeology%20-%20Searching%20for%20Lake%20Parime%20from%20Space-.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elib.dlr.de">[DLR Electronic Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elib.dlr.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic Library Remote Sensing Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic Library Remote Sensing Archaeology</span></span></span>

The most plausible explanation separates the legend into layers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Manoa as a vast gold-covered imperial city:** unsupported by archaeological or documentary evidence.
* **Lake Parime as a permanent lake matching early European maps:** almost certainly incorrect.
* **Large seasonal inundations or an older lake system:** geographically plausible and capable of generating stories about immense inland waters.
* **Indigenous access to gold objects or trading networks:** possible, but not evidence that the metal came from a nearby golden city.
* **European embellishment:** strongly supported by the way uncertain reports became larger, richer and more precise in printed accounts and maps.

</div>

El Dorado therefore matters less as a treasure location than as a lesson in how imagined geography is produced. Indigenous knowledge, mistranslation, colonial ambition and the authority of print combined to place a non-existent city on the map for generations. The search also drew outsiders deeper into the Orinoco–Guiana region, with serious consequences for the people who already lived there.

## Does Taima-Taima rewrite the peopling of the Americas?

Near Coro in Falcón state lies a very different kind of Venezuelan enigma. At Taima-Taima, archaeologists found stone artefacts and the remains of extinct large animals, including a mastodon-like proboscidean. The most celebrated discovery was a stone projectile point associated with, and described as penetrating, part of an animal’s pelvis.

Radiocarbon dates from wood associated with the deposits suggested an age of at least about 13,000 years before the present. The excavators interpreted the site as evidence that people using the distinctive El Jobo stone-tool tradition killed large animals in Venezuela before, or independently of, the Clovis culture once treated as the earliest widespread archaeological culture in the Americas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17738722/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The importance of the claim is easy to understand. Clovis sites in North America are roughly 13,000 years old. If people were already hunting large mammals at a well-established community site in northern South America at a comparable or earlier date, their ancestors must have entered the continent earlier still. Taima-Taima would therefore form part of the evidence against a simple model in which the first Americans rapidly spread south only after Clovis technology appeared.

The debate does not centre on whether ancient bones and stone tools exist at the site. It concerns association and context. Archaeologists must establish that an artefact and an animal bone belong to the same undisturbed deposit, rather than having been brought together by flowing water, animal activity, erosion, excavation disturbance or later movement through sediment. Radiocarbon dates from nearby wood date the wood itself; they do not automatically date every object found close to it.

Taima-Taima’s original investigators argued that its geology, dating and artefact–bone relationships supported a genuine human kill site. Their 1978 report presented multiple radiocarbon results and treated the association as strong evidence of a pre-Clovis hunting tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/110993075/An_El_Jobo_Mastodon_Kill_at_Taima_taima_Venezuela" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) An El Jobo Mastodon Kill at Taima-taima, Venezuela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) An El Jobo Mastodon Kill at Taima-taima, Venezuela</span></span></span>

Later research has strengthened the broader case that humans and extinct megafauna interacted in Late Pleistocene Venezuela. A 2022 peer-reviewed study reported damage on glyptodont skulls from Taima-Taima and nearby Muaco that the authors interpreted as evidence of human action. Glyptodonts were heavily armoured relatives of armadillos, and identifying deliberate blows or butchery marks on their bones can reveal how early people exploited animals very different from any living Venezuelan species.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13358-022-00253-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on springer.com.</span></span></span>

Research released in 2026 has proposed an even wider range of human-exploited megaherbivores at Taima-Taima. However, that work was initially circulated as a preprint, meaning it had not yet completed the conventional peer-review process when posted. Its findings are relevant but should not be treated as a final settlement of the site’s chronology or interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.01.22.701027v1.full-text" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biorxiv.org">[BioRxiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biorxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2026.01.22.701027v1.full text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2026.01.22.701027v1.full text</span></span></span>

The responsible conclusion is that Taima-Taima is a major early South American site, not a curiosity or proven fraud. It provides substantial evidence for human activity among extinct large mammals around the end of the Ice Age. Yet the exact dates of particular events, the integrity of every association and the scale of repeated human occupation remain legitimate research questions.

Its mystery is scientific rather than romantic. There is no missing city or secret expedition. The puzzle lies in sediment, microscopic surface marks and whether a stone point and an ancient skeleton came together through a human act more than thirteen millennia ago. Answering it affects not merely Venezuelan prehistory, but the wider story of when and how people reached South America.

## What these mysteries reveal about Venezuela

These cases survive because they sit where evidence and national meaning overlap. Bolívar’s damaged lungs became a political argument about betrayal. Miranda’s unlocated bones became an empty national shrine. A speculative lake became a geographical fact because printers repeated it. Ancient hunting evidence became part of an international dispute over the first settlement of the Americas.

They should not be treated as equally mysterious. The best-supported assessments are clear:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Bolívar’s precise fatal disease remains uncertain, but assassination has not been demonstrated.
* Miranda died in Spanish imprisonment and was probably placed in an unmarked communal burial; the location of identifiable remains is unknown.
* Manoa, the golden city of Lake Parime, belongs to colonial legend, although seasonal flooding or older waterways may have contributed to the story.
* Taima-Taima contains important evidence of early human–megafauna interaction, while the dating and context of individual finds still require careful testing.

</div>

Venezuela’s historical enigmas are therefore most revealing when stripped of unnecessary conspiracy. Their real fascination comes from seeing how uncertainty is created: by pre-modern medicine, neglected prisoners, colonial cartography, disturbed archaeological layers and the later needs of public memory. Each mystery preserves not simply a missing answer, but a record of how Venezuelans and outsiders have tried to make the country’s past visible.
