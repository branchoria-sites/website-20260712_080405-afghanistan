## Introduction

Prince Louis Rwagasore's assassination remains one of [Burundi]({{ 'what-secrets-still-haunt-burundis/' | relative_url }})'s defining historical mysteries not because the gunman escaped justice, but because the deeper political chain of responsibility has never been conclusively established. On 13 October 1961, only weeks after becoming Burundi's first elected prime minister and months before independence, Rwagasore was shot while dining at the Tanganyika Hotel in Usumbura (now Bujumbura). The immediate conspirators were identified, tried and punished. The enduring question is whether Belgian colonial officials merely failed to prevent the crime, actively encouraged it, or played a more direct role in bringing it about.

The available evidence has changed significantly over time. Contemporary investigations established a political conspiracy involving Rwagasore's domestic opponents, but left unresolved the conduct of senior Belgian administrators. Later archival discoveries have strengthened the case that some colonial officials encouraged or protected the plot, yet they still stop short of proving a complete command chain from Brussels to the assassin. That distinction explains why the case remains historically contested rather than legally settled.

## Who Really Ordered Rwagasore's Murder?

Rwagasore led the nationalist UPRONA party, which won an overwhelming victory in Burundi's September 1961 legislative elections. His programme rejected ethnic politics, sought rapid independence from Belgium, and challenged the influence that the colonial administration had exercised over Burundian political life.

Only sixteen days after taking office, he was assassinated by the Greek resident Ioannis (Jean) Kageorgis while dining with ministers and supporters. Kageorgis was arrested almost immediately. Court proceedings also implicated leading figures from the rival Christian Democratic Party (Parti Démocrate Chrétien, often abbreviated PDC or CDC in different sources), notably Jean-Baptiste Ntidendereza and Joseph Biroli, as organisers of the conspiracy. Kageorgis and several convicted accomplices were executed in 1962.

The criminal case therefore resolved one important question: Rwagasore was not the victim of an isolated gunman acting alone. His murder formed part of a coordinated political conspiracy. The unresolved issue lies one level higher—whether influential Belgian officials encouraged, facilitated or shielded that conspiracy.

## The Proven Political Conspiracy

The evidence accepted during the original trials established several points that historians broadly agree upon.

First, the assassination was politically motivated. Rwagasore had defeated parties regarded by many Burundians as closer to the Belgian administration, upsetting expectations that Belgium would guide Burundi towards a slower, more controlled transition to independence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2022/jan/05/new-evidence-of-belgian-complicity-in-1961-killing-of-burundian-pm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">new evidence of belgian complicity in 1961 killing of burundian pm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianNew evidence of Belgian complicity in 1961 killing of Burundian PM &#124; Burundi &#124; The GuardianJanuary 5, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2022</span></span></span>

Second, the conspiracy involved multiple participants rather than a spontaneous decision by the shooter. Financial arrangements, planning meetings and contacts among political opponents demonstrated preparation before the attack.

Third, the trials did not seriously investigate whether Belgian officials had influenced or encouraged the organisers. That omission later became one of the central criticisms of the judicial process.

Historians generally distinguish between these established facts and broader allegations. The existence of a conspiracy is well documented. The participation of Belgian state officials remains the disputed element.

## What Belgian Officials Knew

The debate changed substantially after Belgian researcher Ludo De Witte examined previously overlooked judicial and diplomatic archives for his study of the assassination.

The most frequently cited evidence concerns a meeting shortly after UPRONA's electoral victory. According to testimony gathered during a Belgian judicial inquiry in 1962, Belgian Resident Roberto Régnier reportedly declared that "Rwagasore must be killed." Four participants independently recalled words to that effect during questioning by the Brussels public prosecutor. Régnier reportedly acknowledged making the statement, although he disputed how it should be interpreted. The prosecutor's report was never published at the time and remained largely unknown for decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2022/jan/05/new-evidence-of-belgian-complicity-in-1961-killing-of-burundian-pm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">new evidence of belgian complicity in 1961 killing of burundian pm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianNew evidence of Belgian complicity in 1961 killing of Burundian PM &#124; Burundi &#124; The GuardianJanuary 5, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2022</span></span></span>

British diplomatic correspondence provides a second strand of evidence. Ambassador James Murray informed London that some Belgian officials displayed what he described as an "almost pathological hatred" of Rwagasore and referred specifically to Régnier's remarks as moving "very far in the direction of incitement to murder." These dispatches show that foreign diplomats were aware of serious concerns regarding the colonial administration's behaviour even before later archival discoveries became public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2022/jan/05/new-evidence-of-belgian-complicity-in-1961-killing-of-burundian-pm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">new evidence of belgian complicity in 1961 killing of burundian pm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianNew evidence of Belgian complicity in 1961 killing of Burundian PM &#124; Burundi &#124; The GuardianJanuary 5, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2022</span></span></span>

These documents significantly strengthen claims that parts of the Belgian colonial administration created an atmosphere in which violence against Rwagasore became conceivable. They also suggest that concerns about official conduct existed during the events themselves rather than being invented decades later.

## Why the Chain of Command Remains Unclear

Despite increasingly troubling archival evidence, historians still cannot reconstruct a complete chain of command.

Several key uncertainties remain:

* No surviving document has been found ordering Rwagasore's assassination from Brussels.
* No conclusive evidence publicly demonstrates that Belgian ministers explicitly instructed colonial officials to organise the murder.
* The surviving records do not identify every intermediary linking hostile statements by officials to the operational planning carried out by the convicted conspirators.
* Some archival material appears incomplete, while researchers have reported missing or inaccessible files that might have clarified decision-making.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2022/jan/05/new-evidence-of-belgian-complicity-in-1961-killing-of-burundian-pm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">new evidence of belgian complicity in 1961 killing of burundian pm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianNew evidence of Belgian complicity in 1961 killing of Burundian PM &#124; Burundi &#124; The GuardianJanuary 5, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2022</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. A political climate that encouraged violence is not identical to a documented government order authorising assassination. Historians therefore separate **incitement**, **complicity**, **administrative protection**, and **direct operational control**, recognising that the available evidence supports these possibilities to different degrees.

De Witte argues that responsibility lay primarily with senior colonial officials in Burundi while political leaders in Brussels tolerated or ignored their actions rather than directing every operational detail themselves. That interpretation has attracted considerable attention but has not produced an official Belgian judicial finding equivalent to the parliamentary conclusions reached regarding Patrice Lumumba's assassination in neighbouring Congo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2022/jan/05/new-evidence-of-belgian-complicity-in-1961-killing-of-burundian-pm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">new evidence of belgian complicity in 1961 killing of burundian pm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianNew evidence of Belgian complicity in 1961 killing of Burundian PM &#124; Burundi &#124; The GuardianJanuary 5, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2022</span></span></span>

## Why Historians Still Disagree

Most modern scholarship no longer treats allegations of Belgian involvement as mere speculation. Instead, debate focuses on the extent of that involvement.

One interpretation argues that Belgian colonial administrators actively encouraged Rwagasore's opponents, creating the political conditions in which assassination became the preferred solution after electoral defeat.

A more cautious interpretation accepts that some officials behaved irresponsibly and expressed hostility towards Rwagasore but argues that surviving evidence does not conclusively prove they directed the operational conspiracy.

A stronger interpretation, advanced most prominently by De Witte, contends that the available archives demonstrate effective Belgian complicity even without a written assassination order, because influential officials allegedly encouraged the conspiracy and subsequently failed to ensure a full investigation.

These positions differ mainly over standards of proof rather than over the existence of political hostility towards Rwagasore.

## Questions left unresolved by Rwagasore
Rwagasore's death removed Burundi's most popular nationalist leader before independence had even begun. His programme sought cooperation across ethnic divisions and rapid political self-government, making his assassination a pivotal moment in the country's modern history.

The unresolved questions also extend beyond Burundi itself. The case has become part of a wider reassessment of Belgium's role during African decolonisation. Following renewed scrutiny of colonial archives and debates surrounding Belgium's historical responsibilities in Congo, Rwanda and Burundi, historians have called for further archival research into Rwagasore's assassination and the conduct of colonial authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2022/jan/05/new-evidence-of-belgian-complicity-in-1961-killing-of-burundian-pm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">new evidence of belgian complicity in 1961 killing of burundian pm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianNew evidence of Belgian complicity in 1961 killing of Burundian PM &#124; Burundi &#124; The GuardianJanuary 5, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2022</span></span></span>

The mystery therefore no longer centres on identifying the killer. That question was answered in court. The enduring historical puzzle is whether the convicted conspirators acted largely on their own initiative or whether influential Belgian colonial officials deliberately encouraged, protected or enabled the plot. The surviving archives increasingly suggest that colonial authorities played a more significant role than was acknowledged in 1961, but they still do not provide a complete documentary record identifying every decision-maker or establishing an unbroken chain of command.
