## Introduction

In 1221, the oasis city of Merv, in what is now [Turkmenistan]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-turkmenistans/' | relative_url }}), fell to the Mongol army led by Tolui, a son of Genghis Khan. What happened afterwards is one of the best-documented atrocities of the Mongol conquests, yet it is also one of medieval history's greatest numerical mysteries. Contemporary and near-contemporary writers agreed that an exceptionally large massacre took place, but they disagreed sharply about how many people died. Some accounts claimed around 700,000 deaths, while others asserted that more than 1.3 million people were killed. Modern historians generally accept that the massacre was catastrophic but regard these medieval totals as impossible to verify and almost certainly exaggerated. The mystery is therefore not whether a massacre occurred, but how its scale should be understood when the surviving evidence consists of literary chronicles rather than reliable population records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A78c64807-5c43-43d9-999b-06ed6943d35b/files/dvx021f52m" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ora.ox.ac.uk">[ORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ora.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 13, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2022</span></span></span>

## The Mongol capture of Merv

By the early thirteenth century, Merv was among the greatest cities of Central Asia. It had flourished under the Seljuks as a centre of trade, scholarship and administration, and its population had grown further as refugees fled south from other towns devastated during the Mongol invasion of the Khwarazmian Empire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A78c64807-5c43-43d9-999b-06ed6943d35b/files/dvx021f52m" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ora.ox.ac.uk">[ORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ora.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 13, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2022</span></span></span>

In February 1221, Tolui's forces surrounded the city. After a short siege, Merv surrendered. According to several Persian and Arabic chroniclers, the inhabitants expected mercy, but instead the population was assembled outside the walls, separated into groups and systematically killed. Skilled artisans were reportedly spared and taken into captivity, a practice that appears repeatedly in Mongol campaigns because craftsmen were considered valuable imperial assets. The city itself was looted and much of it destroyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A78c64807-5c43-43d9-999b-06ed6943d35b/files/dvx021f52m" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ora.ox.ac.uk">[ORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ora.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 13, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2022</span></span></span>

Although the broad sequence of events is accepted by historians, the precise details depend almost entirely on narrative sources written after the conquest rather than administrative records or eyewitness censuses.

## What medieval chroniclers claimed

The debate over Merv's death toll begins with the medieval historians themselves, whose figures differ dramatically.

The principal accounts include:

* **Ibn al-Athir**, writing in the early thirteenth century, described the massacre as one of the greatest disasters ever to strike the Islamic world. One tradition associated with his work gives approximately **700,000 deaths**, though manuscript traditions differ and some later summaries report smaller totals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A78c64807-5c43-43d9-999b-06ed6943d35b/files/dvx021f52m" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ora.ox.ac.uk">[ORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ora.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 13, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2022</span></span></span>
* **Ata-Malik Juvayni**, writing several decades later while serving the Mongol Ilkhanate, presented an even larger figure. He related that a religious scholar supposedly spent thirteen days counting visible bodies and arrived at **more than 1.3 million dead**, excluding corpses hidden in buildings, villages and surrounding countryside. He also repeated the claim that each Mongol soldier had been assigned several hundred victims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merv" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

These extraordinary numbers became famous because later historians repeatedly copied them, allowing the story of Merv to become a symbol of Mongol destruction throughout the medieval Islamic world.

Modern scholarship, however, treats these totals with considerable caution. Medieval chroniclers often employed immense casualty figures to convey the moral and emotional scale of catastrophe rather than to provide statistically accurate counts. Similar million-plus totals also appear in descriptions of other Mongol conquests, including Nishapur and Herat, where historians likewise regard the reported figures as implausible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasion_of_the_Khwarazmian_Empire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mongol invasion of the Khwarazmian Empire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mongol invasion of the Khwarazmian Empire</span></span></span>

## Why the numbers are disputed

The uncertainty exists because no reliable census of Merv survives from immediately before the conquest, and medieval chroniclers had no practical means of counting casualties on the scale they described.

Several problems complicate the estimates.

First, historians do not even agree on Merv's pre-conquest population. Estimates vary widely, with many modern scholars suggesting somewhere between roughly **150,000 and 500,000 inhabitants** at its greatest extent, depending on whether surrounding suburbs and oasis settlements are included. Refugees fleeing earlier Mongol victories probably increased the population temporarily, but by an unknown amount.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A78c64807-5c43-43d9-999b-06ed6943d35b/files/dvx021f52m" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ora.ox.ac.uk">[ORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ora.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 13, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2022</span></span></span>

Second, the logistics of the medieval claims are difficult to reconcile with the size of the Mongol army. Juvayni's statement that every soldier executed several hundred people would require assumptions about troop numbers, prisoner control and available time that many military historians consider unrealistic. Modern estimates of the invading force are substantially smaller than those found in medieval narratives, making the arithmetic increasingly difficult to sustain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Merv_%281221%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Siege of Merv (1221</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Siege of Merv (1221</span></span></span>

Third, medieval casualty figures often mixed together:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* permanent residents;
* refugees from other cities;
* people living in surrounding villages;
* soldiers killed during the siege; and
* victims of subsequent famine, displacement or later reprisals.

</div>

Because chroniclers rarely distinguished between these groups, later readers sometimes interpreted rhetorical totals as precise demographic statistics.

## What archaeology can and cannot show

Archaeology confirms that Merv suffered a profound rupture in 1221, but it cannot determine how many people died.

Excavations have revealed evidence for destruction and later rebuilding of fortifications, while also demonstrating that the city's political and economic importance never fully recovered after the Mongol conquest. The archaeological record therefore supports the historical conclusion that the invasion represented a genuine turning point in Merv's history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merv" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

What archaeology has not uncovered is a mass burial or body count capable of confirming the medieval figures. This absence is not surprising. Bodies may have remained exposed, been buried in numerous locations, or been removed during centuries of later occupation and rebuilding. Equally, much of the surrounding oasis remains only partially investigated.

As a result, archaeology can verify large-scale devastation but cannot distinguish between casualties numbering in the hundreds of thousands and those claimed in the millions.

## The most likely historical interpretation

Today, most historians reject the precise medieval totals without dismissing the horror they were intended to express.

The strongest evidence supports several conclusions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A massacre unquestionably occurred after Merv surrendered.
* Most of the civilian population appears to have been killed or dispersed, with artisans selectively spared for deportation.
* Refugees from surrounding regions probably made the city's population larger than usual.
* Medieval figures of 700,000 to over 1.3 million deaths almost certainly exceed what the city's population could realistically have supported, even allowing for refugees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A78c64807-5c43-43d9-999b-06ed6943d35b/files/dvx021f52m" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ora.ox.ac.uk">[ORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ora.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 13, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2022</span></span></span>

</div>

Rather than treating the chronicles as statistical reports, historians generally read them as testimony to an event so traumatic that contemporaries struggled to express its scale except through enormous numbers.

## Why the mystery still matters

The disputed death toll at Merv illustrates a wider problem in medieval history: catastrophic events are often remembered more clearly than they can be measured.

For Turkmenistan, Merv remains one of the country's most important archaeological landscapes and a reminder of both the splendour of the medieval Silk Road and the violence that reshaped Central Asia in the thirteenth century. The enduring debate is therefore not about whether one of history's great massacres occurred—it did—but about how historians should weigh literary testimony, demographic possibility and archaeological evidence when the surviving sources were written to convey overwhelming devastation rather than produce modern-style statistics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A78c64807-5c43-43d9-999b-06ed6943d35b/files/dvx021f52m" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ora.ox.ac.uk">[ORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ora.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 13, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2022</span></span></span>
