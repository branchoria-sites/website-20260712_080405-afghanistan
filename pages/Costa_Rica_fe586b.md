## Why were the Diquís stone spheres made?

Hundreds of carved stone spheres have been recorded in southern Costa Rica, particularly in the Diquís Delta. They range from small objects to examples more than two and a half metres across. Four archaeological settlements containing spheres in or near their original positions — Finca 6, Batambal, El Silencio and Grijalba-2 — were added to UNESCO’s World Heritage List in 2014. Archaeological evidence places the settlements within complex, hierarchical societies that flourished between roughly AD 500 and 1500.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1453/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentrePrecolumbian Chiefdom Settlements with Stone Spheres of...The property includes four archaeological sites lo...</span></span></span>

The central mystery is not who made the spheres. Their association with Indigenous settlements, mounds, cemeteries and paved areas firmly places them within Costa Rica’s pre-Columbian past. Nor is their manufacture inexplicable. The makers could shape suitable boulders by repeated hammering, controlled heating and grinding or polishing with abrasive material. The real unanswered questions concern meaning: why communities invested so much labour in them, why spheres of different sizes were placed in particular arrangements, and what social or ritual messages those arrangements conveyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museocostarica.go.cr/wp-content/uploads/Investigaciones/Arqueolog%C3%ADa/Diquis/Articulos-descarga/asentamientos-precolombinos-con-esferas-2015.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museocostarica.go.cr">[go.cr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museocostarica.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El Delta del Diquís, sureste de Costa Rica, destaca como la zona con el mayor reporte de esferas de piedra, asociadas a extensos y comple...</span></span></span>

The best-supported interpretation is that the spheres helped express authority and organise important spaces. Some remained aligned near raised residential areas or approaches to elite structures, suggesting that they marked status, controlled movement or formed part of ceremonial landscapes. UNESCO describes the associated settlements as physical evidence of societies divided into different levels of political and social power. That does not prove a single function: an object could simultaneously identify an elite household, define a public route and carry religious meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/document/152714" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreStone Spheres of the Diquís (Costa Rica) No 1453August 2, 2016 — 6 Mar 2014 — The general state of authentici...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 2, 2016</span></span></span>

Claims that the spheres were left by Atlantis, made with unknown technology or arranged as a perfect planetary map have no comparable archaeological support. Their apparent precision has often been exaggerated, and many spheres were removed from their original locations during plantation development, landscaping and collecting. Once an object is moved, its relationship to buildings, burials and neighbouring objects is lost, making confident interpretations much harder. The exceptional value of the protected Diquís sites is therefore not merely that they contain spheres, but that some still survive within readable archaeological settings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/document/152714" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreStone Spheres of the Diquís (Costa Rica) No 1453August 2, 2016 — 6 Mar 2014 — The general state of authentici...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 2, 2016</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery is consequently genuine but restrained. Archaeologists understand the spheres as Indigenous monuments created by organised communities with skilled craftspeople and powerful leaders. What cannot yet be reconstructed is the makers’ own explanation of them. No surviving text tells us what a sphere represented, what ceremonies took place around it or whether its meaning changed over the centuries.

## Why was Guayabo abandoned?

On the forested slopes near Turrialba lie the remains of Guayabo, Costa Rica’s largest protected archaeological complex. The site includes circular building foundations, stone-paved routes, retaining walls, tombs, reservoirs and covered and open water channels. Its infrastructure indicates sustained communal labour and sophisticated management rather than an isolated village. Costa Rica’s National System of Conservation Areas describes Guayabo as the country’s most important preserved site of archaeological structures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sinac.go.cr/EN-US/ac/accvc/gnm/Pages/default.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sinac.go.cr">[SINAC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sinac.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on go.cr.</span></span></span>

Occupation in the wider area extended across many centuries, while Guayabo’s main period of importance preceded the Spanish arrival. The settlement appears to have declined or been abandoned by around AD 1400. Because this happened before Europeans established detailed records in Costa Rica, no colonial observer described the event and no known local written account survives. Even the name by which the inhabitants called themselves is unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sinac.go.cr/EN-US/ac/accvc/gnm/Pages/default.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sinac.go.cr">[SINAC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sinac.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on go.cr.</span></span></span>

Several explanations are possible, but none has been demonstrated. Epidemic disease, conflict between neighbouring groups, political breakdown, environmental pressure and changes in trade have all been suggested. These mechanisms are historically plausible in general, yet plausibility is not evidence that a particular one emptied Guayabo. A gradual shift of population would also leave a different archaeological signature from sudden flight, and the visible ceremonial centre represents only part of the wider inhabited landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sinac.go.cr/EN-US/ac/accvc/gnm/Pages/default.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sinac.go.cr">[SINAC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sinac.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on go.cr.</span></span></span>

The mystery is sometimes overstated by describing Guayabo as a city whose entire population suddenly “vanished”. Archaeology does not establish a single dramatic disappearance. Wooden buildings decayed, much of the surrounding landscape remains incompletely investigated, and people may have moved into smaller or differently organised settlements rather than ceased to exist. The unanswered question is therefore not where an entire civilisation went, but why a prominent centre lost its political, ceremonial or economic role.

Guayabo is especially important because its silence challenges an old misconception that Costa Rica possessed little complex Indigenous history before European settlement. Its roads, mounds and water system show planning and authority on a substantial scale. The mystery of its abandonment survives partly because the society that built it is visible through engineering but largely voiceless in the surviving record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sinac.go.cr/EN-US/ac/accvc/gnm/Pages/default.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sinac.go.cr">[SINAC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sinac.go.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on go.cr.</span></span></span>

## Is the Cocos Island treasure real?

Cocos Island lies far out in the Pacific and is now protected primarily for its exceptional natural environment. Its remoteness, steep terrain, abundant caves and history as a stopping place for sailors made it an ideal setting for stories of buried pirate wealth. The best-known story concerns the Treasure of Lima, supposedly stolen during the wars of independence in Spanish South America and hidden on the island by a sea captain commonly named William Thompson.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/where-to-go/protected-areas/cocos-island-national-park" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitcostarica.com">[Visit Costa Rica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitcostarica.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cocos island national park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cocos island national park</span></span></span>

In the familiar version, officials in Lima placed church valuables aboard Thompson’s vessel in 1820 for removal to safety. He and his crew murdered their guards, sailed to Cocos Island and buried the cargo. The ship was then captured; Thompson escaped execution by promising to reveal the hiding place, but fled after returning to the island. Later searchers supposedly relied on maps, deathbed confessions or information passed through intermediaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_of_Lima" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Treasure of Lima</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Treasure of Lima</span></span></span>

The story’s difficulty is that its colourful details are far better known than its documentary foundation. Versions differ over the ship, the cargo, the people involved and even the island on which the treasure was hidden. Nineteenth-century reports linked Cocos Island to other alleged pirate hoards, while the fully developed Lima narrative became prominent later. Repetition has often been treated as corroboration, although many accounts appear to descend from earlier treasure tales rather than independent records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_of_Lima" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Treasure of Lima</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Treasure of Lima</span></span></span>

Treasure hunting itself is well documented. The most famous long-term searcher, August Gissler, lived on Cocos Island for years around the turn of the twentieth century and found a small number of coins, but nothing resembling the legendary hoard. Numerous later expeditions likewise failed to produce the supposed church treasure. A few coins on an island visited by ships do not establish the existence of a vast buried cargo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_of_Lima" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Treasure of Lima</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Treasure of Lima</span></span></span>

Three possibilities remain reasonable. A real but smaller cache may once have been concealed and later recovered. Several unrelated episodes of piracy and buried property may have merged into one grand story. Alternatively, the Treasure of Lima may be largely literary folklore given credibility by Cocos Island’s real maritime history and difficult landscape. The surviving evidence does not justify claims that the treasure certainly exists, still less modern valuations running into hundreds of millions.

The legend has nevertheless had real consequences. Expeditions altered the island, treasure accounts shaped its international image, and searches competed with what is now a conservation priority. In 1997 UNESCO recognised Cocos Island as a World Heritage Site for its ecosystems and marine life, not for an authenticated treasure deposit. The historical puzzle is less “where is the gold?” than “how did a weakly documented story become powerful enough to send generations of people digging for it?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitcostarica.com/where-to-go/protected-areas/cocos-island-national-park" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitcostarica.com">[Visit Costa Rica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitcostarica.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cocos island national park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cocos island national park</span></span></span>

## What remains unresolved about Codo del Diablo?

On 19 December 1948, six political prisoners were killed while being transferred from Limón towards San José. The victims — Federico Picado Sáenz, Tobías Vaglio Sardi, Lucio Ibarra, Octavio Sáenz Soto, Narciso Sotomayor and Álvaro Aguilar — were associated with the defeated communist and Calderón-aligned side of Costa Rica’s recent civil war. They were taken to a bend in the railway near the Reventazón River known as Codo del Diablo, shot and thrown into the water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/158266" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

This is not a conventional unidentified-killer mystery. Historical accounts name members of the security forces as the direct participants, and legal action was initiated. The deeper questions concern command responsibility, political protection and impunity. Were the killings an unauthorised act of local revenge, part of a wider effort to eliminate opposition figures, or something encouraged by people with influence in the victorious post-war administration? Why were suspects able to escape, and why did the case never produce a lasting judicial reckoning?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codo_del_Diablo_murders" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Codo del Diablo murders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Codo del Diablo murders</span></span></span>

The timing is crucial. The civil war had formally ended, but Costa Rica remained politically fractured. The new authorities were consolidating power, communists faced repression, and memories of wartime violence were still immediate. Historian Eduardo Miller identifies Codo del Diablo as one of the best-known sites of death during the conflict and its aftermath. Other Costa Rican scholarship has treated the murders not as an isolated crime but as part of the punitive political climate that followed the war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/158266" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

Firm claims that a particular national leader personally ordered the executions require stronger evidence than is publicly available. Political responsibility can exist without a surviving written order, but historians must distinguish suspicion, testimony and institutional context from proof. The well-established core is that prisoners in state custody were deliberately killed by men connected to the victorious order, and that the perpetrators did not face effective punishment.

Codo del Diablo complicates Costa Rica’s familiar national story. The abolition of the armed forces, the 1949 constitution and later democratic stability are central to the country’s identity, but they did not erase the violence surrounding the birth of the new political system. The case remained comparatively marginal in public memory until scholarship, commemorations and a 2014 documentary renewed attention to the victims and their families.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://surcosdigital.com/tag/el-codo-del-diablo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: surcosdigital.com">[surcosdigital.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">surcosdigital.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on surcosdigital.com.</span></span></span>

Its unresolved element is therefore both historical and moral. Researchers may eventually clarify who assisted the killers or what senior officials knew, but missing records and the passage of time limit what can be proved. The more certain mystery is how an event involving named victims, identifiable suspects and state custody could be remembered for decades without producing full accountability.

## What these mysteries reveal about Costa Rica

Costa Rica’s major historical enigmas do not all have the same evidential status. The Diquís spheres and Guayabo are archaeological puzzles created by the absence of Indigenous written explanations and by the destruction or incompleteness of physical context. The Cocos Island treasure is primarily a documentary and folkloric problem: there is ample evidence for the hunt, but much weaker evidence for the fabulous object being hunted. Codo del Diablo concerns neither lost technology nor buried wealth, but the limits of archives and justice after political violence.

Keeping those distinctions clear prevents mystery from becoming misinformation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Documented fact:** Indigenous societies created the Diquís spheres and built the monumental settlements around them.
* **Reasonable interpretation:** The spheres probably communicated rank, identity, ritual meaning or control of important space.
* **Open archaeological question:** No single explanation has yet established why Guayabo declined.
* **Legend with historical ingredients:** Cocos Island attracted sailors and treasure seekers, but the standard Treasure of Lima narrative lacks firm contemporary verification.
* **Established crime with disputed responsibility:** Six prisoners were killed at Codo del Diablo, while the extent of higher-level direction or protection remains unresolved.

</div>

Together, the cases show that uncertainty is most valuable when its boundaries are respected. Costa Rica’s past does not need invented ancient visitors, guaranteed pirate gold or an unsupported master conspiracy to be intriguing. Its genuine mysteries arise from the difficult work of recovering Indigenous meaning, separating maritime folklore from records and confronting episodes that sit uneasily beside the country’s preferred national memory.
