## Introduction

The Inca Empire ruled a vast stretch of the Andes without leaving behind an alphabetic archive of its own. Instead, officials relied on **khipus**: carefully organised bundles of coloured cords whose knots, twists and fibres stored information. The numerical side of this system is well understood. Scholars can read many khipus as decimal accounting records recording taxes, labour obligations, storehouses and censuses. The enduring mystery is whether some khipus also encoded **names, stories, laws or spoken language**. If they did, they would represent one of the world's few independent traditions of writing—or at least a unique information system unlike any known script. Despite decades of research, no complete decipherment has been achieved, making the search for a "lost script" one of Peru's most important unresolved historical puzzles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/news/2005/050808/full/news050808-11.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scientists untangle Inca number-strings: Nature News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientists untangle Inca number-strings: Nature NewsAugust 11, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 11, 2005</span></span></span>

## How khipus recorded numbers

The strongest evidence concerns administration rather than literature. An official khipu usually consisted of a main cord from which hung many pendant cords, each distinguished by combinations of colour, fibre, spinning direction and knot type.

Researchers have established that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Different knot forms represented different numerical values.
* The position of knots along a cord followed a decimal place-value system, with units, tens, hundreds and higher values occupying different levels.
* Groups of cords could summarise inventories, tribute, population counts and labour obligations across the empire.

</div>

This interpretation is supported by surviving colonial records describing Inca accountants, known as khipu specialists, and by comparisons between archaeological khipus and early Spanish census documents. Several khipus have even been shown to contain related mathematical totals, demonstrating that the numerical code is systematic rather than symbolic guesswork.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/news/2005/050808/full/news050808-11.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scientists untangle Inca number-strings: Nature News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientists untangle Inca number-strings: Nature NewsAugust 11, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 11, 2005</span></span></span>

The numerical system therefore is no longer regarded as mysterious. The unanswered question begins where the numbers end.

## Could khipus also record names and messages?

Early Spanish chroniclers repeatedly claimed that khipus preserved much more than accounts. They described officials consulting them to recall royal genealogies, historical events, legal decisions and even messages carried across the empire.

For many years historians debated whether these reports reflected literal reading or whether trained specialists simply used khipus as memory aids. Modern research has complicated that picture.

One important clue is that khipus contain far more variation than accounting alone appears to require. Besides knots, they incorporate combinations of:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* cord colour;
* cotton or camelid fibres;
* spinning and twisting direction;
* methods of attachment;
* branching structures; and
* occasional attached objects.

</div>

Gary Urton argued that these binary and multi-state features dramatically increased the amount of information available beyond simple numbers. While this does not prove the presence of writing, it suggests the system possessed far greater expressive capacity than once assumed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/9d33330d-3b2b-4fec-a8f1-91c310ba2034" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dash.harvard.edu">[Harvard Dash]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dash.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harvard DashToward a Grammar of the Inka Khipu: Investigating the Production of Non-numerical SignsMay 16, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 16, 2019</span></span></span>

More provocative evidence emerged from two ceremonial khipus preserved by local authorities in the Peruvian community of San Juan de Collata. Anthropologist Sabine Hyland argued that these cords were not accounting devices but narrative messages concerning warfare and political alliances. She identified around 95 distinct combinations of colour, fibre and construction, enough to suggest a complex sign system. Hyland further proposed that some cord combinations represented specific lineage names rather than numbers alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/691682" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.uchicago.edu">[Chicago Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uchicago.edu.</span></span></span>

If this interpretation is correct, at least some khipus functioned as genuine written communication rather than accounting tools.

## Why full decipherment remains elusive

The attraction of the mystery lies partly in how close researchers seem to be—and yet how many obstacles remain.

The greatest difficulty is the loss of context after the Spanish conquest. Thousands of khipus were probably destroyed because colonial officials associated them with indigenous administration and religion. Without accompanying texts explaining their contents, modern researchers possess only a fraction of the original archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/lac/khipu-database-khipu-archives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Khipu Database (Khipu Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Khipu Database (Khipu Archives) - Memory of the World - Latin America and the Caribbean...</span></span></span>

Several additional problems complicate decipherment.

**Too few surviving examples.** Around a thousand Inca and colonial-era khipus are known today, a tiny sample compared with the numbers once used across the empire. Many are fragmentary or lack archaeological context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/lac/khipu-database-khipu-archives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Khipu Database (Khipu Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Khipu Database (Khipu Archives) - Memory of the World - Latin America and the Caribbean...</span></span></span>

**No bilingual "Rosetta Stone".** Egyptian hieroglyphs became readable because the Rosetta Stone carried the same text in multiple scripts. No equivalent document pairing a complete khipu with an exact written translation has ever been found.

**Mixed purposes.** Many surviving examples are clearly numerical, while others may have combined accounting with non-numerical information. This makes it difficult to assume that every khipu followed identical rules.

**Unknown linguistic structure.** Even if names or spoken language were encoded, scholars still debate whether the system represented sounds, words, ideas or some entirely different method of recording information. It may not fit modern definitions of alphabetic writing at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/9d33330d-3b2b-4fec-a8f1-91c310ba2034" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dash.harvard.edu">[Harvard Dash]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dash.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harvard DashToward a Grammar of the Inka Khipu: Investigating the Production of Non-numerical SignsMay 16, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 16, 2019</span></span></span>

## What competing explanations exist?

The evidence supports several interpretations, none of which commands universal agreement.

**Primarily accounting records.** This remains the least controversial position. Nearly all specialists agree that many surviving khipus recorded numerical administration with remarkable efficiency.

**Memory aids for trained experts.** Some researchers argue that non-numerical information existed mainly in the minds of specialists, with cords acting as prompts rather than independent texts.

**A partial writing system.** Others believe certain khipus encoded names, places or fixed expressions without representing every spoken sentence.

**A full script.** The boldest interpretation proposes that at least some khipus recorded language in a logosyllabic or similarly structured system capable of preserving extended messages. Hyland's Collata research is among the strongest recent arguments for this possibility, but it has not yet achieved broad scholarly consensus or produced a general decipherment applicable to other collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/691682" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.uchicago.edu">[uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uchicago.edu.</span></span></span>

Rather than choosing between "writing" and "not writing", many scholars now suggest that khipus occupied a distinctive middle ground: a sophisticated recording technology unlike alphabetic scripts but capable of storing more than numerical accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/5037552" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Open Khipu Repository</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Open Khipu Repository...</span></span></span>

## How modern research is changing the search

The search for a lost script has become increasingly data-driven.

Large digital catalogues now record every measurable feature of surviving khipus, allowing researchers to compare thousands of cords across museums worldwide. Harvard's Khipu Database and the Open Khipu Repository have transformed what was once scattered documentation into searchable datasets suitable for statistical analysis and computational modelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/lac/khipu-database-khipu-archives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Khipu Database (Khipu Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Khipu Database (Khipu Archives) - Memory of the World - Latin America and the Caribbean...</span></span></span>

Instead of searching immediately for hidden words, researchers increasingly look for recurring structural patterns, relationships between archaeological sites and links to colonial census records. Successful matches between particular khipus and documented communities offer the best hope of eventually identifying recurring names, places or administrative conventions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/5037552" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Open Khipu Repository</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Open Khipu Repository...</span></span></span>

## The unresolved legacy of Inca Khipus
The importance of khipus extends beyond whether they qualify as "writing".

If scholars eventually decipher substantial non-numerical content, historians could gain direct access to Inca voices rather than relying almost entirely on Spanish descriptions written after the conquest. Royal history, local administration, social organisation and indigenous perspectives might become far clearer.

Even if no complete script is ever recovered, khipus have already transformed understanding of the Inca state. They demonstrate that one of the world's largest pre-modern empires managed taxation, labour and resources through a sophisticated information system built from cords instead of ink and paper.

The mystery therefore is not simply whether a secret code survives. It asks whether an entire way of recording knowledge—one developed independently in the Andes—can still be understood after centuries of disruption, loss and silence.
