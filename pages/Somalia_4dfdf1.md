## Was Somalia part of the Land of Punt?

The [Land of Punt]({{ 'land-of-punt/' | relative_url }}) is the best-known ancient mystery associated with Somalia. Egyptian records describe royal expeditions to a distant trading country that supplied incense, ebony, ivory, gold, exotic animals and other valued goods. The most famous account appears in reliefs commissioned by the Egyptian ruler Hatshepsut in the fifteenth century BCE. They portray a maritime expedition, local buildings, trees and inhabitants, but provide no map precise enough to identify Punt with a modern state.

Most scholars place Punt somewhere around the southern Red Sea and the Horn of Africa. Suggested territories have included parts of present-day Sudan, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Djibouti, Somalia and Yemen. The goods shown in Egyptian records strongly favour an African connection, but they do not establish that Punt was confined to one country. It may have been a broad trading region, a network of ports or a name applied by Egyptians to changing commercial partners over many centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33319742/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eritrea, Djibouti, Somalia and Yemen – areas already highlighted</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mummified baboons reveal the far reach of early Egyptian...by NJ Dominy · 2020 · Cited by 42 — the Land of Punt was one of Egypt&#x27;s...</span></span></span>

Somalia remains a plausible part of that landscape. Its northern coast lies on the sea routes connecting Egypt, Arabia and the Indian Ocean, and the region has long produced aromatic resins. Ancient ports later documented by Greek and Roman writers also show that coastal communities were deeply involved in international commerce. Somalia’s Puntland region takes its modern name from the ancient country, although the name itself is not archaeological evidence that Punt lay there.

Scientific analysis of mummified baboons has narrowed the possibilities without solving the problem. A 2020 study compared chemical signatures preserved in baboon remains with the geography of modern populations. Its estimated source area extended across portions of Eritrea, Ethiopia and north-western Somalia. Later genetic work on a different specimen strengthened the case for a connection with the Eritrean coast and the ancient port of Adulis. These studies identify possible supply zones for particular animals; they do not prove that every place called Punt was located in the same zone, or that the animals were shipped directly from where they were born.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33319742/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eritrea, Djibouti, Somalia and Yemen – areas already highlighted</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mummified baboons reveal the far reach of early Egyptian...by NJ Dominy · 2020 · Cited by 42 — the Land of Punt was one of Egypt&#x27;s...</span></span></span>

The most reasonable verdict is that Somalia may have formed part of Punt’s commercial world, especially if Punt was a network rather than a compact kingdom. Claims that the mystery has been decisively solved in favour of Somalia, Eritrea or any other single modern country go beyond the evidence.

## Is Hafun really the lost port of Opone?

A first-century maritime guide known as the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* described a chain of trading ports along the Somali coast. Among them was Opone, an important market connected to the trade in spices, incense, tortoiseshell and other goods moving between north-eastern Africa, Arabia, the Mediterranean and India. Modern scholars usually identify Opone with the Hafun peninsula in north-eastern Somalia, but the match is strong rather than absolute.

The geography fits well. Hafun projects into the Indian Ocean near a natural turning point in long-distance sea routes. Archaeological surveys have also recovered imported ceramics and other material demonstrating contact with Roman, Arabian, Persian and wider Indian Ocean trading systems. Researchers studying early ports in the Horn have described remains at Hafun dating around the late first millennium BCE and early centuries CE, broadly consistent with the commercial world portrayed in the *Periplus*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230295059_Early_ports_in_the_Horn_of_Africa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Early ports in the Horn of AfricaEarly 1st-millennium remains of a port at Hafun West (Somalia) on a sandstone outcrop connec...</span></span></span>

Yet the surviving ruins do not resemble the complete remains of a great classical city. Erosion has damaged parts of the peninsula, occupation shifted between nearby areas, and excavation has been limited. Archaeological work has identified burials, imported wares and traces of settlement, but comparatively few substantial buildings at the main site. Even Neville Chittick, whose surveys transformed knowledge of the Somali coast, warned that many places named in the *Periplus* could not be identified with certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shs.hal.science/halshs-03483948/file/Prioletta%2520et%2520al%2520AAE%2520Saba-Somalia_Submitted%2520version.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shs.hal.science">[SHS Hal Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shs.hal.science</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prioletta%20et%20al%20AAE%20Saba Somalia Submitted%20version</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prioletta%20et%20al%20AAE%20Saba Somalia Submitted%20version</span></span></span>

Hafun may also preserve several different historical layers that are too easily combined into one story. A settlement operating before the *Periplus* was written need not have occupied exactly the same ground as the later port. Traders could have used a cluster of anchorages, markets and seasonal camps around the peninsula rather than one densely built city.

The identification of Hafun with Opone is therefore highly persuasive, but the detailed shape of the port remains mysterious. Archaeologists still need to establish where its principal harbour and commercial centre stood, how large its permanent population was and whether the settlement moved as coastlines, trade patterns and political authority changed.

## Who painted Laas Geel?

Near Hargeisa, in territory administered by Somaliland but internationally recognised as part of Somalia, the shelters of Laas Geel contain some of the Horn of Africa’s finest surviving rock art. Local communities had long known the site, but it entered wider archaeological literature after a French-led survey documented it in 2002. Roughly twenty shelters contain painted people, wild animals and striking long-horned cattle, some shown with decorated necks and bodies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/en/laas-geel-african-lascaux" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[gouv.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gouv.fr.</span></span></span>

The paintings clearly came from communities for whom cattle had economic and perhaps ceremonial importance. Human figures stand near or beneath the animals, suggesting herding, ritual activity or both. Their vivid condition creates an illusion of familiarity, yet almost everything beyond the images remains uncertain. The painters left no known written explanation, and archaeologists cannot confidently attach them to a named people.

Dating is particularly difficult. Published estimates often place the art in the third millennium BCE, and pigment fragments found in archaeological layers have been associated with a period around 3500–2500 BCE. However, those fragments do not directly date each painting. Only limited excavation has taken place, and the key shelter produced little associated material that could fix a secure chronology. As archaeologist Sada Mire has emphasised, the dating problem common to rock art remains especially important at Laas Geel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10437" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on springer.com.</span></span></span>

Interpretations of the decorated cattle must also remain cautious. Their elaborate markings might represent ceremonial coverings, symbolic designs, natural coat patterns or artistic conventions. Descriptions that present them as proof of a specific religion or vanished royal culture are speculative. Comparisons with other rock-art sites in Somaliland and the wider Horn reveal related themes, but not a decipherable system of symbols.

Laas Geel’s real mystery is therefore one of identity and meaning, not authenticity. The art is unquestionably ancient and locally rooted. What remains unknown is who made it, precisely when they worked and what relationship the paintings had to ceremony, social status, memory and everyday pastoral life.

## Who founded medieval Mogadishu?

Mogadishu became one of the great commercial cities of the western Indian Ocean, linking the East African interior with Arabia, Persia and lands farther east. Medieval visitors encountered a wealthy urban centre with mosques, stone buildings, specialised crafts and maritime trade. Its earliest development, however, is difficult to reconstruct.

One influential tradition held that Arab or Persian settlers founded the city and governed it through immigrant families. Versions of this narrative appeared in genealogies, later chronicles and colonial-era scholarship. They fitted a once-common assumption that complex African coastal towns must have been created by outsiders.

Archaeological and historical research has challenged that simple foundation story. Abdullahi Duale Jama’s study of Mogadishu combined excavated material, oral accounts and written sources to trace urban development from about the eleventh century onwards. The broader evidence indicates interaction between established African communities, migrants, merchants and Islamic networks rather than the sudden creation of a city on previously empty ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/5689/1/The%20Origins%20and%20Development%20of%20Mogadishu%20AD%201000%20to%201850.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it">[ArcAdiA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Origins and Development of Mogadishu AD 1000 to 1850</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Origins and Development of Mogadishu AD 1000 to 1850</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that later political and family traditions often project themselves backwards. Claims of prestigious Arabian or Persian descent could legitimise status without serving as literal accounts of a city’s birth. Conversely, rejecting an immigrant foundation myth does not mean foreign settlers were unimportant. Merchants and religious figures from across the Indian Ocean plainly contributed to Mogadishu’s language, architecture, faith and commercial life.

The surviving evidence supports a gradual, mixed process: local settlement and regional exchange developed into an Islamic trading city whose population absorbed newcomers. What cannot yet be reconstructed with confidence is the sequence by which neighbourhoods formed, ruling institutions emerged and different communities negotiated authority. Mogadishu’s founding is not best understood as a contest between an entirely foreign city and an entirely isolated local one. Its mystery lies in how several populations became one urban society.

## What did the looted inscriptions reveal?

One of Somalia’s most revealing archaeological controversies began not with a planned excavation but with illegal digging. In 2019, monumental inscriptions in the ancient South Arabian Sabaic script emerged from a looted sanctuary on the Somali coast. Scholars dated them to roughly the eighth or seventh century BCE, making them important evidence of early contact between communities on opposite sides of the Gulf of Aden.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/aae.12202" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Sabaeans on the Somali coast</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Online Library Sabaeans on the Somali coast</span></span></span>

The discovery matters because similarities between ancient southern Arabia and the Somali coast have often encouraged sweeping claims of migration, colonisation or lost kingdoms. The inscriptions provide firmer evidence: people familiar with Sabaic writing and religious practices were present or influential at the site. But the circumstances of discovery destroyed much of the context needed to interpret that presence.

Because the sanctuary was illicitly excavated, researchers could not observe the original position of every object, the relationship between inscriptions and architecture, or the sequence in which the site developed. Even a genuine inscription can answer only limited questions when removed from its archaeological layer. It may have been carved locally, brought from elsewhere, commissioned by visiting traders or dedicated by a small immigrant community.

The finds therefore demonstrate contact, not conquest. They add to evidence that the Somali coast participated in Red Sea networks centuries before the classical ports described by Greek writers. At the same time, looting converted a potentially rich historical site into a permanent puzzle. The missing context is part of the mystery and cannot be recreated merely by translating the surviving text.

## Where did Somalia’s museum collections and archives go?

Somalia’s most consequential historical mystery concerns material that disappeared within living memory. When the state collapsed and civil war spread through Mogadishu in 1991, libraries, archives, museums and cultural institutions were damaged, abandoned or looted. Somalia’s National Museum closed for nearly three decades. UNESCO states that its building was badly damaged, its staff dispersed and its national collections looted. When rehabilitation was completed, the restored museum initially remained largely empty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/culture-emergencies/heritage-emergency-fund/post-conflict-rehabilitation-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

There is no complete public inventory establishing the fate of every object. Some were probably destroyed during fighting or through neglect. Others may have been taken into private homes, sold locally or trafficked through international antiquities markets. Still others could survive without reliable documentation linking them to the museum. An object separated from its catalogue number and excavation history may become almost impossible to identify legally or academically.

Libraries and broadcasting archives suffered comparable losses. Research into the destruction of Somali libraries describes widespread burning, looting and dispersal during the civil war. Radio recordings were particularly valuable in a society where poetry, speeches, performance and oral testimony carried much of the historical record. A recent project at Radio Mogadishu has been preserving surviving material against the background of what the United Nations described as the near-total loss or destruction of many Somali cultural records and artefacts during decades of conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://repository.ifla.org/items/c96b0d3f-2b8a-4d16-9516-e96a539fe43c/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.ifla.org">[IFLA Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.ifla.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ifla.org.</span></span></span>

This uncertainty affects far more than museum display. Lost excavation notes may have recorded where artefacts were found. Missing photographs might document buildings before wartime destruction. Vanished sound recordings could contain testimony from political figures, poets and witnesses who have since died. The absence of these records makes earlier mysteries harder to solve and creates new disputes over ownership, authenticity and national memory.

Recovery is possible but slow. Somalia reopened its National Museum in 2020 and has worked with international organisations to rebuild professional capacity and strengthen protection against illicit trafficking. Yet identifying dispersed objects requires old photographs, inventories, witness accounts and cooperation from collectors and foreign institutions. Without a reliable pre-war catalogue, even determining the scale of the loss remains difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/somalia-seeks-strengthen-management-its-newly-rehabilitated-national-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">somalia seeks strengthen management its newly rehabilitated national museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Somalia seeks to strengthen management of its newly...27 Nov 2020 — The National Museum of Somalia was founded in 1933, however, d...</span></span></span>

## Why so many questions remain open

Somalia’s unresolved past reflects an unusual combination of research obstacles. Large areas have received little systematic excavation. Coastal erosion has damaged ancient trading sites. Nomadic and pastoral communities often preserved knowledge through landscape, practice and oral memory rather than monumental buildings. Colonial researchers sometimes interpreted African history through assumptions about foreign founders. After independence, promising archaeological work was repeatedly interrupted, and conflict after 1991 weakened institutions capable of protecting sites and records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.incipit.csic.es/es/produccion_cientifica/publicacion/articulo/against-all-odds-the-history-of-archaeological-research-in-somaliland-and-somalia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: incipit.csic.es">[csic.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">incipit.csic.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">maliland and Somalia from the late nineteenth century to the present, situatingRead more...</span></span></span>

Political geography adds another complication. Many celebrated sites, including Laas Geel, lie in Somaliland, which has governed itself since 1991 but is not internationally recognised as an independent state. That situation affects funding, international heritage agreements, access and the way discoveries are described. It should not be allowed to erase either the local authorities who protect the sites or their place within the wider archaeological history of Somali territories.

The surviving evidence nevertheless supports several firm conclusions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Somalia’s coast was connected to Red Sea and Indian Ocean trade far earlier than is often assumed.
* Hafun is a strong candidate for ancient Opone, though the port’s precise layout and development remain uncertain.
* Northern Somalia may have belonged to the commercial world called Punt, but no modern country can claim the identification as proven.
* Laas Geel records an ancient cattle-centred society whose name, chronology and beliefs remain only partly recoverable.
* Mogadishu developed through interaction between local communities and overseas networks, not through a single simple act of foreign foundation.
* War and looting have destroyed evidence on a scale that directly limits what historians can now know.

</div>

These are genuine mysteries because the available evidence permits several interpretations while ruling out others. They should neither be treated as solved national legends nor exaggerated into conspiracies. Somalia’s enigmatic history is most revealing where archaeology, memory and missing records meet: the unanswered questions show not an absence of history, but the scale and complexity of a past that has only begun to be reconstructed.
