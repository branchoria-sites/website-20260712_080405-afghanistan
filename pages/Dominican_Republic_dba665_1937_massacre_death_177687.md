## Introduction

The broad facts of the October 1937 massacre in the [Dominican Republic]({{ 'dominican-republic/' | relative_url }}) are no longer seriously disputed. Dominican soldiers, acting under the dictatorship of Rafael Trujillo, killed Haitians and many Dominicans of Haitian descent across the north-western borderlands over several days. The enduring mystery is not whether the killings occurred, but how many people died and who the victims were. Nearly ninety years later, historians still cannot produce a definitive death toll because many bodies were never recovered, official records were incomplete or deliberately suppressed, and entire border communities disappeared without leaving reliable documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/dh/article-abstract/20/3/301/427367" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicGenocide Next Door: The Good Neighbor Policy, the Trujillo Regime, and the Haitian Massacre of 1937 &#124; Diplomatic History &#124; Ox...</span></span></span>

The uncertainty is therefore a historical problem created by missing evidence rather than by doubt about the massacre itself. Modern scholarship has narrowed some questions while revealing why an exact count may never be possible.

## What contemporary reports establish about the killings

Evidence from 1937 leaves little doubt that a coordinated campaign of killing took place along the Dominican-Haitian frontier. Diplomatic correspondence, eyewitness testimony, later oral histories and government negotiations all confirm that large numbers of civilians were murdered during the first days of October. The United States, Haiti and other governments quickly recognised that a major international incident had occurred, even while disagreeing over the scale of the deaths.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/dh/article-abstract/20/3/301/427367" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicGenocide Next Door: The Good Neighbor Policy, the Trujillo Regime, and the Haitian Massacre of 1937 &#124; Diplomatic History &#124; Ox...</span></span></span>

What proved much harder to establish was the number of victims. Reports produced during the crisis varied dramatically:

* Early American consular reports suggested that around 1,000 people had been killed.
* Haitian diplomatic estimates soon rose to just over 2,000 identified victims.
* Later investigations by diplomats, journalists and historians produced figures ranging from roughly 10,000 to 20,000 or more.
* Some modern works cite estimates as high as 30,000 or 35,000, although these are generally treated as broad upper estimates rather than documented counts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319122107_LESSON_FROM_DOMINICAN_DICTATOR_RAFAEL_TRUJILLO" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">319122107 LESSON FROM DOMINICAN DICTATOR RAFAEL TRUJILLO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) LESSON FROM DOMINICAN DICTATOR RAFAEL TRUJILLOAugust 15, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2017</span></span></span>

Rather than reflecting simple disagreement, these changing figures illustrate how information emerged in stages. Early estimates relied on the victims who could immediately be identified, while later research attempted to account for entire missing communities.

## Why casualty estimates and victim identities differ

The greatest obstacle to determining the death toll is the nature of the border itself in 1937. Many residents lived in isolated rural settlements where births, deaths and migration were rarely recorded. Families crossed the frontier regularly for farming, trade and seasonal work, making it difficult even before the massacre to distinguish permanent residents from temporary migrants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/more-than-a-massacre/6CD7AA4DC466D44D23F3C9BB1CDE55B1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment More than a Massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMore than a Massacre...</span></span></span>

Several factors continue to complicate reconstruction.

**Many victims were never formally registered.** Large numbers of ethnic Haitians lacked official documentation despite having lived in the Dominican Republic for years or even generations. Some had been born there and were legally Dominican citizens under the constitutional rules then in force, yet appeared nowhere in comprehensive government registers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/content/oa_book_edited/j.ctvnp0k4x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Masacre de 1937. 80 años después: Reconstruyendo la memoria &#124; JSTOR...</span></span></span>

**Bodies often could not be counted.** Killings occurred at numerous scattered locations rather than a single massacre site. Some victims were buried hurriedly in mass graves, while others were left in rivers or remote countryside, making later recovery impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/more-than-a-massacre/6CD7AA4DC466D44D23F3C9BB1CDE55B1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment More than a Massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMore than a Massacre...</span></span></span>

**The dictatorship had little incentive to preserve evidence.** Trujillo's government initially denied responsibility, blamed local conflicts and sought to minimise the scale of the killings during international negotiations. Historians argue that this official response discouraged accurate documentation from the outset.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/dh/article-abstract/20/3/301/427367" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicGenocide Next Door: The Good Neighbor Policy, the Trujillo Regime, and the Haitian Massacre of 1937 &#124; Diplomatic History &#124; Ox...</span></span></span>

**Later estimates used different methods.** Some scholars relied heavily on diplomatic archives, others compared population records before and after 1937, while others incorporated survivor testimony and local oral history. Each approach measures slightly different things, helping explain why estimates remain inconsistent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/more-than-a-massacre/6CD7AA4DC466D44D23F3C9BB1CDE55B1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment More than a Massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMore than a Massacre...</span></span></span>

## Why historians no longer focus on a single number

Earlier discussions sometimes treated competing casualty figures as if only one could be correct. More recent historians tend to view the uncertainty itself as historically meaningful.

Many researchers regard approximately 10,000 to 20,000 deaths as the most defensible range based on surviving documentary evidence, while recognising that the true figure could be somewhat lower or higher because entire families vanished without leaving records. Eric Paul Roorda, for example, judged that around 12,000 deaths was a plausible estimate from the available evidence, whereas other recent studies favour figures closer to 15,000 or around 20,000 after incorporating additional archival and oral sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/dh/article-abstract/20/3/301/427367" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[oup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicGenocide Next Door: The Good Neighbor Policy, the Trujillo Regime, and the Haitian Massacre of 1937 &#124; Diplomatic History &#124; Ox...</span></span></span>

The debate therefore concerns evidence rather than the reality of the atrocity. Few serious historians now argue that only a few hundred people died, but neither can they verify the highest estimates with surviving documentation.

## Border communities, missing records and public memory

The uncertainty extends beyond numbers to identities. Thousands of victims have never been individually named. In many border districts, entire extended families disappeared, leaving descendants with fragmented memories rather than official death certificates or burial records. Oral testimony collected decades later has helped restore some personal histories, but it cannot reconstruct a complete list of victims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/more-than-a-massacre/6CD7AA4DC466D44D23F3C9BB1CDE55B1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment More than a Massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMore than a Massacre...</span></span></span>

This absence has shaped public memory in both the Dominican Republic and Haiti. Memorial efforts increasingly emphasise recovering names, family histories and local experiences rather than seeking a single definitive death toll. Historians argue that understanding who disappeared—and why so many cannot now be identified—is as important as resolving the numerical debate itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/more-than-a-massacre/6CD7AA4DC466D44D23F3C9BB1CDE55B1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment More than a Massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMore than a Massacre...</span></span></span>

The uncertain death toll remains one of the central historical puzzles of the 1937 massacre because the missing evidence reflects the violence itself. The scattered killing sites, weak civil registration in frontier communities, deliberate official obstruction and the disappearance of entire families mean that the exact number of victims is unlikely ever to be established. What is clear, however, is that the massacre claimed many thousands of civilian lives and left a permanent gap in both the historical record and the collective memory of the Dominican-Haitian border.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/more-than-a-massacre/6CD7AA4DC466D44D23F3C9BB1CDE55B1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment More than a Massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMore than a Massacre...</span></span></span>
