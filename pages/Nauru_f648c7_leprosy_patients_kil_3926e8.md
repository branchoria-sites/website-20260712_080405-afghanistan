## Introduction

The murder of [Nauru]({{ 'what-happened-in-naurus-lost-wartime/' | relative_url }})'s leprosy patients is one of the best documented yet still partly unresolved wartime atrocities in the island's history. In July 1943, during the Japanese occupation of Nauru, 39 Nauruans living with leprosy were removed from the Meneng leprosy settlement, placed aboard a small boat and taken out to sea. None returned. Australian post-war investigations established beyond reasonable doubt that they were deliberately killed, but important questions about the precise sequence of events, the involvement of individual participants and the extent of command responsibility have never been fully answered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

The case remains historically significant not because there is doubt that a massacre occurred, but because gaps in surviving testimony, the destruction of evidence and incomplete war-crimes proceedings have left historians reconstructing events from fragmentary statements made years later. It also illustrates how prejudice against people with leprosy shaped wartime decisions as much as military circumstances.

## What is firmly established about the 39 victims

Several core facts are supported by multiple independent sources and are no longer seriously disputed.

The victims were 39 Nauruans with leprosy—24 men and 15 women—living in the island's segregated leprosy settlement at Meneng. Contemporary records identify the youngest victim as an eleven-year-old boy and the oldest as a sixty-nine-year-old woman. An administrative list compiled during the occupation preserved all 39 names, making this one of the best documented groups of civilian victims from wartime Nauru.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[apjjf.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

The killings occurred in July 1943 after Japanese forces had occupied Nauru for almost a year. Earlier claims that the patients had been transferred safely to medical facilities elsewhere proved false during Australian investigations conducted after Japan's surrender. Witness testimony, military interrogations and surviving documentation all converged on the conclusion that the patients were intentionally murdered at sea rather than evacuated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

Unlike many wartime disappearances, this is therefore not a mystery about whether the victims survived. The unresolved questions concern exactly how the killings unfolded, who participated directly and how responsibility moved through the Japanese chain of command.

## Conflicting accounts of the voyage and sinking

The absence of survivors means that every reconstruction depends on later testimony, much of it given by Japanese military personnel or civilian employees under interrogation after the war. Those accounts agree on the broad outline but differ in important details.

Initially, Lieutenant Hiromi Nakayama claimed the patients had been transported to a hospital in the Marshall Islands aboard the *Shinshu Maru*. Australian investigators soon recognised that this explanation was false after questioning numerous witnesses and comparing their statements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

Subsequent testimony suggested a more detailed sequence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The patients were loaded into a smaller boat.
* That boat was towed offshore by the *Shinshu Maru*.
* Once Nauru could no longer be seen, the tow line was cut.
* The accompanying vessel then deliberately sank the patients' boat.
* Survivors in the water were reportedly shot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

</div>

Although this reconstruction is widely accepted, historians remain cautious because the evidence comes from statements made years later, sometimes by men attempting to minimise their own responsibility. Some witnesses emphasised gunfire from rifles, others referred to cannon fire, while earlier summaries simply described the victims as being sunk at sea. Since no bodies were recovered and no independent observer witnessed the entire operation, the exact mechanics of the killings cannot be verified beyond those testimonies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. The central historical uncertainty is not whether the massacre happened, but how confidently individual actions during the final stages of the voyage can be reconstructed.

## Who ordered the killings?

One of the most important findings of post-war investigations concerns command responsibility.

Evidence gathered by Australian investigators indicated that Lieutenant Hiromi Nakayama ordered the patients to be "disposed of" on 9 July 1943. Investigators concluded that he appears to have exploited an unusual administrative transition between commanders of the 67th Naval Guard Force. The outgoing commander had effectively ceased exercising authority, while the incoming commander had not yet arrived on Nauru.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

Later testimony from Captain Soeda Hisayuki stated that he knew nothing about the operation before taking command. Other evidence similarly failed to show that either his predecessor or higher headquarters explicitly authorised the massacre. Because Nakayama had already been executed for another war crime before these later investigations were completed, Australian investigators could never test his account against newer evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

Most historians therefore regard Nakayama as the principal decision-maker while acknowledging that uncertainty remains over how much other officers knew in advance and whether the crime reflected broader institutional approval or an individual initiative.

## Why were the patients targeted?

The explanation offered by those involved combined military reasoning with deep prejudice against people with leprosy.

Japanese officers later argued that increasing Allied air raids might damage the leprosy settlement, allowing patients to escape and spread disease among the civilian population. Modern historians note that this reflected contemporary misconceptions about leprosy and Japan's own harsh segregation policies rather than genuine medical necessity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

Research into Japan's twentieth-century treatment of Hansen's disease shows that compulsory isolation, severe discrimination and denial of patients' rights were official policy for decades. The massacre on Nauru therefore occurred within an existing ideological framework that regarded people with leprosy as a threat to society rather than patients deserving protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mhlw.go.jp/topics/bukyoku/kenkou/hansen/kanren/dl/4e2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mhlw.go.jp">[Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mhlw.go.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on go.jp.</span></span></span>

This broader context helps explain why the victims could be singled out, but it does not eliminate the remaining questions about the operational decision-making on Nauru itself.

## Trials, convictions and the limits of justice

Justice for the massacre was incomplete.

Nakayama was tried by an Australian Military Court in 1946 and executed, but that trial concerned the murder of five Australian civilians on Nauru rather than the killing of the leprosy patients. By the time investigations into the massacre had developed fully, he was already dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

Australian investigators nevertheless pursued other participants. In 1948, Tadae Sakata and Tamotsu Nakamura were convicted in Hong Kong for their roles in carrying out the killings and received life sentences. Another serviceman, Inaba Tokujiro, was initially convicted because he had been present during the voyage, but his conviction was later overturned after review concluded that the evidence did not prove active participation in the killings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

Even after these proceedings, important questions remained unanswered:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which individuals actually fired the fatal shots?
* Did anyone refuse or resist the order?
* How widely was the operation known within the garrison?
* Why was the original false story of a medical transfer accepted for so long?

</div>

Because several key witnesses died, records were incomplete and much testimony conflicted, the courts could establish criminal responsibility only for part of the operation.

## Why the case remains historically important

The enduring mystery is not whether the massacre happened but why so much of its detail disappeared.

Unlike many wartime atrocities, the victims left no surviving eyewitnesses. Families on Nauru knew relatives had vanished, yet could learn little about their final hours. Australian investigators recovered enough testimony to establish deliberate murder, but not enough to produce a complete narrative accepted beyond dispute.

The victims' social position also contributed to decades of relative silence. People with leprosy had already been isolated before the war, making it easier for occupying authorities to remove them and easier for later histories to overlook them. Only later scholarship, drawing on Australian archives, Japanese testimony and medical records, restored the massacre to its proper place in Nauru's wartime history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apjjf.org/yuki-tanaka/3441/article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apjjf.org">[apjjf.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apjjf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Asia-Pacific Journal: Japan FocusJapanese Atrocities on Nauru during the Pacific War: The murder of Australians, the massacre of lepers a...</span></span></span>

Today the killing of Nauru's 39 leprosy patients stands as one of the island's clearest examples of how prejudice, wartime secrecy and incomplete documentation can leave even a well-established crime surrounded by enduring historical uncertainty.
