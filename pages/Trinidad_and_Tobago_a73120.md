## Introduction

In several cases, the central event is well established but its meaning remains unresolved. In others, tourism and folklore have built an attractive mystery around evidence that is actually quite thin. Separating those categories reveals something important: Trinidad and Tobago’s historical enigmas are often mysteries of interpretation, memory and missing testimony rather than problems waiting for one dramatic solution.

## What do the Caurita carvings mean?

High in the Caurita Hills of Trinidad’s Maracas Valley stands a boulder roughly six feet high and eight feet wide, covered with carved lines and shapes. Known as the Caurita Stone, it is the only confirmed pre-Columbian petroglyph site in Trinidad. The carvings were made by Indigenous people, but there is no surviving explanation of what they represent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nationaltrust.tt/location/caurita-petroglyphs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.tt">[National Trust of Trinidad and Tobago]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.tt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust of Trinidad and TobagoCaurita PetroglyphsThe Caurita Petroglyph or Caurita Stone is a 6ft high by 8ft wide boulder baring...</span></span></span>

That gap has made the stone one of the country’s clearest genuine archaeological mysteries. Some figures appear geometric; others have been described as human-like or animal-like. Similar rock art elsewhere in the Caribbean suggests that the Caurita carvings belong to a wider regional tradition, but resemblance alone cannot establish their date, makers or purpose. They may record stories, mark a sacred location, identify territory or combine several functions.

The National Trust has associated both Caurita and Banwari Trace with Trinidad’s Indigenous heritage and notes that the Caurita site retains spiritual importance for First Peoples communities. That living significance matters because interpreting it simply as an archaeological code to be “solved” can overlook the possibility that it was part of ritual practice rather than a literal written message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nationaltrust.tt/home/celebrating-our-first-peoples/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.tt">[National Trust of Trinidad and Tobago]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.tt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust of Trinidad and Tobago Celebrating Our First Peoples</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust of Trinidad and TobagoCelebrating Our First PeoplesOctober 20, 2020 — 20 Oct 2020 — More than 5000 years old in both insta...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 20, 2020</span></span></span>

What is securely known is limited:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The marks are deliberate carvings rather than natural fractures.
* The site is pre-Columbian and nationally protected.
* Comparable forms of rock art occur on other Caribbean islands.
* No accepted translation or single interpretation exists.

</div>

Claims that the stone is a map, astronomical chart or record of a vanished civilisation go beyond the available evidence. The honest mystery is narrower but more meaningful: a unique surviving expression of Trinidad’s Indigenous past remains visible, while the cultural knowledge needed to read it has been disrupted by centuries of colonisation.

## Who was buried at Banwari Trace?

Banwari Trace in south-west Trinidad has produced some of the earliest firmly dated evidence of human settlement in the Caribbean. Archaeological deposits indicate occupation thousands of years before European arrival, when communities lived by hunting, fishing and gathering rather than pottery-making agriculture. Its best-known discovery is a crouched human burial uncovered in November 1969.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sta.uwi.edu/conferences/iaca/banwari.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sta.uwi.edu">[The University of the West Indies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sta.uwi.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">found 20-cm below the surface</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The University of the West IndiesThe International Association for Caribbean ArchaeologyIn November 1969, the Trinidad and Tobago Histori...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1969</span></span></span>

The skeleton became popularly known as “Banwari Man”, although it has also been called “Banwari Woman”. That inconsistent naming reflects an unresolved issue: public accounts have not always agreed on the individual’s sex, and early labels were sometimes repeated more confidently than the surviving evidence justified. The burial was found about 20 centimetres below the surface, with a rounded stone near the skull and a pointed object near the hip. Its position within the archaeological layers places it late in the site’s occupation rather than at its very beginning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nationaltrust.tt/home/archaeology-in-t-t/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.tt">[National Trust of Trinidad and Tobago]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.tt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust of Trinidad and TobagoArchaeology in T&amp;T: Then and NowAugust 28, 2020 — 28 Aug 2020 — Trinidad&#x27;s most famous archaeologica...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 28, 2020</span></span></span>

The mystery is therefore not whether an ancient person was buried there. That is well documented. The unanswered questions concern identity and meaning:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was the individual male or female?
* Were the nearby objects grave offerings, tools or unrelated debris?
* Why was the body placed within a shell midden, an accumulation of discarded shells and other food remains?
* Did the crouched position express a particular belief about death, ancestry or rebirth?

</div>

Without DNA results, detailed modern skeletal analysis or an Indigenous written record, no complete biography can be recovered. Even so, the burial is more than an anonymous curiosity. It provides direct evidence that Trinidad was not merely a stepping stone on a migration route. It was home to established communities whose lives and burial practices formed part of the earliest known human history of the Caribbean.

## What does Betty Stiven’s tombstone actually say?

Near Fort James in Plymouth, Tobago, an eighteenth-century tomb carries one of the country’s strangest inscriptions. It commemorates Betty Stiven, who died in 1783 in her early twenties. The wording describes her through a sequence of apparent contradictions, including the famous claim that she was a mother without knowing it and a wife without informing her husband.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visittobago.gov.tt/local-culture-people-heritage/history-culture/historical-sites-and-attractions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visittobago.gov.tt">[visittobago.gov.tt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visittobago.gov.tt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Tobago Historical Sites and Attractions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Tobago Historical Sites and Attractions</span></span></span>

The physical tomb is real, as is the inscription. Almost everything beyond that is interpretation. The most common explanation is that Betty died while pregnant, perhaps before she knew she was carrying a child. This could account for being called a “mother” without knowing it. The line about marriage is harder to explain. It might refer to a secret relationship, an informal union, a disputed marriage or simply elaborate eighteenth-century wordplay.

Local retellings sometimes describe Betty as an enslaved woman involved with a European man. Others present her as the wife of Alexander Stiven, the man named on the memorial. These stories may preserve fragments of local memory, but easily accessible historical accounts do not provide decisive documentation for Betty’s legal status, ancestry or relationships. Tobago’s official visitor material therefore presents the grave as a riddle rather than endorsing one solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visittobago.gov.tt/local-culture-people-heritage/history-culture/historical-sites-and-attractions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visittobago.gov.tt">[visittobago.gov.tt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visittobago.gov.tt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Tobago Historical Sites and Attractions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Tobago Historical Sites and Attractions</span></span></span>

The inscription may also have suffered from modern assumptions. Readers often expect every line to encode a hidden scandal, whereas eighteenth-century epitaphs could be rhetorical, religious and deliberately paradoxical. A grieving husband might have intended the wording to express the suddenness and incomprehensibility of death rather than to leave a factual puzzle.

The strongest conclusion is modest: Betty Stiven was a real young woman whose memorial was designed to provoke thought, but the private circumstances behind its language have not survived. The tomb became mysterious because the inscription endured while the family history needed to interpret it disappeared.

## Did Trinidad lead Walter Raleigh to El Dorado?

In 1595, Walter Raleigh arrived in Trinidad while pursuing reports of a wealthy inland kingdom associated with El Dorado. Trinidad was not a harmless stopping point. Raleigh attacked the recently founded Spanish settlement of San José de Oruña, captured its governor, Antonio de Berrío, and used the island as a base for travelling towards the Orinoco. Both men were seeking information about supposed gold-rich territories in Guiana.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raleigh%27s_El_Dorado_expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Raleigh&#x27;s El Dorado expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Raleigh&#x27;s El Dorado expedition</span></span></span>

Raleigh later published an account presenting Guiana as a land of immense riches and describing a great city called Manoa near a vast Lake Parime. His narrative blended travel observations, Indigenous reports, intelligence obtained from Spanish rivals and claims intended to attract political and financial backing in England. No golden city or enormous inland lake was found.

El Dorado was not originally a simple tale of one lost city. Stories about a richly adorned ruler, gold-bearing regions and powerful inland communities were gradually combined by Europeans into a geographical target. Each unsuccessful expedition encouraged the imagined destination to move farther into unmapped territory. Lake Parime continued appearing on some maps long after evidence for it had failed to materialise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oldworldauctions.com/info/article/2014-10" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oldworldauctions.com">[Old World Auctions]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oldworldauctions.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on oldworldauctions.com.</span></span></span>

Trinidad’s role in the mystery is therefore concrete but uncomfortable. The island was a strategic gateway from which European rivals interrogated local people, attacked settlements and launched expeditions into northern South America. Raleigh did encounter real landscapes and societies, but his promise of an easily conquered golden empire was exaggerated.

Several explanations for the legend can coexist. Reports of actual Indigenous gold-working may have been misunderstood; seasonal flooding could have encouraged descriptions of a vast lake; and European promoters had strong reasons to enlarge every rumour. The evidence supports cultural distortion and imperial ambition far more strongly than the survival of an undiscovered city of gold.

## How many people died in the Hosay massacre?

On 30 October 1884, colonial forces fired upon Hosay processions approaching San Fernando. Hosay, Trinidad’s local observance of Muharram, had developed into a major public event involving Indian Muslims, Hindus and other participants. Colonial authorities attempted to control the routes and prevent processions from entering the town. When participants continued towards San Fernando, police opened fire at several approaches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hosay_massacre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hosay massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosay massacre</span></span></span>

The massacre itself is not disputed. The uncertainty lies in the casualty total and in how the event was later described. Published estimates range from nine deaths at one location to at least sixteen overall, while other historical work places the number at roughly twenty-two, with more than one hundred people injured. Some wounded participants may have fled into cane fields or died later, making an exact count difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hosay_massacre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hosay massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosay massacre</span></span></span>

Colonial language also shaped the record. Calling the event a “riot” placed responsibility on the processionists and presented armed state violence as a response to disorder. The term “massacre”, increasingly preferred in public memory, emphasises the unequal force used against a largely unarmed crowd. Neither word by itself answers every question about confrontations during the day, but surviving accounts clearly establish that officials anticipated resistance, positioned armed forces and authorised live fire.

The missing names and uncertain totals are not trivial accounting problems. Indentured workers and their families were less likely than colonial officials to leave written records preserved in state archives. Consequently, the documentation is strongest for government orders and weakest for the experiences of those who were shot.

The central mystery may never be reduced to a definitive number. What can be recovered is the structure of the event: a colonial government restricted a culturally important procession, prepared an armed response and killed participants whose individual stories were only incompletely recorded.

## What was lost when the Red House burned?

Port of Spain’s Water Riots began on 23 March 1903 during public opposition to proposed changes in water regulation and charging. Protesters gathered around the Red House, where the Legislative Council was meeting. Stones were thrown, the building caught fire and police fired upon the crowd. Sixteen people were killed and forty-two injured, while the Red House was left as a burnt shell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/40653092" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

The fire produced a second, quieter historical disaster: government records were destroyed. Accounts of the development of Trinidad and Tobago’s National Archives trace its origins partly to the recognition, after 1903, that the colony lacked adequate systems for protecting historically valuable documents. Fireproof storage was later incorporated into government buildings, although a systematic archival policy developed only gradually.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archives_of_Trinidad_and_Tobago" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives of Trinidad and Tobago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives of Trinidad and Tobago</span></span></span>

It is difficult to specify every document lost because the inventories that would identify them were themselves incomplete. The destruction may have affected correspondence, administrative papers, legal records and earlier material relating to colonial government. That uncertainty has encouraged sweeping claims that the fire erased the “true history” of Trinidad. Such language goes too far: important records survive in newspapers, church collections, private papers and overseas repositories, particularly Britain’s colonial archives.

Nevertheless, the loss has real consequences. Historians trying to reconstruct disputes over land, labour, taxation or official decisions may find one side of an exchange preserved in London while local petitions, attachments or departmental files are absent. Archival silence can then resemble deliberate concealment even when neglect, fire and poor record management offer a simpler explanation.

The Red House fire is thus both a documented political tragedy and an enduring archival puzzle. It left gaps that cannot always be distinguished from records that were never created, never retained or later lost for unrelated reasons.

## Was Tobago the island of Robinson Crusoe?

Tobago has long been associated with Daniel Defoe’s 1719 novel *Robinson Crusoe*. The claim has geographical appeal. Crusoe describes his island as lying near the coast of South America and within sight of Trinidad, while Tobago possesses the tropical scenery that readers associate with the story. A silent film version of *Robinson Crusoe* was also made there in 1926, reinforcing the connection in popular culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visittobago.gov.tt/local-culture-people-heritage/history-culture/historical-timeline" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visittobago.gov.tt">[visittobago.gov.tt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visittobago.gov.tt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">historical timeline</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">historical timeline</span></span></span>

Yet the novel does not place Crusoe on Tobago. Its internal geography points towards an imaginary island near the Orinoco and Trinidad rather than to a precisely identifiable real location. Defoe appears to have combined several castaway accounts and literary traditions. The most famous possible model, Alexander Selkirk, spent four years on Más a Tierra in the Pacific, but scholars caution against treating any one person or island as the sole source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/robinson-crusoe-alexander-selkirk-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[nationalgeographic.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">robinson crusoe alexander selkirk history</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">robinson crusoe alexander selkirk history</span></span></span>

Tobago’s claim is therefore neither entirely baseless nor historically proven. Its location and environment resemble parts of Defoe’s fictional description, but there is no firm evidence that Defoe visited Tobago or consciously modelled Crusoe’s island upon it. The Pacific Selkirk story contributed the famous experience of prolonged isolation, while Caribbean geography, plantation economics, religious ideas and other survival narratives helped shape the novel.

The question remains popular because it offers Tobago a place inside one of the world’s best-known adventure stories. The best answer is that Crusoe’s island is a literary composite. Tobago fits the fictional landscape better than Selkirk’s cool Pacific island in some respects, but resemblance is not proof of direct inspiration.

## Where documented history becomes folklore

Trinidad and Tobago also has many reputedly haunted estates, forts, caves and abandoned settlements. Chacachacare, once used as a leprosy settlement and later occupied by United States forces during the Second World War, is frequently presented as a ghost island. Lopinot is associated with tales about the spirit of the estate owner after whom the village was named. Such places possess substantial histories of illness, enslavement, forced separation, migration and abandonment, but claims of supernatural activity are matters of folklore rather than verifiable historical evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chacachacare" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The distinction does not make the stories culturally worthless. Ghost narratives often attach themselves to landscapes where suffering has been inadequately commemorated. An abandoned hospital or plantation house provides a visible setting, while oral storytelling gives emotional form to histories that official records describe impersonally.

Problems arise when the ghost story replaces the human history. Chacachacare’s significance lies primarily in the lives of people isolated there because of disease and stigma, not in modern thrill-seeking. Lopinot’s picturesque reputation likewise needs to be balanced against the estate economy and enslaved labour upon which its early prosperity depended.

Folklore is best treated as evidence of how later generations remember a place, not as proof that reported apparitions occurred. That approach preserves the stories while keeping documented suffering, rather than manufactured horror, at the centre.

## What these mysteries reveal

The country’s strongest historical mysteries share a common pattern. Material evidence survives, but the explanatory voice is missing. The Caurita carvings remain while knowledge of their makers’ intentions has been disrupted. Betty Stiven’s inscription survives without the family circumstances that made it intelligible. Casualty reports record the Hosay massacre but fail to name or count every victim. The Red House still stands, rebuilt after 1903, while some of the records destroyed inside it can never be reconstructed.

These are not invitations to fill gaps with conspiracy. They show how power determines what enters an archive, whose testimony is preserved and which interpretation later becomes official. Indigenous communities, indentured labourers, enslaved people, patients in institutions and ordinary protesters were less able to create records that governments considered worth keeping.

The most plausible solutions are therefore often partial. The Caurita Stone may never receive a definitive translation. Betty Stiven’s epitaph may remain deliberately paradoxical. El Dorado can be explained as an evolving mixture of real reports and imperial fantasy without identifying one place where the legend began. The precise Hosay death toll may remain uncertain even though responsibility for the shooting is clear.

Trinidad and Tobago’s historical enigmas matter because their unresolved details expose the limits of the surviving record. They remind readers that uncertainty is not an empty space into which any theory may be placed. It is itself historical evidence: evidence of cultural rupture, unequal documentation, lost archives and the continuing struggle over how the country remembers its past.
