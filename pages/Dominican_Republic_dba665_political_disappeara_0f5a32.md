## Introduction

Political disappearances under the dictatorships of Rafael Trujillo and Joaquín Balaguer are among the [Dominican Republic]({{ 'dominican-republic/' | relative_url }})'s most enduring historical mysteries because they combine strong evidence of state involvement with crucial gaps that have never been filled. In several prominent cases, investigators, historians and international human rights bodies broadly agree that critics were abducted by or with the support of state agents. Yet the victims' bodies were never recovered, official records remain incomplete or contested, witnesses died or changed their accounts, and criminal accountability was limited or absent. These disappearances are therefore mysteries not because the existence of repression is in serious doubt, but because the final details—who gave the orders, exactly what happened in custody, and where the victims' remains lie—remain unresolved.

Two cases illustrate these mechanisms particularly clearly: the 1956 disappearance of Jesús de Galíndez during Trujillo's rule and the 1994 disappearance of Narciso González Medina after criticising Joaquín Balaguer's government. Together they show how intimidation, damaged investigations and missing documentation can leave even well-known political disappearances without complete answers.

## The disappearances of Jesús de Galíndez and Narciso González

### Jesús de Galíndez: an international abduction

Jesús de Galíndez was a Basque exile, lecturer at Columbia University and one of the best-informed foreign critics of Rafael Trujillo. On 12 March 1956 he disappeared after teaching an evening class in New York. His doctoral research examined Trujillo's dictatorship in exceptional detail, making him a particularly sensitive opponent of the regime. Historians widely accept that Dominican intelligence organised his kidnapping from the United States and transported him to the Dominican Republic, where he was interrogated and killed. The precise sequence of events, however, has never been reconstructed beyond dispute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/amr27/001/1994/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Dominican Republic: &quot;disappearance&quot;: Narciso Gonzalez</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalDominican Republic: &quot;disappearance&quot;: Narciso Gonzalez - Amnesty InternationalJune 6, 1994...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 6, 1994</span></span></span>

The mystery deepened because others connected with the operation also died or disappeared. American pilot Gerald Lester Murphy, believed to have flown an aircraft connected with the operation, vanished in the Dominican Republic later that year. Another pilot, Octavio de la Maza, was officially blamed before being found dead in prison beside a supposed confession that United States investigators later regarded as fraudulent. These overlapping deaths destroyed potential witnesses and complicated later investigations.

Today, most historians regard the central question as settled: Galíndez almost certainly became the victim of a transnational assassination ordered by Trujillo's regime. The unresolved elements concern operational details—the exact participants, the full chain of command and the location of his remains.

### Narciso González: the last great disappearance linked to Balaguer

Nearly four decades later, another prominent government critic disappeared under very different political circumstances but with strikingly familiar patterns.

Narciso González Medina, widely known as "Narcisazo", was a lawyer, university professor, journalist and outspoken critic of Joaquín Balaguer. Shortly after denouncing alleged fraud surrounding the May 1994 presidential election, he disappeared on 26 May 1994 in Santo Domingo. His family immediately reported him missing, while witnesses claimed he had been detained by military personnel. Amnesty International rapidly issued urgent appeals after reports that he had been taken to military intelligence facilities, but Dominican authorities denied holding him and never acknowledged responsibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/amr27/001/1994/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Dominican Republic: &quot;disappearance&quot;: Narciso Gonzalez</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalDominican Republic: &quot;disappearance&quot;: Narciso Gonzalez - Amnesty InternationalJune 6, 1994...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 6, 1994</span></span></span>

Unlike many Cold War disappearances elsewhere in Latin America, González vanished during a period when the Dominican Republic formally maintained democratic institutions. That contrast made the case especially disturbing. Instead of occurring during an openly declared dictatorship, it happened amid contested elections, producing years of domestic protest and international scrutiny.

After lengthy proceedings, the Inter-American human rights system concluded that González had been the victim of an enforced disappearance carried out by the Dominican state. The Inter-American Court of Human Rights found sufficient evidence that state agents were responsible and ordered further investigation, renewed searches for his remains and reparations for his family. Even with that judicial finding, however, his body has never been located and the complete operational history remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.corteidh.or.cr/ver_ficha_tecnica.cfm?lang=es&amp;nId_Ficha=199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: corteidh.or.cr">[or.cr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">corteidh.or.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Corte IDHCorte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Corte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos - Ficha técnica...</span></span></span>

## Why these disappearances proved so difficult to solve

Although the Galíndez and González cases occurred almost forty years apart, they reveal several recurring mechanisms that make political disappearances exceptionally resistant to complete investigation.

First, **the removal of the victim eliminated the central source of evidence**. Without recovered remains, investigators could rarely establish precise causes of death or reconstruct final movements with forensic certainty.

Second, **witness testimony was unstable**. In both cases, reports emerged from people claiming to have seen the victims in military or intelligence facilities, yet many witnesses feared retaliation, gave inconsistent statements or could not provide evidence sufficient for criminal prosecution. Amnesty International documented repeated reports that González had been seen alive in military custody shortly after his disappearance, but authorities denied those claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/amr27/002/1994/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalDominican Republic: Further information: &quot;Disappearance&quot;: Narciso Gonzalez - Amnesty International...</span></span></span>

Third, **official records were incomplete or inaccessible**. Intelligence services generally produce few publicly available documents concerning covert detention. Where records existed, they were sometimes classified, destroyed or never fully disclosed, leaving historians dependent on diplomatic correspondence, witness testimony and fragmentary archival material.

Finally, **political influence weakened investigations**. During Trujillo's dictatorship, independent investigation was virtually impossible. Under Balaguer, although judicial institutions formally existed, critics argued that investigators failed to pursue military suspects vigorously enough, allowing evidence to disappear and key opportunities to be lost. International human rights bodies later criticised the Dominican state's failure to conduct an effective investigation into González's disappearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.corteidh.or.cr/ver_ficha_tecnica.cfm?lang=es&amp;nId_Ficha=199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: corteidh.or.cr">[or.cr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">corteidh.or.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Corte IDHCorte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Corte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos - Ficha técnica...</span></span></span>

## Witnesses, intelligence files and damaged investigations

The surviving evidence comes from several distinct categories, each with important strengths and limitations.

**Witness accounts** provide many of the most detailed narratives but are vulnerable to memory errors, fear and conflicting recollections. In González's case, numerous reports placed him inside military or police installations after his disappearance, yet these accounts never produced a definitive criminal reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.corteidh.or.cr/ver_ficha_tecnica.cfm?lang=es&amp;nId_Ficha=199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: corteidh.or.cr">[CorteIDH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">corteidh.or.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Corte IDHCorte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Corte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos - Ficha técnica...</span></span></span>

**Diplomatic and intelligence records** are valuable because they preserve information gathered independently of domestic authorities. Material released over time has strengthened historians' confidence that the Galíndez operation involved organised state action rather than a private crime. Nevertheless, intelligence reporting often contains hearsay alongside verified information, requiring careful interpretation.

**Judicial findings** have clarified important questions without resolving every factual uncertainty. The Inter-American Court's judgment in the González case established state responsibility under international human rights law, but a legal finding of enforced disappearance is not the same as a complete historical reconstruction. Questions about the exact sequence of detention, killing and disposal of remains remain unanswered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.corteidh.or.cr/ver_ficha_tecnica.cfm?lang=es&amp;nId_Ficha=199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: corteidh.or.cr">[CorteIDH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">corteidh.or.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Corte IDHCorte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Corte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos - Ficha técnica...</span></span></span>

## What is established and what remains unknown

Several conclusions now rest on strong historical and legal foundations.

Well established are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Trujillo's government employed kidnapping, assassination and intimidation against political opponents, including beyond Dominican territory.
* Jesús de Galíndez disappeared in circumstances that historians overwhelmingly attribute to a covert operation directed by Trujillo's regime.
* Narciso González disappeared shortly after publicly attacking Balaguer's government during the disputed 1994 election period.
* International human rights institutions concluded that González was subjected to enforced disappearance by agents of the Dominican state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.corteidh.or.cr/ver_ficha_tecnica.cfm?lang=es&amp;nId_Ficha=199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: corteidh.or.cr">[CorteIDH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">corteidh.or.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Corte IDHCorte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Corte Interamericana de Derechos Humanos - Ficha técnica...</span></span></span>

</div>

Important uncertainties nevertheless remain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The complete operational chain of command behind Galíndez's abduction.
* The exact circumstances of both men's deaths.
* The locations of their remains.
* Whether all documentary evidence generated by intelligence and military agencies has survived or will ever become available.

</div>

These unresolved questions explain why the disappearances continue to occupy a distinctive place in Dominican historical memory. They are not mysteries because the existence of political repression is doubtful; rather, they demonstrate how enforced disappearance deliberately creates uncertainty. By removing victims, intimidating witnesses and obscuring official records, those responsible ensured that even decades of investigation have produced compelling evidence without complete historical closure.
