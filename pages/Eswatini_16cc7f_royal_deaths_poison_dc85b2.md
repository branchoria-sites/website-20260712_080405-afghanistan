## Introduction

Stories that royal heirs in [Eswatini]({{ 'what-remains-unsolved-in-eswatinis-past/' | relative_url }}) were secretly poisoned have circulated for more than a century. They remain among the country's most persistent historical mysteries because several untimely deaths changed the line of succession at moments of intense political competition, while the royal court traditionally revealed little about illness, internal disagreements or the circumstances surrounding a monarch's final days. The result is a historical record in which rumours often travelled further than evidence.

The best-supported conclusion is cautious rather than dramatic. Some royal deaths, particularly those of the young kings Ludvonga and Bhunu, genuinely transformed the political future of the kingdom and prompted immediate suspicions of foul play. Yet surviving contemporary records have never produced convincing proof that poisoning occurred. Instead, historians generally view the allegations as products of succession struggles, court secrecy and later political memory rather than established fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-law/article/abs/development-of-swazilands-constitution-monarchical-responses-to-modern-challenges/2CEE9EEA0D068EA8EC13C8D390B69370" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Development of Swaziland&#x27;s Constitution: Monarchical Responses to Modern Challenges &#124; Journal...</span></span></span>

## The deaths that fuelled the rumours

Two nineteenth-century royal deaths dominate discussions of alleged poisoning because each abruptly altered who would rule the kingdom.

Prince-turned-King **Ludvonga**, the son and successor of King Mswati II, inherited the throne while still a child after his father's death in 1865. He died around 1874 before reaching adulthood. His death cleared the way for his half-brother Mbandzeni to become king. Official historical summaries acknowledge the succession but provide little detail about Ludvonga's illness or final days, leaving space for later stories that he had been deliberately poisoned. Modern historical accounts, however, cannot demonstrate that claim with documentary evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gov.sz/index.php/about-us-sp-15933109/who-we-are/chosing-a-king" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gov.sz">[Government of Eswatini]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gov.sz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ernment of Eswatini Choosing a King</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ernment of EswatiniChoosing a King...</span></span></span>

A generation later, **King Bhunu** inherited the throne after Mbandzeni's death. His reign coincided with mounting pressure from European settlers, concession seekers and colonial powers. Bhunu died suddenly on 10 December 1899 while still a young man, leaving only an infant son, Nkhotfotjeni, who would later become Sobhuza II after a lengthy regency. Because Bhunu's death immediately reshaped political authority during an exceptionally unstable period, poisoning allegations again emerged. Yet, as with Ludvonga, historians have found no contemporary medical or forensic evidence capable of proving deliberate poisoning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gov.sz/index.php/about-us-sp-15933109/who-we-are/chosing-a-king" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gov.sz">[Government of Eswatini]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gov.sz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ernment of Eswatini Choosing a King</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ernment of EswatiniChoosing a King...</span></span></span>

Other members of the royal family have also been the subjects of occasional poison rumours across different periods, but these usually rest on oral tradition, political accusation or retrospective speculation rather than verifiable historical documentation.

## Why succession made poison stories believable

The persistence of these allegations is easier to understand when viewed through the structure of the Swazi monarchy rather than through modern crime investigation.

Unlike hereditary systems with a single automatic heir, succession in Eswatini historically depended upon complex decisions involving the status of royal mothers, senior councillors and the royal council. The identity of the future king was closely connected to the selection of the future Queen Mother, making succession both political and ceremonial. A death could therefore benefit one branch of the royal family while disadvantaging another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gov.sz/index.php/about-us-sp-15933109/who-we-are/chosing-a-king" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gov.sz">[Government of Eswatini]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gov.sz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ernment of Eswatini Choosing a King</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ernment of EswatiniChoosing a King...</span></span></span>

Several factors encouraged suspicion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Multiple competing royal households.** Kings had many wives and numerous children, creating several potential succession interests.
* **Restricted information.** Royal affairs, especially illness and death, were treated as matters of state and custom, limiting public knowledge.
* **High political stakes.** Every unexpected royal death could change the balance of influence among senior families and advisers.
* **Colonial disruption.** During the late nineteenth century, British and Boer involvement complicated internal politics, making later reconstructions of events even more difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gov.sz/index.php/about-us-sp-15933109/who-we-are/chosing-a-king" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gov.sz">[Government of Eswatini]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gov.sz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ernment of Eswatini Choosing a King</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ernment of EswatiniChoosing a King...</span></span></span>

</div>

Under these conditions, poisoning became a convenient explanation whenever a death appeared sudden or politically advantageous.

## Why historians remain unconvinced

Modern readers often expect mysterious deaths to be resolved through pathology reports, police investigations or preserved forensic evidence. None of those exists for the principal nineteenth-century cases.

Instead, historians rely upon:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* colonial correspondence;
* missionary accounts;
* later royal traditions;
* oral histories;
* official genealogies and succession records.

</div>

These sources establish **that the deaths occurred** and **that succession changed dramatically**, but they rarely explain the precise medical cause. The absence of evidence, however, is not evidence of poisoning.

Many later publications repeat poison allegations without identifying a contemporary witness or document. In numerous cases, one secondary account simply reproduces another until the claim acquires an appearance of certainty that the original evidence does not support.

This distinction is important. An unresolved death is not automatically a murder, and a longstanding rumour is not automatically a historical fact.

## How secrecy strengthened the legend

Royal courts throughout southern Africa traditionally guarded sensitive information, particularly concerning illness, succession and burial. Eswatini was no exception.

Secrecy served several purposes. It protected royal dignity, reduced opportunities for rivals to exploit perceived weakness and preserved ceremonial authority during periods when political stability depended heavily upon the monarchy. The unintended consequence was that later generations inherited incomplete records.

Where documentation is sparse, oral explanations naturally fill the gaps. Stories of poison often become attractive because they provide a simple narrative for events that otherwise appear abrupt or inexplicable. Similar traditions can be found elsewhere in southern African royal history, where sudden deaths frequently generated allegations of palace intrigue even when no proof emerged.

## Separating documented uncertainty from later accusation

For readers trying to assess the mystery, the evidence can be divided into three levels.

**Well established**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* Ludvonga died before reaching adulthood.
* Bhunu died unexpectedly in 1899.
* Both deaths produced major succession changes.
* Long regencies followed, reshaping the kingdom's political development.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gov.sz/index.php/about-us-sp-15933109/who-we-are/chosing-a-king" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gov.sz">[Government of Eswatini]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gov.sz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ernment of Eswatini Choosing a King</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ernment of EswatiniChoosing a King...</span></span></span>

</div>

**Reasonably supported**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Poisoning rumours circulated within later historical traditions.
* The political circumstances gave contemporaries understandable reasons to suspect intrigue.
* Court secrecy limited reliable public information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-law/article/abs/development-of-swazilands-constitution-monarchical-responses-to-modern-challenges/2CEE9EEA0D068EA8EC13C8D390B69370" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Development of Swaziland&#x27;s Constitution: Monarchical Responses to Modern Challenges &#124; Journal...</span></span></span>

</div>

**Not demonstrated**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* That either Ludvonga or Bhunu was actually poisoned.
* That a documented conspiracy existed.
* That surviving primary evidence identifies perpetrators or methods.

</div>

This careful distinction explains why modern scholarship usually discusses the allegations as part of Eswatini's political history rather than as solved crimes.

## The historical picture around Royal Deaths
The enduring fascination of these royal deaths lies less in the possibility of an undiscovered poison than in what the rumours reveal about power, legitimacy and memory.

Every disputed succession encouraged later generations to reinterpret the past through the lens of political advantage. Families and supporters could explain unexpected changes by pointing to hidden enemies rather than illness or chance. Over time, these explanations became embedded in popular historical memory, even though the documentary record remained fragmentary.

For historians, the real mystery is therefore twofold. First, the precise medical causes of several important royal deaths cannot now be recovered with confidence. Second, it is impossible to determine exactly when later poison narratives emerged and how much they reflected genuine contemporary suspicion rather than retrospective political storytelling.

The surviving evidence supports neither complete scepticism nor dramatic conspiracy. It shows that unexpected royal deaths genuinely altered Eswatini's history, while the allegations of poisoning remain enduring possibilities that have never progressed beyond suspicion into proof.
