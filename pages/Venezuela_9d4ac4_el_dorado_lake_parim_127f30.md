## Introduction

For more than two centuries, European maps showed an immense inland lake in the Guiana highlands, south of the Orinoco River, with the legendary city of Manoa standing on its shores. The lake was known as Lake Parime, and it became one of the most enduring geographical myths in the history of [Venezuela]({{ 'venezuela/' | relative_url }}) and the wider Guiana region. Although explorers repeatedly searched for it, no such lake was ever found. Today, historians see Lake Parime as a striking example of how rumour, misunderstanding, imperfect exploration and repeated copying between mapmakers combined to create a feature that seemed increasingly real despite the absence of reliable evidence. The mystery matters not because the lake vanished, but because generations of explorers genuinely believed it existed, influencing expeditions, colonial ambitions and the European understanding of northern South America.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/b3w-1032.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ub.edu">[Universitat de Barcelona]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ub.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitat de BarcelonaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna ParimeJuly 5, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2013</span></span></span>

## How Manoa Reached the Orinoco

The legend of El Dorado did not begin in Venezuela. Early Spanish accounts from the sixteenth century described a ruler in the highlands of present-day Colombia who supposedly covered himself in gold dust during ceremonial rituals. Over time, this story evolved. Instead of a gilded ruler, European imagination increasingly focused on a fabulously wealthy city called Manoa, somewhere deep within the unexplored interior of South America.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/El_Dorado" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Dorado</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1911 Encyclopædia Britannica/El Dorado - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

As Spanish expeditions pushed eastwards, reports from Indigenous peoples were often misunderstood, exaggerated or filtered through interpreters. Stories about distant settlements, seasonal wetlands or prosperous trading communities became merged with expectations of immense mineral wealth. By the late sixteenth century, explorers such as Antonio de Berrío had shifted the supposed location of Manoa towards the Guiana region between the Orinoco and Amazon river systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/b3w-1032.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ub.edu">[Universitat de Barcelona]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ub.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitat de BarcelonaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna ParimeJuly 5, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2013</span></span></span>

The English explorer Sir Walter Raleigh played a crucial role in fixing this new geography in the European imagination. Following his expedition up the Orinoco in 1595, Raleigh published *The Discoverie of the Large, Rich and Beautiful Empire of Guiana*, describing Manoa as lying beside a vast inland lake. Although he never reached such a place, his account gave the story enormous authority among European readers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/El_Dorado" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Dorado</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1911 Encyclopædia Britannica/El Dorado - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

## How Mapmakers Made the Lake Seem Real

One of the most remarkable aspects of the mystery is that Lake Parime became widely accepted not because explorers confirmed it, but because mapmakers copied one another.

During the seventeenth century, influential European cartographers began depicting a huge lake occupying much of the interior of Guiana. Once respected atlases included the feature, later mapmakers often reproduced it rather than omitting it. In an age when much of inland South America remained poorly surveyed, blank spaces on maps invited educated speculation. A lake supported by prestigious earlier maps could appear more credible than admitting ignorance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/b3w-1032.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ub.edu">[Universitat de Barcelona]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ub.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitat de BarcelonaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna ParimeJuly 5, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2013</span></span></span>

Several factors encouraged this cartographic persistence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Large parts of the Guiana Shield were extremely difficult for Europeans to reach.
* Seasonal flooding across extensive savannahs could create enormous temporary wetlands.
* Information travelled slowly and was often second-hand.
* Cartographers frequently relied on previous published maps instead of new surveys.

</div>

The result was a geographical feedback loop. Explorers expected to find Lake Parime because maps showed it, while new maps continued to include it because explorers had searched for it. The apparent agreement between expeditions and cartography reinforced belief even though neither provided direct proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/b3w-1032.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ub.edu">[Universitat de Barcelona]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ub.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitat de BarcelonaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna ParimeJuly 5, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2013</span></span></span>

## Why the Landscape Encouraged the Myth

The mystery becomes easier to understand when viewed against the physical geography of southern Venezuela and neighbouring Guyana.

The Rupununi savannahs and surrounding lowlands experience major seasonal flooding. During the rainy season, rivers overflow and extensive plains can resemble an enormous inland sea when viewed from limited vantage points. Travellers encountering these conditions without modern surveying equipment could reasonably overestimate the size and permanence of the water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/b3w-1032.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ub.edu">[Universitat de Barcelona]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ub.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitat de BarcelonaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna ParimeJuly 5, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2013</span></span></span>

Equally important was the fragmented nature of exploration. Few Europeans travelled continuously across the region. Instead, knowledge came from disconnected river journeys, Indigenous guides, missionary reports and military expeditions, each describing only small sections of a vast landscape. Attempts to combine these fragments into a single coherent map often produced errors that persisted for generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/b3w-1032.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ub.edu">[Universitat de Barcelona]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ub.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitat de BarcelonaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna ParimeJuly 5, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2013</span></span></span>

Modern research therefore suggests that Lake Parime was not a real vanished lake but a misunderstanding shaped by genuine environmental features, especially seasonal inundation, combined with incomplete geographical knowledge.

## Why Explorers Never Found It

From the late sixteenth century through the eighteenth century, numerous expeditions entered the Guiana interior searching for Manoa or Lake Parime. None discovered the enormous permanent lake depicted on European maps.

As scientific exploration became more systematic during the eighteenth century, surveyors produced increasingly accurate descriptions of the Orinoco basin and neighbouring regions. Boundary expeditions organised during negotiations between the Spanish and Portuguese empires also generated far better geographical information than earlier treasure-seeking journeys. These investigations steadily reduced the space in which the mythical lake could exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portaldelaciencia.uva.es/documentos/61b998008bc05f42e9386f6e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portaldelaciencia.uva.es">[Portal de la Ciencia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portaldelaciencia.uva.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Portal de la CienciaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna Parime &#124; Documents - Universidad de V...</span></span></span>

The decisive change came with the scientific expeditions of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Explorers including the Prussian naturalist Alexander von Humboldt travelled extensively through the Orinoco basin and demonstrated that the supposed lake had no physical counterpart. As more reliable surveys accumulated, cartographers gradually abandoned Lake Parime. By the early nineteenth century it had disappeared from serious maps.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/El_Dorado" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Dorado</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1911 Encyclopædia Britannica/El Dorado - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

## Landscape, Legend and Repeated Error

Lake Parime remains a classic example of how myths can become embedded in scientific-looking documents.

Unlike a simple folktale, the lake gained credibility because it appeared on maps—objects that readers naturally associated with measurement and authority. Yet early modern cartography often balanced observation with informed conjecture. Where direct evidence was lacking, respected reports and inherited traditions could become part of accepted geography.

The story also illustrates the limits of colonial knowledge. European empires possessed impressive maritime navigation, but vast inland regions of South America remained imperfectly understood for centuries. Indigenous knowledge was often filtered through translation, misunderstood or selectively interpreted in ways that matched European expectations of hidden kingdoms and mineral wealth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/b3w-1032.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ub.edu">[Universitat de Barcelona]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ub.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitat de BarcelonaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna ParimeJuly 5, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2013</span></span></span>

## Did Lake Parime and El Dorado Exist?

The evidence points to a clear distinction between legend and reality.

A permanent inland lake of the size shown on historical maps almost certainly never existed in the Guiana interior. Modern geography, hydrology and centuries of exploration leave no convincing evidence for such a body of water. Likewise, there is no credible evidence that the golden city of Manoa existed as described in European accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/b3w-1032.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ub.edu">[Universitat de Barcelona]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ub.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitat de BarcelonaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna ParimeJuly 5, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2013</span></span></span>

However, the legend did not arise from pure invention. It drew upon genuine Indigenous traditions, observations of seasonally flooded landscapes, reports of prosperous communities, and European hopes of discovering extraordinary mineral wealth. Each retelling shifted the story further from its original context until it became attached to a precise location that never matched reality.

The enduring mystery, therefore, is not where Lake Parime disappeared. It is how an imaginary lake became one of the most confidently mapped features in northern South America for over two hundred years, shaping exploration and colonial ambition across what is now southern Venezuela and the Guiana region before finally yielding to careful scientific observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/b3w-1032.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ub.edu">[ub.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ub.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Universitat de BarcelonaUn mito geográfico de larga tradición: La perduración cartográfica de la laguna ParimeJuly 5, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 5, 2013</span></span></span>
