## Introduction

On 30 June 1908, a tremendous explosion devastated a remote area of central Siberia near the Podkamennaya Tunguska River, flattening more than 2,000 square kilometres of forest yet leaving no conventional impact crater. More than a century later, the central mystery is no longer whether something from space caused the event—the overwhelming scientific consensus is that it did—but why the object disintegrated in the atmosphere and exactly what it was made of. The evidence from eyewitnesses, the pattern of fallen trees, modern computer modelling and geological investigations strongly supports a cosmic airburst, while competing ideas continue to debate whether the body was primarily a stony asteroid, a comet, or left surviving fragments on the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019103518305104" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties - ScienceDirectJuly 15, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2019</span></span></span>

## Why did Tunguska leave no crater?

The absence of a large impact crater has fuelled speculation ever since the event became widely known outside Siberia. Early investigators expected to find a meteorite impact similar to a conventional crater-forming collision. Instead, expeditions led by Leonid Kulik during the 1920s found millions of trees knocked flat in a vast butterfly-shaped pattern, but no giant hole in the ground.

Today, this is no longer considered surprising. Modern impact physics shows that relatively small cosmic bodies travelling at tens of kilometres per second can fragment catastrophically under atmospheric pressure before reaching the surface. The resulting explosion releases enormous energy several kilometres above the ground. Rather than excavating a crater, the blast wave spreads outward and topples trees over a huge area while leaving the landscape itself largely intact. Numerical models developed after the 2013 Chelyabinsk airburst have strengthened this interpretation by demonstrating that atmospheric explosions naturally produce the kind of damage observed at Tunguska.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-98362-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misunderstandings about the Tunguska event, shock wave physics, and airbursts have resulted in misinterpretations of evidence at Ta...</span></span></span>

## Eyewitness reports and the flattened forest

The physical evidence begins with the people who experienced the explosion. Hundreds of witness accounts describe an intensely bright object crossing the sky, followed by heat, deafening explosions and a powerful shock wave. Some observers reported being thrown to the ground, while windows shattered many kilometres away.

Modern researchers have carefully re-examined more than 700 surviving eyewitness accounts rather than relying on the most dramatic stories. Although individual testimonies sometimes contradict one another—as expected when people viewed the event from different directions—they consistently describe a brilliant fireball followed by multiple explosive sounds, behaviour expected from an object breaking apart in the atmosphere. Researchers also conclude that at least three deaths were directly attributable to the event, with several dozen people close enough to suffer injuries or lose consciousness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019103518305104" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties - ScienceDirectJuly 15, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2019</span></span></span>

The forest itself provides even stronger evidence. Around the centre of the blast, trees remained standing but were stripped of branches, while farther away they were flattened radially away from the explosion. This "butterfly" pattern closely matches the predicted effects of a high-altitude shock wave generated by an object travelling along a shallow trajectory rather than by an impact into the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019103518305104" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties - ScienceDirectJuly 15, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2019</span></span></span>

## The scientific case for an atmospheric airburst

Several independent lines of evidence now converge on the airburst explanation.

**The blast pattern.** The direction in which trees fell indicates a shock wave originating above the forest rather than from beneath it. The surviving upright trees near the centre are consistent with a downward-moving pressure wave rather than excavation by an impact.

**No large impact crater.** Geological surveys have failed to identify the kind of crater expected from a body releasing its energy at ground level. Instead, the evidence points to most of the object's kinetic energy being deposited in the atmosphere.

**Energy estimates.** Modern reconstructions generally estimate an explosion equivalent to roughly 10–15 megatons of TNT, occurring several kilometres above the surface. That is sufficient to flatten the forest without excavating a major crater.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-98362-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misunderstandings about the Tunguska event, shock wave physics, and airbursts have resulted in misinterpretations of evidence at Ta...</span></span></span>

**Comparison with Chelyabinsk.** The 2013 Chelyabinsk meteor provided a smaller but well-observed modern example of atmospheric fragmentation. Although far less energetic than Tunguska, it confirmed that incoming asteroids can explode in mid-air, generate destructive shock waves and still leave only relatively small surviving meteorites. This real-world comparison has greatly increased confidence in Tunguska airburst models.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019103518305104" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties - ScienceDirectJuly 15, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2019</span></span></span>

## What was the object?

While the airburst itself is widely accepted, the nature of the incoming body remains debated.

The leading candidates are:

* **A stony asteroid.** Many researchers now favour a relatively weak stony asteroid around 50–80 metres across. This interpretation fits modern fragmentation models and explains why little recoverable material reached the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13620" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Computation of a possible Tunguska&#x27;s strewn field</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Computation of a possible Tunguska&#x27;s strewn fieldFebruary 27, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 27, 2023</span></span></span>
* **A cometary body.** Earlier studies often favoured an icy comet because so little meteorite material has been found. A largely icy object would have vaporised more completely, although modern modelling shows that weak asteroids can also disintegrate almost entirely in the atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.1067" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cometary airbursts and atmospheric chemistry: Tunguska and a candidate Younger Dryas eventJuly 6, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2009</span></span></span>

Current evidence has narrowed the debate to these broadly cosmic explanations rather than more exotic alternatives.

## Fragments, Lake Cheko and weaker alternatives

One unresolved question is whether any sizeable fragments survived the explosion.

### Lake Cheko

The best-known proposal identifies nearby Lake Cheko, about 8 kilometres from the main devastation zone, as a possible impact crater formed by a surviving fragment. Italian researchers argued that its conical shape, sediment thickness and apparent absence from early maps were compatible with formation during the 1908 event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-3121.2008.00792.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Lake Cheko and the Tunguska Event: impact or non‐impact?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryLake Cheko and the Tunguska Event: impact or non‐impact? - Gasperini - 2008 - Terra Nova - Wiley Online LibraryFebrua...</span></span></span>

However, this interpretation remains controversial. Other researchers argue that the lake predates the explosion, that historical mapping of such a remote region was incomplete, and that similar lakes occur naturally in the surrounding landscape. Recent modelling also suggests that if fragments survived, their predicted landing area may not coincide with Lake Cheko. Consequently, Lake Cheko is regarded as an interesting but unproven possibility rather than confirmed evidence of an impact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13620" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Computation of a possible Tunguska&#x27;s strewn field</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Computation of a possible Tunguska&#x27;s strewn fieldFebruary 27, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 27, 2023</span></span></span>

### Surviving meteorites

Despite repeated expeditions, no universally accepted large Tunguska meteorite has been recovered. This absence does not contradict the airburst model. Many atmospheric explosions completely vaporise weak material, while any surviving fragments may be small, deeply buried beneath permafrost, or difficult to distinguish from ordinary terrestrial rocks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13620" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Computation of a possible Tunguska&#x27;s strewn field</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Computation of a possible Tunguska&#x27;s strewn fieldFebruary 27, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 27, 2023</span></span></span>

### Speculative explanations

The lack of a crater encouraged a remarkable range of alternative theories during the twentieth century, including miniature black holes, antimatter, natural gas explosions, alien spacecraft, secret weapons and even early nuclear devices.

These ideas are not supported by the physical evidence. None explains the observed flight path, the characteristic tree-fall pattern, eyewitness descriptions, atmospheric effects and energy release as consistently as a cosmic airburst. ## Why the mystery still endures

Tunguska remains scientifically fascinating not because its basic cause is unknown, but because important details cannot be reconstructed with complete certainty. The explosion occurred in an extremely remote region, and the first systematic scientific expedition arrived almost two decades later. By then, weathering, forest growth and the passage of time had erased much of the original evidence.

The enduring questions are therefore narrower than popular accounts often suggest. Researchers continue to investigate the object's exact composition, fragmentation sequence, explosion altitude and whether any substantial fragments reached the ground. Those uncertainties make Tunguska an important natural laboratory for understanding the hazards posed by near-Earth objects, even as the central explanation—a powerful atmospheric explosion caused by a cosmic body—is now supported by the strongest available evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019103518305104" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tunguska eyewitness accounts, injuries, and casualties - ScienceDirectJuly 15, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2019</span></span></span>
