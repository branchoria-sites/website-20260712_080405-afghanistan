## Introduction

The Salme ship burials on the Estonian island of Saaremaa are among the most remarkable archaeological discoveries in northern Europe. Excavated between 2008 and 2012, they contained the remains of more than 40 men laid to rest in two ships dating to around AD 750, decades before the traditional beginning of the Viking Age. Archaeologists can now say with considerable confidence that these were Scandinavian warriors, probably from central Sweden, and that many died violently in a single conflict. Yet the central mystery remains unresolved: why were they on Saaremaa, who killed them, and who took the time to bury them so carefully? Modern archaeology, genetics and isotope analysis have answered many questions about the men themselves, but the purpose and fate of their expedition continue to be debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519301278" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human remains, context, and place of origin for the Salme, Estonia, boat burials - ScienceDirectJune 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2020</span></span></span>

## What the two ship graves contained

The discovery began accidentally in 2008 during construction work near the village of Salme. Initial finds revealed a small clinker-built boat, now known as Salme I. Further excavations uncovered a much larger sailing ship, Salme II, containing an extraordinary mass burial unlike anything previously found in [Estonia]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-estonias/' | relative_url }}). Radiocarbon dating and associated artefacts place both burials around AD 750, during the transition from the Vendel Period into the early Viking Age.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519301278" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human remains, context, and place of origin for the Salme, Estonia, boat burials - ScienceDirectJune 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2020</span></span></span>

The two vessels differed in scale but appeared to belong to the same tragic event.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Salme I** contained the remains of seven men together with weapons and equipment.
* **Salme II** held 34 additional individuals arranged in several layers inside a sailing ship approximately 17 metres long.
* The total number of identified dead exceeds 40, making this one of the largest known ship burials from early medieval northern Europe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/isotopic-provenancing-of-the-salme-ship-burials-in-previking-age-estonia/2104723979CFABF9B89CD36851218E62" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentIsotopic provenancing of the Salme ship burials in Pre-Viking Age Estonia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge...</span></span></span>

</div>

The grave goods immediately suggested that these were not ordinary travellers. Archaeologists recovered dozens of swords, shields, spearheads, gaming pieces carved from whalebone and walrus ivory, decorated antler combs, hunting birds, dogs, jewellery, padlocks and animal remains interpreted as provisions. Many objects have close parallels in central Sweden rather than Estonia, indicating that both the ships and their occupants came from across the Baltic Sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/isotopic-provenancing-of-the-salme-ship-burials-in-previking-age-estonia/2104723979CFABF9B89CD36851218E62" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentIsotopic provenancing of the Salme ship burials in Pre-Viking Age Estonia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge...</span></span></span>

One striking detail is that the dead were not simply abandoned where they fell. The bodies were deliberately placed within the ships, covered, and buried with valuable possessions. That careful treatment implies that survivors—or at least people sympathetic to them—had enough time and security to conduct a formal burial.

## Origins, kinship and violent injuries

For years archaeologists relied mainly on artefacts to identify the men as Scandinavians. Scientific analysis has since strengthened that conclusion.

Stable isotope analysis of tooth enamel compares chemical signatures acquired during childhood with regional geological patterns. The Salme individuals consistently point towards central Sweden, especially the Mälaren region around modern Stockholm, although some variation suggests not everyone came from exactly the same locality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/isotopic-provenancing-of-the-salme-ship-burials-in-previking-age-estonia/2104723979CFABF9B89CD36851218E62" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentIsotopic provenancing of the Salme ship burials in Pre-Viking Age Estonia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge...</span></span></span>

Ancient DNA has revealed another remarkable finding: several of the dead were close relatives. At least four were brothers, while another individual appears to have been a more distant family member. Rather than being a randomly assembled force, the expedition seems to have included kin groups travelling together. This offers a rare glimpse into how early Scandinavian military expeditions may have been organised before the large Viking fleets of later centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519301278" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human remains, context, and place of origin for the Salme, Estonia, boat burials - ScienceDirectJune 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2020</span></span></span>

The skeletons also tell a vivid story of combat.

Many individuals suffered fatal wounds from swords, spears and arrows. Arrowheads remained embedded in shields and ship timbers, while some skeletons display cuts to the skull or upper body consistent with close fighting. Several injuries appear to have been immediately lethal, leaving little doubt that the men died in battle rather than from disease or shipwreck.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/isotopic-provenancing-of-the-salme-ship-burials-in-previking-age-estonia/2104723979CFABF9B89CD36851218E62" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentIsotopic provenancing of the Salme ship burials in Pre-Viking Age Estonia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge...</span></span></span>

The combination of elite weapons, close relatives, careful burial and violent death makes Salme unique. It captures what appears to have been a single disastrous expedition frozen in archaeological time.

## Competing accounts of the lost expedition

Although the identities of the dead have become clearer, their mission remains one of Estonia's most intriguing historical puzzles.

### Were they raiders?

The most obvious explanation is that they were an early Scandinavian raiding party defeated after landing on Saaremaa.

Saaremaa occupied a strategic position controlling routes through the eastern Baltic, and later medieval sources describe its inhabitants as experienced seafarers capable of resisting Scandinavian attacks. The evidence of battle certainly fits a failed raid. Yet several details complicate this picture. The burial was respectful rather than hurried, and valuable weapons were left with the dead instead of being stripped as spoils.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uu.se/en/research/the-viking-phenomenon/research-clusters/boat-grave-culture/salme" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uu.se">[Uppsala University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uu.se</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uppsala University Salme – Uppsala University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uppsala University Salme – Uppsala University</span></span></span>

### Were they a diplomatic mission?

Some archaeologists argue that the expedition may have been diplomatic rather than purely military.

The unusually rich equipment, elaborate dress accessories and high-status objects suggest individuals of considerable rank. One interpretation proposes that they formed an elite escort protecting important leaders engaged in negotiations or alliance-building across the Baltic. If talks collapsed into violence, the survivors—or even victorious locals following accepted customs—may have arranged the burials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/isotopic-provenancing-of-the-salme-ship-burials-in-previking-age-estonia/2104723979CFABF9B89CD36851218E62" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentIsotopic provenancing of the Salme ship burials in Pre-Viking Age Estonia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge...</span></span></span>

### Who buried them?

Perhaps the greatest unanswered question concerns the burial itself.

Several possibilities remain plausible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Surviving companions** buried their dead before returning to Scandinavia.
* **Local inhabitants** conducted the burial after the fighting, perhaps as an act of respect or according to regional customs governing fallen enemies.
* **A negotiated peace** after the conflict allowed both sides to honour the dead.

</div>

No written source records the event, so archaeology alone cannot distinguish between these scenarios. The orderly arrangement of the bodies, however, strongly indicates that the burial was intentional and organised rather than a simple disposal of corpses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519301278" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human remains, context, and place of origin for the Salme, Estonia, boat burials - ScienceDirectJune 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2020</span></span></span>

## Why Salme changed the history of the Viking Age

The Salme discoveries have altered scholarly understanding of Scandinavian expansion across the Baltic.

Before these excavations, large ship burials of armed Scandinavian groups were generally associated with the Viking Age after the raid on Lindisfarne in AD 793. Salme demonstrates that organised overseas expeditions involving sailing ships, elite warriors and long-distance travel were already taking place several decades earlier. Rather than marking a sudden beginning, the Viking Age increasingly appears to have developed from earlier patterns of maritime warfare, diplomacy and mobility already visible by the mid-eighth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uu.se/en/research/the-viking-phenomenon/research-clusters/boat-grave-culture/salme" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uu.se">[uu.se]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uu.se</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uppsala University Salme – Uppsala University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uppsala University Salme – Uppsala University</span></span></span>

For Estonia, the site also highlights Saaremaa's importance as a crossroads of Baltic politics. The island was not a remote frontier but part of an interconnected maritime world in which Scandinavian and eastern Baltic societies regularly encountered one another, sometimes peacefully and sometimes violently.

## What remains uncertain

Modern science has solved many aspects of the Salme mystery.

Researchers are confident that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the burials date to around AD 750;
* the dead were overwhelmingly Scandinavian, probably from central Sweden;
* many were elite warriors;
* several were closely related;
* they died in a violent encounter before being carefully buried in their ships.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519301278" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human remains, context, and place of origin for the Salme, Estonia, boat burials - ScienceDirectJune 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2020</span></span></span>

</div>

The crucial unanswered questions are historical rather than archaeological. No contemporary chronicle explains why the expedition sailed to Saaremaa, who their opponents were, whether the encounter was a raid, diplomatic mission or something else, or exactly who organised the remarkable ship burials. Those missing human decisions—not the physical evidence—are what continue to make the Salme warriors one of Estonia's most compelling historical mysteries.
