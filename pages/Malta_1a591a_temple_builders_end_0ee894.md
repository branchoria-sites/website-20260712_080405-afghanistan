## Introduction

The end of [Malta]({{ 'what-really-lies-behind-maltas-greatest/' | relative_url }})'s temple-building culture is one of the island's most enduring archaeological mysteries, but it is no longer viewed as the sudden disappearance of an entire people. Around the middle of the third millennium BC, construction of Malta's great megalithic temples ceased, long-established religious traditions ended, and the archaeological record changed dramatically. Within a few centuries, the islands were occupied by Bronze Age communities with different pottery, burial customs and settlement patterns. The central question is not whether change occurred—it clearly did—but why it happened and whether it reflected environmental collapse, migration, cultural transformation, or a combination of all three. Modern archaeology increasingly favours a complex explanation rather than a single catastrophic event, although important uncertainties remain about exactly how the transition unfolded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.771683/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”January 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 11, 2022</span></span></span>

## What changed after the Temple Period?

The most striking feature of the transition is the abrupt end of a society that had spent more than a thousand years constructing some of Europe's earliest monumental stone architecture. By about 2500 BC, no new megalithic temples were being built, and many existing ceremonial sites fell out of their original use. The elaborate artistic traditions associated with the Temple Period also disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.771683/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”January 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 11, 2022</span></span></span>

The archaeological evidence from the succeeding Bronze Age looks markedly different:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Monumental temples were no longer constructed.
* Cremation became common, replacing the earlier collective burial traditions.
* Pottery styles changed significantly.
* Metal objects, especially bronze, appeared.
* Settlements increasingly occupied naturally defensive locations, suggesting different priorities from the ceremonial landscapes of the Temple Period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarxien_Cemetery_phase" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tarxien Cemetery phase</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tarxien Cemetery phase</span></span></span>

</div>

One of the clearest pieces of evidence comes from the Tarxien temple complex itself. Excavations uncovered an Early Bronze Age cremation cemetery built within parts of the abandoned prehistoric temple. Rather than destroying the monuments completely, later communities reused portions of them for entirely different purposes. This discovery became so important that archaeologists named Malta's earliest Bronze Age cultural phase the **Tarxien Cemetery phase**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritagemalta.mt/news/a-new-guide-book-for-tarxien-temples/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagemalta.mt">[heritagemalta.mt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagemalta.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heritage Malta A new guide book for Tarxien Temples</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heritage MaltaA new guide book for Tarxien Temples - Heritage Malta...</span></span></span>

## Why does the archaeological break appear so dramatic?

For much of the twentieth century, archaeologists interpreted the evidence as showing that the temple builders had vanished and been replaced by an entirely different population. This view was strongly influenced by the excavations of Themistocles Zammit, who identified what appeared to be a sterile layer separating Temple Period deposits from Bronze Age remains at Tarxien.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/handle/123456789/7755" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: um.edu.mt">[L-Università ta&#x27; Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">um.edu.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.mt.</span></span></span>

More recent research has complicated that picture.

Improved radiocarbon dating and renewed excavation suggest that the transition may not have involved a long period during which Malta was completely abandoned. Instead, archaeologists increasingly recognise an intermediate phase—sometimes referred to as the **Thermi phase** because of distinctive pottery—that may bridge the final Temple Period and the earliest Bronze Age. If this interpretation is correct, cultural change may have been more gradual than earlier generations believed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.771683/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”January 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 11, 2022</span></span></span>

This does not eliminate the mystery. It simply changes the question from "Why did the temple builders disappear overnight?" to "How did a highly distinctive religious society evolve into something recognisably different over several centuries?"

## Climate, erosion and scarce resources

Environmental pressure remains one of the leading explanations for the end of the Temple Period.

Malta possesses limited freshwater supplies, thin soils and relatively little natural woodland. Maintaining a growing farming population on small limestone islands would always have been difficult. Archaeological surveys suggest that cultivation expanded onto increasingly marginal land during the final Temple Period, which may indicate rising pressure on available resources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.771683/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”January 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 11, 2022</span></span></span>

Researchers have proposed several interacting environmental problems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* progressive soil erosion following woodland clearance
* declining agricultural productivity
* reduced resilience during periods of drought
* increasing pressure on limited grazing and farmland

</div>

These problems need not have caused sudden collapse. Instead, they could gradually have undermined the economic foundation that supported the labour-intensive construction and maintenance of ceremonial monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.771683/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”January 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 11, 2022</span></span></span>

An additional possibility involves the **4.2-kiloyear climate event**, a period of widespread climatic instability affecting parts of the Mediterranean, Near East and North Africa around 2200 BC. Although this event occurred after the principal end of temple building, some researchers argue that broader climatic deterioration during the transition may have intensified existing environmental stresses and affected later stages of cultural change. The precise relationship remains debated because archaeological dating still contains uncertainties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.771683/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”January 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 11, 2022</span></span></span>

## Migration, collapse and cultural continuity

Another major debate concerns whether the Bronze Age communities represented newcomers from Sicily or descendants of the Temple Period population who adopted new customs.

The archaeological evidence certainly points to stronger links with Sicily during the Early Bronze Age. Pottery styles resemble those found across parts of southern Italy, while bronze technology and cremation practices also fit broader Mediterranean developments. This has long encouraged the idea that migrants introduced a new cultural tradition to Malta.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/handle/123456789/7755" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: um.edu.mt">[L-Università ta&#x27; Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">um.edu.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.mt.</span></span></span>

However, migration and continuity are not mutually exclusive.

Small groups of newcomers can introduce new technologies, burial customs and social practices without completely replacing the existing population. Likewise, local communities experiencing environmental and social stress may rapidly adopt foreign cultural influences. Modern archaeologists therefore tend to consider several overlapping possibilities:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* immigration from nearby Sicily;
* gradual cultural assimilation rather than violent conquest;
* local social reorganisation following economic decline;
* combinations of environmental stress and external influence.

</div>

The available evidence has not produced a definitive answer. Anthony Bonanno's reassessment of the Tarxien evidence concluded that replacement by a new population remained the stronger interpretation based on the evidence then available, while acknowledging that some finds hint at possible continuity and contact between the two periods. More recent chronological research has further strengthened arguments for at least some overlap between the final Temple Period and the earliest Bronze Age.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/handle/123456789/7755" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: um.edu.mt">[L-Università ta&#x27; Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">um.edu.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.mt.</span></span></span>

## What the Bronze Age reveals about Malta

The Bronze Age was not a cultural "dark age" following the Temple Period, but a fundamentally different society.

Instead of investing enormous communal effort in constructing ceremonial complexes, Bronze Age communities increasingly occupied defended settlements such as those associated with the Borġ in-Nadur phase. Their priorities appear to have shifted towards security, metallurgy and changing patterns of trade within the wider central Mediterranean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritagemalta.mt/explore/tas-silg-archaeological-complex/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagemalta.mt">[Heritage Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagemalta.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heritage Malta Tas-Silġ Archaeological Complex</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heritage MaltaTas-Silġ Archaeological Complex - Heritage Malta...</span></span></span>

Some prehistoric sacred places also remained important despite profound cultural change. At Tas-Silġ, for example, activity continued from the Temple Period into the Bronze Age before the site was successively adapted by Phoenician, Punic and Roman worshippers. This continuity of place, despite repeated changes in religion and population, illustrates that landscapes can retain significance even when the cultures using them transform completely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritagemalta.mt/explore/tas-silg-archaeological-complex/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagemalta.mt">[Heritage Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagemalta.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heritage Malta Tas-Silġ Archaeological Complex</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heritage MaltaTas-Silġ Archaeological Complex - Heritage Malta...</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery endures

The disappearance of Malta's temple-building culture remains mysterious because archaeologists lack written records and many key excavations took place before modern archaeological recording methods existed. Important evidence about chronology, environmental conditions and everyday settlement has therefore been lost permanently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.771683/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”January 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 11, 2022</span></span></span>

Several conclusions nevertheless command broad agreement:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The temple builders did not simply vanish without trace in a single catastrophic event.
* Monument construction ended before Bronze Age cultural traditions became established.
* Environmental stress probably contributed to social change.
* Connections with Sicily became stronger during the Bronze Age.
* Whether those changes involved population replacement, gradual migration, cultural adoption or a mixture of all three remains unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.771683/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[frontiersin.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”January 11, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 11, 2022</span></span></span>

</div>

The enduring fascination of the mystery lies not in fantasies of a lost civilisation but in the challenge of explaining how one of prehistoric Europe's most remarkable monument-building societies transformed into something entirely different on a small group of islands with limited natural resources. It is a reminder that profound cultural change can leave dramatic traces in the archaeological record while still resisting any single, simple explanation.
