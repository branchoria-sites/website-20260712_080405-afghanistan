## Introduction

These cases share a practical difficulty. The archipelago is vast, heavily forested and seismically active, while many events occurred before sustained written record-keeping. Colonial accounts are incomplete and often ignored local knowledge; tropical conditions destroy wood, paper and human remains; earthquakes move coastlines; and deep water conceals ships and aircraft. As a result, oral histories, archaeology, archives and modern scientific surveys must often be read together. The best evidence usually narrows the possibilities without producing a single dramatic solution.

## Did an inhabited island disappear beneath the sea?

One of the Solomon Islands’ strangest traditions describes [Teonimenu]({{ 'teonimenu/' | relative_url }}), also called Teonimanu: an island said to have once stood between Ulawa and the Olu Malau islands in what is now Makira-Ulawa Province. According to versions preserved on several neighbouring islands, Teonimenu was overwhelmed by enormous waves and sank, leaving only a few survivors to escape by canoe.

The traditional story gives the disaster a human and supernatural cause. A woman left her husband for another man on Teonimenu, after which the abandoned husband obtained a powerful wave curse and used it to destroy the island. Details vary between communities, as would be expected after generations of oral transmission. Yet the central elements remain remarkably consistent: Teonimenu was inhabited, it disappeared suddenly, destructive waves accompanied the event, and refugees reached nearby islands. Some families continue to identify themselves as descendants of those survivors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-05-08/the-long-lost-island-still-visible-from-space/103740538" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[abc.net.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scientists have worked out what really happened</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsThe story of Teonimenu, a lost island of the PacificMay 7, 2024 — 7 May 2024 — Legend has it a jealous husband used a wave curse...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2024</span></span></span>

The curse cannot be tested historically, but the physical setting can. Near the position described in the traditions lies Lark Shoal, a shallow submerged feature on a steep undersea ridge beside the Cape Johnson Trench. The surrounding seabed is tectonically unstable, with slopes descending thousands of metres into an active zone where earthquakes can trigger enormous submarine landslides. Research led by geographer Patrick Nunn has argued that a strong earthquake may have caused part of the ridge, including an inhabited island, to collapse beneath the water. The resulting landslide and tsunami would explain why the remembered catastrophe involved both sudden subsidence and overwhelming waves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-05-08/the-long-lost-island-still-visible-from-space/103740538" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scientists have worked out what really happened</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsThe story of Teonimenu, a lost island of the PacificMay 7, 2024 — 7 May 2024 — Legend has it a jealous husband used a wave curse...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2024</span></span></span>

This is a plausible explanation, not a proven reconstruction. No securely dated settlement has been excavated beneath Lark Shoal, and scientists cannot yet establish the island’s dimensions, population or exact date of destruction. It is also possible that erosion, subsidence and repeated earthquakes acted together rather than in one instantaneous collapse.

Nevertheless, Teonimenu is more than a generic legend about a lost land. The consistency of the geographical clues, the existence of descendant traditions and the unstable submerged ridge make it reasonable to suspect that the story preserves a real disaster. Its deeper importance is methodological: a narrative once treated primarily as myth may contain observations of an earthquake, landslide and tsunami that occurred before written scientific records.

## Why was Tetepare abandoned?

Tetepare, in Western Province, is one of the largest uninhabited islands in the South Pacific. Its rainforest, rivers and reefs can give the impression of a landscape untouched by people, but descendants’ accounts and physical remains show that communities once lived there. Old garden sites, defensive features, burial places and settlement traces survive beneath the vegetation.

The island appears to have been abandoned during the nineteenth century, probably around the middle of it. The descendants of Tetepare’s inhabitants now live mainly on neighbouring islands, including Rendova and New Georgia. The central mystery is not whether people left, but why an entire inhabited island ceased to support a permanent population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tetepare.org/about-tetepare-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tetepare.org">[Tetepare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tetepare.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">about tetepare island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About Tetepare IslandAround 1860, the people of Tetepare abandoned their island home in a mass exodus. There are three potential...</span></span></span>

Three broad explanations recur in oral histories:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Epidemic disease:** Some accounts describe widespread sickness, sometimes understood through the spiritual language of harmful sea forces or sorcery. Introduced infections could spread rapidly through communities with little previous exposure, although no contemporary medical record identifies a particular outbreak.
* **Headhunting and external attack:** Nineteenth-century Western Solomon Islands experienced raiding and warfare. Tetepare’s population may have dispersed because defending a large, exposed island became too dangerous.
* **Internal conflict:** Fighting between Tetepare groups may have divided the population and encouraged families to seek protection among relatives elsewhere.

</div>

These explanations are not mutually exclusive. Disease could have reduced the population, internal disputes could have weakened collective defence, and external raiding could have made permanent resettlement unattractive. Oral traditions may also preserve different experiences from separate communities rather than corrupted versions of one simple event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tetepare.org/about-tetepare-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tetepare.org">[Tetepare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tetepare.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">about tetepare island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About Tetepare IslandAround 1860, the people of Tetepare abandoned their island home in a mass exodus. There are three potential...</span></span></span>

The absence of a decisive written account is unsurprising. British authority in the region was limited when the abandonment occurred, and outsiders rarely documented internal movements unless they affected missionaries, traders or colonial officials. Archaeology could potentially establish when individual settlements were last occupied, but it would be far harder to distinguish death from disease, flight from warfare and planned migration from hurried evacuation.

Tetepare’s empty status later gave the mystery an unexpected modern consequence. Because no permanent settlement reclaimed the island, extensive forest and coastal ecosystems survived. Descendant communities organised to resist commercial logging and created the Tetepare Descendants’ Association, linking the protection of biodiversity with ancestral identity, burial grounds and customary ownership. The island’s historical puzzle is therefore not merely about disappearance; it continues to shape debates over who speaks for Tetepare and what should happen to it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-05-23/tetepare-solomon-islands-mystery-spirits-conservation/103879558" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">It remains a mystery as to why, and accounts of the exodus differ.Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsTetepare Island&#x27;s people fled for mysterious reasons 200...22 May 2024 — Tetepare was once home to tribes of people, but it was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2024</span></span></span>

Claims that Tetepare was abandoned overnight or solely because of supernatural terror go beyond the evidence. The more responsible conclusion is that a genuine population dispersal occurred, probably under severe pressure, but its causes survive in several overlapping traditions rather than one verifiable account.

## What happened to the last survivors of La Pérouse?

In 1785, France sent Jean-François de Galaup, comte de La Pérouse, on an ambitious scientific voyage around the world. His two ships, *Boussole* and *Astrolabe*, carried naval officers, sailors, scientists and artists. They departed Botany Bay in March 1788 and then vanished from European knowledge.

For decades, the expedition’s fate became one of Europe’s great maritime mysteries. Search voyages found no conclusive trace. The decisive evidence eventually came from Vanikoro, now part of the Solomon Islands’ Temotu Province. In 1827–28, the Irish-born trader and explorer Peter Dillon followed reports of European objects on Pacific islands and connected them with wreckage at Vanikoro. Later investigations confirmed that both French ships had been lost there. Archaeological expeditions have recovered anchors, instruments, weapons, ceramics and other objects from the wreck sites and associated land areas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aima-underwater.org.au/app/download/files/get/aima-sp11laperouse.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aima-underwater.org.au">[aima-underwater.org.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aima-underwater.org.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aima sp11laperouse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Laperouse and the loss of the Astrolabe and the Boussole (...The 1986 Laperouse expedition: A report on investigations of two French shi...</span></span></span>

The broad outline is now well established. The expedition encountered a cyclone or severe storm near Vanikoro. One ship appears to have struck a reef and broken apart, while the other was wrecked in a separate location. Some people survived the initial disaster and established a camp ashore.

What happened next remains uncertain. Vanikoro traditions recorded that survivors built a vessel from wreckage and eventually sailed away. Other expedition members may have remained on the island, whether through choice, injury or inability to board. Archaeological work has identified areas connected with survivor activity, but it has not established a complete list of who lived through the wrecks or how long they stayed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aima-underwater.org.au/app/download/files/get/aima-sp11laperouse.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aima-underwater.org.au">[aima-underwater.org.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aima-underwater.org.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aima sp11laperouse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Laperouse and the loss of the Astrolabe and the Boussole (...The 1986 Laperouse expedition: A report on investigations of two French shi...</span></span></span>

The fate of the improvised vessel is the central unresolved question. It may have been lost soon after leaving Vanikoro, reached another island without leaving a recognisable record, or travelled far enough for its survivors to be absorbed into another community. Reports collected during later searches occasionally suggested that Europeans had lived elsewhere in the region, but none has conclusively identified La Pérouse or his officers.

The case also demonstrates the historical value of local testimony. European investigators spent decades searching a huge ocean, while people at Vanikoro retained accounts of wrecks, fighting, survivor camps and a departure by boat. Those accounts were not exact passenger manifests or navigational logs, but they preserved the essential location and sequence of events. Artefacts later moved between communities, complicating attempts to determine precisely where particular objects had been recovered, yet their circulation also helped direct outsiders towards the truth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/40287720" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

La Pérouse’s disappearance has therefore been partly solved. The ships’ destination is no longer unknown, and the wreck sites are identified. The enduring mystery concerns the human aftermath: how many survived, what relations they formed with Vanikoro communities, who departed aboard the makeshift vessel, and where that final voyage ended.

## How were the Solomon Islands lost on European maps?

The country’s name comes from a geographical misunderstanding. In 1568, an expedition led by Álvaro de Mendaña crossed the Pacific from Peru and reached several islands in the archipelago, including Santa Isabel, Guadalcanal and Makira. Reports of gold encouraged a connection with the biblical wealth of King Solomon, producing a name that promised far more mineral riches than the expedition had actually established.

Mendaña’s navigators recorded the voyage, but sixteenth-century longitude calculations were highly unreliable. Pacific distances were difficult to measure, charts were inconsistent, and reported positions could be distorted further when copied. When later Spanish expeditions attempted to return, they struggled to match Mendaña’s descriptions with islands encountered at sea. The Solomon Islands had not physically vanished, but European navigators effectively lost their location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static-prod.lib.princeton.edu/visual_materials/maps/websites/pacific/mendana-queiros/mendana-queiros.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static-prod.lib.princeton.edu">[Princeton Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static-prod.lib.princeton.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on princeton.edu.</span></span></span>

Mendaña returned to the region in 1595 but did not successfully re-establish contact with the principal islands visited in 1568. Instead, his expedition founded a short-lived settlement at Gracioso Bay on Nendö in the Santa Cruz group. Disease, internal division and violent relations with local inhabitants devastated the colony. Mendaña died, and the settlement was abandoned under the command of Pedro Fernandes de Queirós.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_Islands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Solomon Islands</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Solomon Islands</span></span></span>

For roughly two centuries, European maps contained confused, displaced or speculative versions of the archipelago. Eighteenth-century voyages gradually identified its islands again, but matching new observations with the Spanish records was not straightforward. Different navigators gave existing places new names, while supposed islands sometimes appeared twice under separate identities.

Calling this a “lost civilisation” would be misleading. Solomon Islanders continued to live, travel and trade throughout the period. It was European geographical knowledge that failed. The episode matters because it reverses a familiar exploration story: the islands were not awaiting discovery, and they were never lost to the people who knew their waters. They were lost within an imported system of charts whose measurements were too imprecise to guide later ships back reliably.

## Why are so many wartime dead still missing?

The Solomon Islands campaign of 1942–45 left a landscape crowded with unresolved wartime histories. Guadalcanal, Tulagi, New Georgia and the surrounding seas saw prolonged fighting involving ground forces, warships and hundreds of aircraft. Iron Bottom Sound received its name from the concentration of vessels sunk in and around the waters north of Guadalcanal.

Many wrecks are known and visited by divers, but others were poorly located, misidentified or buried beneath sediment. Aircraft disappeared into jungle, swamp or deep sea. Temporary battlefield graves were lost when markers decayed, units moved or later fighting altered the ground. Japanese and Allied records were themselves incomplete, and some locations were described using wartime maps that do not align neatly with present landscapes. The wider campaign cost tens of thousands of lives and hundreds of ships and aircraft, ensuring that many individual losses could not be investigated immediately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/solomon-islands-campaign-guadalcanal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalww2museum.org">[National WWII Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalww2museum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National WWII Museum The Solomon Islands Campaign: Guadalcanal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National WWII Museum The Solomon Islands Campaign: Guadalcanal</span></span></span>

Modern work treats these not as romantic treasure hunts but as missing-person investigations. On Guadalcanal, teams working with the United States Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency have used archival research, local testimony and archaeological survey to search for American servicemen whose bodies were never recovered. One recent project focused on 17 missing personnel associated with a particular battle area. Investigators must separate wartime deposits from later disturbance and establish whether human remains, equipment and unit records belong to the same event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.binghamton.edu/news/story/4635/missing-in-action-research-in-guadalcanal-aims-to-bring-servicemen-home-to-the-u.s" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: binghamton.edu">[Binghamton University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">binghamton.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on binghamton.edu.</span></span></span>

Local knowledge is often essential. Residents may know of aircraft fragments, isolated graves or areas where fighting occurred, even when official coordinates are inaccurate. Yet memories can combine several incidents, and wreck material has sometimes been moved, reused or sold. Researchers therefore compare testimony with military reports, aerial photographs, serial numbers, dental records and DNA evidence.

The search continues for Japanese personnel as well. More than eight decades after the campaign, international and local organisations remain involved in locating remains and reducing the dangers posed by unexploded ordnance. The latter creates an additional problem: a suspected burial or crash site can contain live shells, grenades or bombs, making ordinary archaeological excavation unsafe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.halotrust.org/news/halo-helps-search-for-long-lost-victims-of-ww2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: halotrust.org">[The HALO Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">halotrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The HALO Trust Firsthand accounts from HALO Ukraine staff</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The HALO Trust Firsthand accounts from HALO Ukraine staff</span></span></span>

New underwater technology is also revising what is known about the naval battlefield. Remotely operated vehicles and high-resolution sonar can inspect depths beyond normal diving limits, confirm wreck identities and reveal damage patterns. A 2025 survey in the Guadalcanal area revisited known vessels and documented wrecks that had not previously been examined in detail. Such discoveries may explain how a ship sank, but they do not always settle the fate of every person aboard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/shipwrecks-wwii-guadalcanal-warships" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">shipwrecks wwii guadalcanal warships</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">shipwrecks wwii guadalcanal warships</span></span></span>

## What these mysteries have in common

The Solomon Islands’ unresolved histories repeatedly expose the limits of any single kind of evidence. Written archives may provide dates and names but overlook island communities. Oral traditions may preserve locations and disasters while expressing causes through moral or spiritual narratives. Archaeology can confirm occupation, wreckage and destruction, but not always motive. Geology can show how an island might collapse without proving exactly when people witnessed it.

The strongest interpretations come from convergence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Teonimenu becomes historically credible when oral accounts of sudden waves are compared with an unstable submerged ridge.
* Tetepare’s abandonment is confirmed by settlement remains and descendant memory, even though no source decisively chooses between disease, warfare and internal conflict.
* Vanikoro archaeology verifies the wreck of La Pérouse’s ships, while local traditions preserve the otherwise undocumented survival and departure of some crew members.
* Wartime archives identify missing units and aircraft, but field investigations and community knowledge are needed to locate them.

</div>

This approach also helps separate genuine uncertainty from modern embellishment. There is no good evidence that Teonimenu belonged to an advanced sunken civilisation, that Tetepare conceals a vanished city in the usual sensational sense, or that the La Pérouse expedition discovered a secret destination. These additions are unnecessary. The documented mysteries are already significant because they involve real communities, identifiable disasters and evidence that remains incomplete.

## Why the unresolved past still matters

These mysteries are part of living public memory rather than sealed historical puzzles. Teonimenu’s traditions carry knowledge of extreme natural hazards in a country still exposed to earthquakes and tsunamis. Tetepare’s abandoned settlements underpin modern claims of descent, land responsibility and conservation. Vanikoro connects Solomon Islands history with French exploration, underwater archaeology and debates over the ownership and movement of recovered artefacts. The wartime missing remain the relatives of families in several countries, while unexploded weapons continue to threaten Solomon Islands communities.

They also challenge an older habit of treating Pacific history as a sequence of European arrivals. In several of the country’s best-known mysteries, outsiders possessed ships, written records and official expeditions, yet local people retained the information that ultimately made sense of the evidence. The islands Mendaña’s successors could not find were never missing to their inhabitants. The fate of La Pérouse’s ships endured in Vanikoro memory. Stories of Teonimenu may have preserved the anatomy of a geological catastrophe for centuries.

The most defensible answers are therefore measured ones. Teonimenu probably reflects a real vanished island, although its destruction has not been archaeologically dated. Tetepare was deliberately or forcibly depopulated, but likely through several pressures rather than one dramatic cause. La Pérouse’s ships were wrecked at Vanikoro, while the final voyage of some survivors remains unknown. The Solomon Islands campaign is extensively documented, yet individual ships, aircraft and human remains are still being located. In each case, the mystery survives not because there is no evidence, but because the surviving evidence stops just before the final answer.
