## Were the stones of Green Castle Hill deliberately erected?

Green Castle Hill rises above Antigua’s central plain, with wide views towards the western coast. Its summit and slopes contain upright and angular volcanic rocks commonly described as “megaliths”. The site has consequently acquired the nickname “the Stonehenge of the Caribbean”, while individual formations have been labelled as altars, seats, standing stones or astronomical markers. The central question is whether Indigenous inhabitants deliberately moved and arranged these rocks, adapted natural formations for ceremonial purposes, or simply occupied a hill whose unusual geology already gave it a striking appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archaeologyantigua.org/index.php/green-castle-hill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeologyantigua.org">[Archaeology Antigua]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeologyantigua.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archaeology AntiguaGreen Castle HillOn the summit and slopes of this hill, are unusual standing rock formations referred to by Antiguans...</span></span></span>

There is no serious doubt that Green Castle Hill has genuine archaeological importance. Excavations found evidence of Ceramic Age occupation, broadly dated to around AD 900–1200. The elevated position, commanding views and surviving cultural material establish that pre-Columbian people used the hill. What the finds do not automatically prove is that the largest stones were quarried, transported or raised by those inhabitants. Association is not the same as construction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sta.uwi.edu/ojs/index.php/hia/article/download/1110/985/2261" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sta.uwi.edu">[UWI St Augustine Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sta.uwi.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Augustine Journals Columbian Inhabitants of Greencastle Hill in Antigua</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Greencastle Hill on the island of Antigua indicated that Greencastle Hill was inhabited by a. The more ambitious interpretation proposes that some stones formed a ceremonial or astronomical complex. Researchers and local heritage presentations have suggested alignments with celestial events or constellations, and earlier writers described supposed altars and “sacrificial slabs”. These claims are intriguing because sky observation, seasonal knowledge and ritual landscapes were important in many Indigenous Caribbean societies. Yet an alignment selected after examining many irregular stones is weaker evidence than a clearly engineered structure with demonstrable construction features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oas.org/en/sedi/DSD/Biodiversity/Sustainable_Cities/Sustainable_Communities/Events/SC%20Course%20Antigua%20and%20Barbuda%202016/Module%20VI/Presentations/Murphy%20Heritage%20of%20Antigua%20and%20Barbuda.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oas.org">[Organization of American States]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research by UWI shows the major stones align with Little Dipper on the Solstice. Geology provides a less dramatic but powerful explanation. Green Castle Hill is part of Antigua’s ancient volcanic landscape. Weathering and erosion can expose resistant volcanic masses after softer surrounding material has worn away, leaving upright slabs, blocks and angular forms that appear intentionally placed. This would explain why the “megaliths” do not consistently display sockets, worked faces, transport traces or the organised architectural plan expected at an unmistakably human-built stone monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.internationalparks.org/antigua-and-barbuda/Green%20Castle%20Hill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: internationalparks.org">[International Parks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">internationalparks.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Parks Green Castle Hill, Antigua and Barbuda (National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International ParksGreen Castle Hill, Antigua and Barbuda (National Park)February 15, 2026 — The so-called megaliths on the hilltop are l...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 15, 2026</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusion separates three propositions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Human occupation is established.** Indigenous people lived on or used the hill.
* **Cultural meaning is plausible.** Naturally prominent stones may have served as landmarks, gathering places, observation points or sacred features.
* **Large-scale construction remains unproved.** Present evidence does not securely demonstrate that the principal formations were erected as an Antiguan equivalent of Stonehenge.

</div>

The mystery therefore survives in a more subtle form. The question is probably not whether an unknown civilisation built a vast megalithic temple, but how pre-Columbian people understood and used an already extraordinary natural landscape. That distinction protects the site from exaggerated claims while preserving the genuinely unresolved relationship between geology, ritual and human occupation.

## What was the ship hidden beneath English Harbour?

In Tank Bay, within the English Harbour area, archaeologists encountered the remains of an exceptionally large wooden vessel beneath shallow water and sediment. The hull was more than 40 metres long, with substantial surviving timbers and architectural components. Its size, construction and location were surprising because Antigua’s naval dockyard is one of the Caribbean’s best-documented colonial maritime sites, yet researchers initially found no obvious British record explaining why such a ship had been left there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiguaobserver.com/archaeologists-hail-discovery-of-colossal-centuries-old-ship-in-dockyard/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiguaobserver.com">[antiguaobserver.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiguaobserver.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">archaeologists hail discovery of colossal centuries old ship in dockyard</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">archaeologists hail discovery of colossal centuries old ship in dockyard</span></span></span>

Early investigation placed the vessel in the eighteenth century. Its construction appeared inconsistent with a routine small harbour craft, and archaeologists considered whether it might be a rare surviving French East India Company ship. Chinese porcelain fragments and other evidence connected it to the long-distance commercial world linking Europe, Africa, India and East Asia. Unlike a romantic treasure wreck, its value lies chiefly in the surviving hull, which can reveal how large trading and naval ships were built and modified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeology.org/news/2021/06/24/210625-antigua-wooden-hull/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeology.org">[archaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Magazine News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Magazine News</span></span></span>

Research has since produced a persuasive identification. The ship is now associated with the *Beaumont*, constructed at Lorient in 1762 for the French East India Company. After the company’s decline, it entered French naval service and was later sold into private ownership and renamed *Lyon*. Historical work indicates that the vessel eventually reached Antigua and was incorporated into British dockyard use before being deliberately sunk or abandoned at Tank Bay in the late eighteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://event.fourwaves.com/iaca2024/abstracts/dc7e14c1-0bac-442d-b378-d51dc75b076d" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: event.fourwaves.com">[Fourwaves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">event.fourwaves.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fourwaves.com.</span></span></span>

This is an example of a mystery being narrowed rather than instantly solved. Several factors explain why the wreck remained unidentified for so long. Ships could change names, owners and functions; captured vessels might be reused without retaining a neat archival identity; and a worn-out hull could be stripped of fittings before disposal. If the ship became a stationary store, workshop or harbour structure, officials may no longer have treated it as an active vessel worth recording in the same way as a commissioned warship.

The *Beaumont–Lyon* identification is strengthened by the convergence of construction evidence, artefacts and archival research. Even so, researchers must still establish the precise sequence of alterations and the circumstances of the hull’s deposition. Was it intentionally sunk as part of dockyard engineering, converted into a fixed facility, or simply discarded after everything useful had been removed? Those questions matter because a ship’s final years can be as historically revealing as its celebrated voyages.

The case also overturns the popular expectation that a mysterious wreck must contain gold, weapons or sealed cargo. The important survival is the vessel itself. If the identification holds, Tank Bay preserves an unusually substantial example of a French East India Company ship whose career passed through commerce, warfare, private ownership and British colonial reuse. Its obscurity resulted not from a cover-up but from an object losing its name as its purpose changed.

## Why was Governor Daniel Parke killed without a clear reckoning?

On 7 December 1710, an armed uprising in Antigua ended with the killing of Daniel Parke, captain-general and governor of the British Leeward Islands. Militiamen and colonists attacked the governor’s residence in St John’s, overcame its defenders, dragged Parke outside and killed him. The broad sequence is established by colonial correspondence and later historical accounts. What remains contentious is how blame should be divided between an authoritarian governor, rival planters, militia leaders and imperial officials who failed to resolve the crisis before violence erupted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/168084/1/WRAP_Theses_Qing_2021.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wrap.warwick.ac.uk">[warwick.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wrap.warwick.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on warwick.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Parke arrived in Antigua in 1706 after military service in Europe. His administration soon became entangled in disputes over property, trade, appointments, smuggling and the powers of the local assembly. His opponents submitted formal complaints accusing him of abusive and unlawful conduct. Parke denied their allegations in correspondence sent to London, portraying himself as a defender of royal authority against self-interested colonists. The surviving documents are therefore not neutral descriptions: they are arguments written by people attempting to influence imperial decision-makers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.british-history.ac.uk/cal-state-papers/colonial/america-west-indies/vol25/pp185-214" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: british-history.ac.uk">[British History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">british-history.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pp185 214</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pp185 214</span></span></span>

Personal hostility deepened the political conflict. Parke quarrelled with members of Antigua’s planter and merchant elite, and reports about his private conduct became inseparable from constitutional grievances. He survived earlier threats against his life, used soldiers to reinforce his authority and resisted demands that he leave the island. By late 1710, disputes over defence, the assembly and the militia had created a situation in which each side could claim that the other endangered Antigua.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Parke" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Daniel Parke</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daniel Parke</span></span></span>

The attack itself was not an unexplained murder by an unknown assailant. Numerous participants and organisers were identifiable. The deeper puzzle is why so little punishment followed. Papers concerning the killers, requests to bring them to justice and petitions for a general pardon reached imperial authorities. Parke’s successor, Walter Douglas, eventually became associated with allegations that money had been demanded before pardons were issued. The aftermath transformed a public rebellion into a confused process of negotiation, pardon and institutional self-protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.british-history.ac.uk/cal-state-papers/colonial/america-west-indies/vol26/v-xliii" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: british-history.ac.uk">[british-history.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">british-history.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on british-history.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Three interpretations compete.

**Parke as the principal author of the crisis.** In this view, his confrontational rule, disregard for local institutions and personal feuds made resistance inevitable. This explains the breadth of hostility, but it risks turning murder into an unavoidable popular verdict.

**A planter-led defence of local power.** Antigua’s elite had strong economic and political interests of its own. Opposition to Parke may therefore have combined legitimate grievances with resistance to external regulation, investigations and royal interference.

**An imperial failure.** London received complaints but operated slowly across the Atlantic. Orders, testimony and appointments took months to travel. By the time metropolitan authorities could act, local confrontation had already become armed rebellion.

The evidence supports elements of all three. Parke appears to have governed aggressively and accumulated dangerous enemies. His opponents nevertheless chose organised violence, and the subsequent lack of accountability suggests that local influence could obstruct imperial justice. The unresolved element is not who struck the fatal blows so much as how a colonial political system absorbed the killing of its senior royal representative without producing a transparent trial and authoritative public record.

Parke’s death matters because it exposes the unstable balance beneath British rule in the early eighteenth-century Caribbean. Colonial government was neither a simple chain of command from London nor a peaceful partnership with local assemblies. It depended on bargaining among governors, soldiers, merchants and plantation owners within a society sustained by slavery and coercion. When that bargaining collapsed, even a governor could be killed—and the archive later shaped by people with reasons to soften, redirect or suppress responsibility.

## Was Redonda ever an independent kingdom?

Redonda is a steep, small and historically uninhabited island belonging to Antigua and Barbuda. Its physical isolation made it an ideal setting for one of the Caribbean’s most durable literary legends: the supposed Kingdom of Redonda. The story is associated with fantasy writer Matthew Phipps Shiel, born on nearby Montserrat in 1865. Shiel later claimed that his father had asserted possession of Redonda and that, at the age of 15, he was crowned its king by a bishop during a ceremony on the island.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Redonda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kingdom of Redonda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kingdom of Redonda</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that Shiel did not publish the account until 1929, almost half a century after the alleged coronation. No contemporary royal charter, Colonial Office recognition or independent record has emerged to confirm that Britain accepted a sovereign Redondan monarchy. Versions of the story also differ over the cleric who supposedly conducted the ceremony. These are serious problems for anyone treating the claim as constitutional history rather than imaginative autobiography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Redonda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kingdom of Redonda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kingdom of Redonda</span></span></span>

Redonda was not an unused blank space waiting for a private king. Britain treated the island as a dependency within its Leeward Islands possessions, and commercial guano extraction took place there during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. A private family celebration, even had one occurred, could not by itself create internationally recognised sovereignty over land already claimed and administered by an imperial power.

The legend nevertheless developed a remarkable afterlife. Shiel passed his supposed title to the poet John Gawsworth, who created Redondan noble titles for writers and acquaintances. Competing successions followed, producing several claimants to the throne. The Spanish novelist Javier Marías later embraced one branch of the tradition and granted playful honours to cultural figures. Redonda thus became a literary realm whose influence existed in books, friendships and imagination rather than government, law or territorial control.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ideasimagination.columbia.edu/news/redonda-s-rulers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ideasimagination.columbia.edu">[Institute for Ideas and Imagination]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ideasimagination.columbia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute for Ideas and Imagination Redonda&#x27;s Rulers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute for Ideas and Imagination Redonda&#x27;s Rulers</span></span></span>

The best assessment is therefore straightforward:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Redonda is real and forms part of Antigua and Barbuda.
* Shiel’s account of a childhood coronation is uncorroborated.
* There is no persuasive evidence that a legally independent kingdom existed.
* The later succession is a cultural and literary game, although one with a surprisingly serious body of collectors, writers and rival claimants.

</div>

Calling the tale a fabrication can still miss its historical interest. The Kingdom of Redonda illustrates how invented tradition takes hold. A remote island supplied the setting; Shiel supplied a retrospective founding story; Gawsworth created a circle of aristocratic writers; and later participants preserved the fiction through documents, titles and rival genealogies. Repetition then gave the legend an appearance of antiquity that its evidential foundations do not possess.

## What these mysteries reveal about Antigua and Barbuda

These cases occupy different positions on the scale between established fact and modern myth. The Tank Bay wreck is a genuine archaeological identification problem that archival work is steadily resolving. Daniel Parke’s death is a documented political killing whose motives and aftermath remain contested because the records are partisan and accountability failed. Green Castle Hill combines certain Indigenous occupation with uncertain claims about human-built megaliths. Redonda’s kingdom is the reverse: a weak historical claim that became culturally powerful precisely because writers continued to elaborate it.

The distinction matters. Treating every uncertainty as proof of conspiracy makes the real historical questions harder to see. At Green Castle Hill, the useful question concerns Indigenous engagement with a natural formation, not a vanished race of master builders. At English Harbour, the mystery lies in a ship’s changing identity and use, not missing treasure. In Parke’s case, the puzzle is institutional impunity rather than an unknown killer. On Redonda, the subject is the creation of tradition rather than hidden sovereignty.

Antigua and Barbuda’s historical enigmas endure because the country sits at the meeting point of Indigenous history, Atlantic slavery, imperial warfare, global trade and literary invention. Its archives preserve official voices more readily than those of enslaved people, labourers and Indigenous communities. Its tropical seas conceal timber beneath sediment, while its exposed landscapes encourage later generations to reinterpret natural forms. The most compelling mysteries are therefore not those with the wildest explanation, but those in which a surviving place or object forces the evidence to be read again.

The four cases also show that solving a mystery rarely means eliminating all uncertainty. Identification may replace one question with another. An excavated site can prove occupation without revealing belief. A murder can be historically certain while justice remains obscure. A legend can be false as political history yet entirely real as cultural memory. That tension—between what happened, what can be proved and what people chose to remember—is the central mystery running through Antigua and Barbuda’s past.
