## Introduction

The mystery of the Dahlak tombstones is not that they are unreadable. On the contrary, hundreds of medieval Arabic funerary inscriptions from [Eritrea]({{ 'what-secrets-still-survive-in-eritrea/' | relative_url }})'s Dahlak Archipelago can still be read with remarkable clarity. The puzzle is that these stones preserve the names of rulers, merchants, scholars and travellers from a prosperous Muslim society whose wider political history has largely disappeared. The inscriptions provide dates, family relationships, places of origin and even the names of stone carvers, yet they reveal only fragments of the story behind the medieval Dahlak Sultanate. As a result, historians must reconstruct the islands' past from scattered clues in cemeteries, ruined coral-stone settlements, foreign travel accounts and a small number of surviving written records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islamicworld.britishmuseum.org/collection/RRM12373/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islamicworld.britishmuseum.org">[islamicworld.britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islamicworld.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The British Museum: Islamic Gallery...</span></span></span>

## A medieval trading society in the Red Sea

Between roughly the ninth and sixteenth centuries, the Dahlak Archipelago off the Eritrean coast occupied a strategic position on one of the world's busiest maritime routes. Ships travelling between Egypt, Arabia, East Africa and the wider Indian Ocean regularly passed through the Red Sea, making the islands an important stopping point for trade, water and shelter.

Archaeological remains show that Dahlak was much more than a collection of fishing villages. Excavations have uncovered coral-stone buildings, mosques, cisterns, harbours and extensive cemeteries. These remains point to a settled maritime community closely connected with the commercial networks linking the Islamic world with northeast Africa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0001_1975_num" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: persee.fr">[Persée]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">persee.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Persée Stèles funéraires musulmanes des Iles Dahlak (Mer Rouge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PerséeStèles funéraires musulmanes des Iles Dahlak (Mer Rouge) - Persée...</span></span></span>

The prosperity of the islands depended on several activities:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Maritime trade between Africa, Arabia and the wider Indian Ocean.
* Pearl fishing and the harvesting of marine resources.
* Customs duties levied on passing merchants.
* Political links with larger regional powers, including rulers in Yemen and the Ethiopian Highlands.

</div>

Rather than existing in isolation, Dahlak functioned as a crossroads where merchants, pilgrims and officials from many regions met before continuing their journeys across the Red Sea.

## Rulers, merchants and lives recorded in stone

The cemetery inscriptions are among the richest collections of medieval Arabic epigraphy in sub-Saharan Africa. Hundreds of tombstones have survived, many carved from local basalt and decorated with carefully executed Arabic script.

Their importance lies not simply in recording deaths but in preserving details that rarely survive elsewhere. The inscriptions frequently include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Full personal names extending through several generations.
* Titles indicating political or religious status.
* Dates of death, allowing historians to build chronological sequences.
* Places of origin, revealing long-distance migration.
* Qur'anic quotations and prayers.
* Occasionally, the names of the craftsmen who carved the stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islamicworld.britishmuseum.org/collection/RRM12373/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islamicworld.britishmuseum.org">[islamicworld.britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islamicworld.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The British Museum: Islamic Gallery...</span></span></span>

</div>

One especially valuable gravestone in the British Museum commemorates Isa ibn Ahmad al-Makki, who died in 1188. Both the deceased and the stone carver identify themselves as originating from Mecca, demonstrating that skilled craftsmen and educated residents moved between Arabia and Dahlak. Even more unusually, the monument records the name of its calligrapher, Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Harami al-Makki, giving historians direct evidence for artistic production on the islands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islamicworld.britishmuseum.org/collection/RRM12373/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islamicworld.britishmuseum.org">[islamicworld.britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islamicworld.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The British Museum: Islamic Gallery...</span></span></span>

Another surviving stone commemorates Sheikh Makki ibn Jami al-Sawakini, who came from Sawakin on the Sudanese coast and died on Dahlak in 1210. Such inscriptions show that the islands attracted people from across the Red Sea rather than serving only the local population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museumtombstone &#124; British Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumtombstone &#124; British Museum</span></span></span>

The names recorded across the cemetery point towards connections stretching across the Islamic world. Individuals can be linked to places including Mecca, Sawakin and other regions identified through family names and geographical surnames, illustrating how closely Dahlak participated in medieval maritime networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.habeshahistory.com/p/the-dahlak-sultanate-a-medieval-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: habeshahistory.com">[habeshahistory.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">habeshahistory.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Dahlak Sultanate: A Medieval History (8th-16th AD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Dahlak Sultanate: A Medieval History (8th-16th AD</span></span></span>

## The missing history of the Dahlak Sultanate

Despite this abundance of inscriptions, the political history of the Dahlak Sultanate remains surprisingly incomplete.

The islands were ruled by local Muslim dynasties for centuries and appear regularly in the records of neighbouring states. Yet almost none of the sultanate's own administrative archives have survived. Unlike larger contemporary kingdoms, Dahlak left very few chronicles describing succession disputes, diplomacy or government.

Instead, historians must reconstruct the sultanate through indirect evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Funerary inscriptions.
* Archaeological remains.
* References in Egyptian, Yemeni and Ethiopian texts.
* Accounts by medieval geographers and travellers.

</div>

This creates an unusual situation. Scholars possess many individual names but only a limited understanding of how power was organised or how rulers governed the islands from one generation to the next.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0001_1975_num" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: persee.fr">[Persée]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">persee.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Persée Stèles funéraires musulmanes des Iles Dahlak (Mer Rouge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PerséeStèles funéraires musulmanes des Iles Dahlak (Mer Rouge) - Persée...</span></span></span>

The problem is made worse because surviving written references often describe Dahlak only when it interacted with larger regional powers. The islands appear during diplomatic exchanges, military campaigns or commercial disputes but disappear again once those events end. The everyday functioning of the sultanate remains largely hidden.

## Why do the tombstones leave so many questions unanswered?

Funerary inscriptions are exceptionally reliable for recording names and dates, but they were never intended to serve as political histories.

Most follow established Islamic commemorative formulas. They honour the deceased, ask for divine mercy and sometimes identify family relationships or occupations. They rarely explain wider historical events.

This means that historians often know:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Who died.**
* **When they died.**
* **Where they or their families came from.**

</div>

But they often cannot answer equally important questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* How were the sultans chosen?
* How much territory did they control beyond the islands?
* How independent were they from neighbouring powers?
* What caused periods of political change or decline?

</div>

The stones therefore illuminate individual lives while leaving broader political developments only partially visible.

## What the inscriptions reveal about medieval society

Although they cannot answer every historical question, the Dahlak tombstones offer remarkable insight into everyday life.

The inscriptions suggest a society with:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Skilled Arabic calligraphers.
* Religious scholars and judges.
* Wealthy merchant families.
* Long-distance migrants.
* Strong connections to centres of Islamic learning.

</div>

Their scripts also interest specialists in the history of Arabic writing. The monuments preserve examples of carefully carved medieval calligraphy whose dates are securely known, making them valuable reference points for studying the development of scripts such as *naskh*. Recent research has highlighted the importance of Dahlak's inscriptions both as historical documents and as evidence for the evolution of Islamic calligraphic traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://repository.sustech.edu/bitstream/handle/123456789/1335/%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D8%B3%D8%AA%D8%AE%D9%84%D8%B5.pdf?isAllowed=y&amp;sequence=9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.sustech.edu">[SUSTech Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.sustech.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SUSTech Repositoryﺑﺴﻢ اﷲ اﻟﺮﺣﻤﻦ اﻟﺮﺣﻴﻢMarch 4, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2026</span></span></span>

The cemeteries also demonstrate continuity. Rather than isolated burials, they represent generations of families living within an established urban community, reinforcing archaeological evidence that medieval Dahlak was a stable commercial centre rather than a temporary trading station.

## Why the mystery still matters

The Dahlak tombstones remind historians that surviving evidence can be both abundant and incomplete. Hundreds of inscriptions preserve precise personal details across several centuries, yet the larger narrative of the Dahlak Sultanate remains elusive because administrative records, royal archives and local chronicles have largely vanished.

This makes Dahlak one of Eritrea's most important archival mysteries. The islands preserve the voices of individuals but not the full story of the state they served. Each newly studied inscription helps fill another small gap, refining chronologies, identifying families and tracing connections across the Red Sea. Yet the central question remains unresolved: how exactly did this influential maritime sultanate develop, govern itself and eventually fade from history?
