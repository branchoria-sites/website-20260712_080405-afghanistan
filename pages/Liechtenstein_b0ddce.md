## Introduction

The strongest cases fall into five categories: the disputed identity of the “81st soldier” who returned from Liechtenstein’s final military deployment; the deliberately damaged record of the country’s witch persecutions; the uncertain meaning of the Iron Age bronze figures found below Gutenberg Castle; the fate of Russian soldiers who voluntarily left Liechtenstein for the Soviet Union in 1945; and the still-unsolved disappearance of Hedwig Rosa Beck in 2008. Some are genuine unresolved cases. Others show how a documented event can acquire a more mysterious life in popular memory than the surviving evidence warrants.

## Did Liechtenstein’s army really return with an extra soldier?

The best-known Liechtenstein mystery concerns the Austro-Prussian War of 1866. Liechtenstein supplied a contingent to the German Confederation, but Prince Johann II limited its mission to defending the Austrian Tyrol. The force, commanded by Peter Rheinberger, was stationed near the Stelvio Pass against a possible Italian advance and saw no fighting. When the war ended, the men marched home and received a ceremonial welcome in Vaduz. Two years later, with the German Confederation dissolved and the army increasingly unpopular and expensive, Liechtenstein abolished its military.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history_of_Liechtenstein" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Military history of Liechtenstein</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Military history of Liechtenstein</span></span></span>

The familiar version says that 80 men departed and 81 returned. In internet retellings, the additional man is often described as an Italian soldier or farmer whom the Liechtensteiners befriended. The joke-like conclusion — that the only army to gain a man during a war promptly disbanded — has helped make the story internationally famous.

The broad event is real, but the “mystery” has been exaggerated. Historical summaries identify the additional traveller more plausibly as an Austrian liaison officer who accompanied the contingent on its homeward march. A Liechtenstein-based account likewise states that the 81st man was an Austrian lieutenant, while other retellings preserve the rival tradition of an Italian companion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history_of_Liechtenstein" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Military history of Liechtenstein</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Military history of Liechtenstein</span></span></span>

Even the number 80 needs care. The military establishment had been organised at slightly more than 80 men, and two reservists reportedly remained at Vaduz Castle. “Eighty went and eighty-one returned” is therefore better understood as a polished national anecdote than as a formally unresolved personnel problem. No persuasive evidence suggests that an unknown man secretly joined the Liechtenstein army or remained in the country under a concealed identity.

The story still matters because it compresses Liechtenstein’s transformation into one memorable scene. Its last military mission was bloodless, its supposed extra soldier was friendly rather than threatening, and the army disappeared soon afterwards. The tale turns the end of military obligation into a national parable about neutrality, small-state pragmatism and good fortune.

## Why are the witch-trial records incomplete?

Liechtenstein’s darkest archival mystery concerns the persecution of alleged witches in the territories of Vaduz and Schellenberg. The first securely dated trials in Vaduz occurred in 1597, 1598 and 1600 and resulted in at least eleven recorded executions. Larger waves followed in the mid-seventeenth century and again during the 1670s and early 1680s. The Historical Dictionary of the Principality of Liechtenstein estimates that the major persecution of 1648–51 alone led to roughly 100 deaths.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historisches-lexikon.li/Hexenverfolgung" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historisches-lexikon.li">[Liechtenstein Lexicon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historisches-lexikon.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein LexiconHexenverfolgungDie ersten datierbaren Gerichtsverfahren gegen vermeintliche Hexen in Vaduz fanden 1597, 1598 und 160...</span></span></span>

What makes the history difficult to reconstruct is not merely the age of the documents. Relevant pages were removed from official record books, while other trial material was destroyed or lost. As a result, historians can identify phases of persecution and some individual victims but cannot establish a fully reliable total or reconstruct every prosecution. The gaps themselves are part of the case: somebody appears to have had a reason to eliminate evidence of how the trials were conducted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historisches-lexikon.li/Hexenverfolgung" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historisches-lexikon.li">[Liechtenstein Lexicon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historisches-lexikon.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein LexiconHexenverfolgungDie ersten datierbaren Gerichtsverfahren gegen vermeintliche Hexen in Vaduz fanden 1597, 1598 und 160...</span></span></span>

The surviving material indicates that accusations spread through familiar early-modern mechanisms: local quarrels, rumours of harmful magic, pressure to name accomplices and confessions extracted under torture. Both women and men were prosecuted. Property confiscation may also have encouraged abuse, especially under the heavily indebted rule of Count Ferdinand Karl of Hohenems, although financial gain does not by itself explain the scale of the panic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historisches-lexikon.li/Hexenverfolgung" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historisches-lexikon.li">[Liechtenstein Lexicon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historisches-lexikon.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein LexiconHexenverfolgungDie ersten datierbaren Gerichtsverfahren gegen vermeintliche Hexen in Vaduz fanden 1597, 1598 und 160...</span></span></span>

One surviving story exposes the system with unusual clarity. Maria Eberle of Planken was arrested in November 1680, imprisoned at Vaduz Castle, tortured and sentenced to death. She then escaped through the castle interior, reached the roof and reportedly descended using sheets tied together. Once free, she formally challenged the legality of the proceedings. Her escape helped opponents of the trials bring the abuses to outside attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frauenarchiv.li/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2024_04_13_Lie-Zeit_Maria_Eberle_124_April_2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frauenarchiv.li">[Frauenarchiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frauenarchiv.li</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maria Eberle und das Ende der Hexen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 1680 festgenom- men und auf Schloss Vaduz einge- sperrt. Unter Folter...Read more...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1680</span></span></span>

Other condemned prisoners withdrew their confessions and declared their innocence before execution. The Triesen priest Valentin von Kriss intervened, and the case eventually reached higher imperial authorities. An investigating commission concluded that unlawful procedures had been used, bringing the prosecutions to an end.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_witch_trials" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Liechtenstein witch trials</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein witch trials</span></span></span>

The central uncertainty is therefore not whether persecution occurred. That is firmly documented. The unresolved questions are how many people were killed, how extensively officials manipulated the process, why particular records vanished and whether confiscation, local hostility or political misrule was the main force behind the final wave.

Later folklore blurred these issues by turning victims, officials and opponents into supernatural figures. The responsible historical reading is more disturbing than the legends: no evidence is required for actual witchcraft, while abundant evidence survives of torture, coerced testimony, judicial misconduct and institutional efforts to conceal or forget what happened.

## What were the Gutenberg bronze figures used for?

In 1932, excavators working on the south-eastern slope below Gutenberg Castle at Balzers found an extraordinary group of small, solid-cast bronze figures. The ensemble dates from the later Iron Age, broadly between the fifth and first centuries BC, and includes human and animal forms. The Historical Dictionary describes the group’s composition as unique.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historisches-lexikon.li/Gutenberger_Votivstatuetten" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historisches-lexikon.li">[Liechtenstein Lexicon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historisches-lexikon.li</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gutenberger Votivstatuetten</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein LexiconGutenberger VotivstatuettenAm südöstlichen Hang des Burghügels Gutenberg (Balzers) wurde 1932 in umgelagerten Schich...</span></span></span>

The figures were not discovered in an undisturbed shrine. They came from displaced layers on a hill repeatedly occupied, rebuilt and disturbed over thousands of years. Medieval castle construction removed or shifted much earlier material, leaving prehistoric remains largely in redeposited soil down the slope. This loss of original context is the main reason the objects cannot be interpreted with certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hls-dhs-dss.ch/de/articles/019977/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hls-dhs-dss.ch">[HLS-DHS-DSS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hls-dhs-dss.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hls-dhs-dss.ch.</span></span></span>

Archaeologists nevertheless have a strong working explanation. Concentrations of ash and animal bones found with or near the bronzes suggest a place where animals or other offerings were burned. The figurines are therefore commonly interpreted as votive gifts: objects deposited in fulfilment of a vow, as an appeal to a deity or as thanks for divine assistance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historisches-lexikon.li/Gutenberger_Votivstatuetten" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historisches-lexikon.li">[Liechtenstein Lexicon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historisches-lexikon.li</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gutenberger Votivstatuetten</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liechtenstein LexiconGutenberger VotivstatuettenAm südöstlichen Hang des Burghügels Gutenberg (Balzers) wurde 1932 in umgelagerten Schich...</span></span></span>

The figures also show influences associated with both Celtic and Raetian cultural worlds. That mixture fits Balzers’ location at an Alpine crossroads where traditions from the northern foreland and inner Alpine regions met. The hill’s archaeological sequence similarly records several cultural influences rather than an isolated or culturally uniform settlement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historisches-lexikon.li/Gutenberg_%28Burg%2C_Burgh%C3%BCgel%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historisches-lexikon.li">[Liechtenstein Lexicon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historisches-lexikon.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historisches-lexikon.li.</span></span></span>

What cannot be recovered is the ritual’s precise meaning. The larger male figure is often interpreted as a god or warrior deity, while animals such as the stag and boar may have held religious significance. Yet there is no inscription naming a god, no intact sanctuary plan and no written account from the worshippers. Claims that the site belonged to a specific named Celtic cult go beyond the evidence.

This is a genuine archaeological mystery rather than a supernatural one. The broad conclusion — that the bronzes were probably offerings connected with ritual burning — rests on material evidence. The identity of the worshippers, the names of their gods and the reasons each figure was deposited remain unknown because later construction destroyed the arrangement that might have answered those questions.

## What happened to the Russians who returned east in 1945?

During the night of 2–3 May 1945, as Nazi Germany collapsed, nearly 500 members and dependants of the First Russian National Army crossed from Austria into neutral Liechtenstein near Schellenberg. The force, commanded by Boris Smyslovsky, had fought under German authority against the Soviet Union. Its members feared capture and forced repatriation. Contemporary and archival accounts give a total of about 494 people, including women and children.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://crossing-the-border.info/indexprev3_en.php?id=38" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crossing-the-border.info">[crossing-the-border.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crossing-the-border.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">End of the war</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">End of the war</span></span></span>

Liechtenstein interned and supported the group rather than immediately surrendering it. In August 1945, Soviet representatives arrived to press for repatriation. Liechtenstein refused to use force, distinguishing its conduct from the compulsory transfers carried out elsewhere in Europe. Those who chose not to return were eventually allowed to remain temporarily or emigrate, with many later travelling to Argentina.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.e-periodica.ch/cntmng?pid=hnz-001%3A2012%3A78%3A%3A560" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: e-periodica.ch">[e-periodica.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">e-periodica.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on e-periodica.ch.</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery concerns those who agreed to go back. Counts differ between accounts, partly because members departed at different times and because categories such as soldiers, relatives and civilians were not always recorded consistently. One archival study states that 290 eventually returned voluntarily, while widely repeated summaries give a figure of roughly 200 for the principal repatriation movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Russian_National_Army" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">First Russian National Army</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">First Russian National Army</span></span></span>

Once they entered the Soviet-controlled system, their individual fates became difficult to trace. Some popular accounts say that nothing was ever heard from them again. That wording is too absolute: scattered information may exist in Soviet security, military or family records. Yet the larger point is sound. There is no complete, publicly established account of what happened to every returnee, and people suspected of serving with German forces faced a serious risk of interrogation, imprisonment, labour camps or execution.

This case is sometimes simplified into a heroic fable in which tiny Liechtenstein alone defied Stalin and saved an innocent “Russian army”. The reality is morally more complicated. Smyslovsky’s formation had collaborated militarily with Nazi Germany, although individual motives ranged from anti-communism and Russian nationalism to survival and coercion. Liechtenstein’s refusal to compel repatriation did not erase that history; it asserted that people should not be delivered into Soviet hands without their consent.

The unresolved element lies east of the border, not in Liechtenstein’s decision-making. Names, departure lists and diplomatic exchanges can document who left. Only fuller access to Soviet-era records, combined with individual family research, could establish how many reached home, how they were treated and where they died.

## Where did Hedwig Rosa Beck go?

Hedwig Rosa Beck’s disappearance is Liechtenstein’s clearest modern unresolved missing-person case. On Monday, 21 July 2008, the 51-year-old Liechtenstein citizen left her home on Landstrasse in Triesen at about 7 pm to go Nordic walking. She was last seen in the area of Sandhüslerweg. The Liechtenstein National Police continue to publish her case among their missing-person notices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landespolizei.li/en/latest-news/wanted-missing-persons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landespolizei.li">[Liechtenstein Police]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landespolizei.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landespolizei.li.</span></span></span>

The official appeal describes her as about 172 centimetres tall, slim, blonde and wearing glasses. She left home in dark sports clothing, possibly with pink shoes. Beyond those basic facts, the police have released little publicly verified information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landespolizei.li/aktuelles/fahndungen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landespolizei.li">[Liechtenstein Police]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landespolizei.li</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landespolizei.li.</span></span></span>

That scarcity has encouraged online speculation, including unsupported claims about recovered walking equipment, accidents, voluntary disappearance and criminal involvement. Such details should not be treated as established unless confirmed by police or another reliable source. The official record supports only a narrow conclusion: Beck set out for exercise, was seen locally and did not return.

Liechtenstein’s scale makes the case especially unsettling. Triesen lies in a confined Rhine Valley landscape bordered by steep mountain terrain, yet proximity does not guarantee visibility. A fall, concealed natural feature or route into neighbouring territory could defeat a search. Conversely, the absence of a confirmed accident leaves open the possibility of human involvement. There is insufficient public evidence to rank those explanations confidently.

Unlike the 81st-soldier story, this is not folklore polished into a mystery. A woman remains missing, her family has no publicly known resolution, and the police request for information remains active. The humane approach is to preserve the distinction between possible scenarios and evidence.

## What Liechtenstein’s mysteries reveal

Liechtenstein’s most durable enigmas are largely mysteries of incomplete records. The 81st soldier survives because an ordinary liaison officer became detached from his documentation and transformed into a charming legend. The [witch trials]({{ 'witch-trials/' | relative_url }}) remain partly obscure because evidence was physically removed. The Gutenberg figures lost much of their meaning when their archaeological setting was disturbed. The returning Russians entered an opaque Soviet penal and security system. Hedwig Beck vanished without leaving a publicly established trail.

The cases therefore require different verdicts:

* **Largely explained:** the extra man returning from the 1866 deployment was probably an Austrian liaison officer, although the popular Italian-companion version persists.
* **Documented crime with archival gaps:** the witch persecutions and judicial abuses are established, but destroyed papers prevent a complete accounting.
* **Plausible interpretation, uncertain detail:** the [Gutenberg bronzes]({{ 'gutenberg-bronzes/' | relative_url }}) were probably ritual offerings, though their gods, ceremonies and precise sequence are unknown.
* **Historically traceable disappearance into a closed system:** the Russians’ departure is documented, but the individual fate of many returnees remains obscure.
* **Still unresolved:** no publicly confirmed explanation accounts for Hedwig Rosa Beck’s disappearance.

Together, these mysteries show why national memory needs both imagination and restraint. Liechtenstein has no shortage of evocative stories, but their value depends on preserving the boundary between what the records prove, what historians reasonably infer and what later storytellers added.
