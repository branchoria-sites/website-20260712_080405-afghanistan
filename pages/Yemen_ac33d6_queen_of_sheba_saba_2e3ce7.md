## Introduction

The short answer is that **the Queen of Sheba may have been imagined as a ruler of the ancient kingdom of Saba in present-day [Yemen]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-shape-yemens-past/' | relative_url }}), but there is no surviving historical evidence that identifies a real Sabaean queen behind the famous story**. This is one of Yemen's best-known historical mysteries because archaeology strongly confirms that Saba was a wealthy and powerful kingdom centred on Marib, yet the woman who made it famous in the Bible, the Qur'an and later traditions has never been found in its own inscriptions.

The puzzle lies in the contrast between abundant evidence for the kingdom itself and the complete absence of a named queen in contemporary records. Scholars therefore distinguish between two separate questions: whether Saba existed—which is firmly established—and whether the biblical Queen of Sheba can be linked to a documented Sabaean ruler, which remains unproven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLandmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 25, 2023</span></span></span>

## What archaeology confirms about Saba

Modern archaeology leaves little doubt that the kingdom of Saba was one of the major civilisations of ancient Arabia. From roughly the early first millennium BC, its rulers controlled an extensive agricultural system centred on Marib and participated in the lucrative incense trade that connected southern Arabia with the Mediterranean, East Africa and neighbouring kingdoms.

The archaeological remains include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the ancient walled city of Marib;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/2517" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[whc.unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* the monumental Great Dam of Marib, which irrigated one of Arabia's largest artificial oases;
* the Awam Temple and Baran Temple, major religious sanctuaries;
* extensive inscriptions recording rulers, construction projects, warfare and religious dedications.

</div>

UNESCO's inscription of the Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba recognises these sites as evidence of a highly organised state with sophisticated engineering, monumental architecture and a centralised administration. Importantly, the inscriptions demonstrate that Saba was not legendary—it was an historical kingdom whose political institutions can be studied directly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLandmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 25, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why the queen is missing from the inscriptions

This is where the mystery begins.

Thousands of inscriptions have survived from ancient South Arabia. They mention kings, officials, priests, military campaigns, irrigation works, temple donations and alliances. Yet none securely names the Queen of Sheba described in biblical tradition.

That absence does not necessarily prove she never existed. Ancient inscriptions survive unevenly, and many monuments have been destroyed, reused or remain unexcavated. Yemen's long-running conflict has also limited archaeological work and endangered important sites around Marib. Nevertheless, if such a famous monarch ruled Saba, historians would normally expect at least some contemporary reference to her.

Instead, the available evidence points in another direction:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Sabaean inscriptions document numerous male rulers.
* No inscription can confidently be connected to the queen who visited Solomon.
* None records the famous diplomatic journey described in the Hebrew Bible.

</div>

This silence is especially striking because Sabaean rulers regularly celebrated important political achievements in monumental inscriptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLandmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 25, 2023</span></span></span>

## Could the biblical story still preserve history?

Many historians think the story may preserve a historical memory without preserving an identifiable individual.

Several elements are historically plausible.

First, Saba really was wealthy enough to impress distant observers. Control of the incense trade generated exceptional prosperity, making gifts of spices, gold and luxury goods entirely believable in a diplomatic setting.

Second, South Arabia maintained commercial connections across the Red Sea and into the wider Near East. Long-distance diplomacy between kingdoms was not unusual during the first millennium BC.

Third, women could exercise political authority elsewhere in ancient Arabia. Classical writers and inscriptions from other Arabian societies refer to influential queens or female rulers, showing that female leadership was not impossible in the region.

However, none of these observations identifies a specific queen. They show only that the cultural setting makes the story conceivable rather than impossible.

An additional complication is chronology. The biblical account places the visit during the reign of Solomon, yet both the dating of Solomon's kingdom and the development of Saba remain subjects of continuing archaeological debate. This makes it difficult to match the literary narrative to securely dated historical events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLandmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 25, 2023</span></span></span>

## How Yemeni and Ethiopian traditions developed

The Queen of Sheba became increasingly important long after the period in which she supposedly lived.

In Yemeni tradition, she is often identified as a queen associated with Marib. Local names became attached to ancient monuments, with the Baran Temple frequently called the "Throne of Bilqis" and the Awam Temple known as her sanctuary. Archaeology confirms these were genuine Sabaean religious buildings, but not that they belonged to the legendary queen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLandmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 25, 2023</span></span></span>

Ethiopian tradition developed the story in a different direction. The medieval *Kebra Nagast* transformed the Queen of Sheba into the founder of Ethiopia's Solomonic dynasty through her son Menelik I. This work became central to Ethiopian royal identity, but it was written many centuries after the events it describes and cannot be treated as contemporary historical evidence. Instead, it illustrates how the same ancient story acquired new political and religious meanings on the opposite side of the Red Sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLandmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJanuary 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 25, 2023</span></span></span>

Rather than choosing between Yemen and Ethiopia, many historians emphasise that the Red Sea linked these regions through trade, migration and cultural exchange. Stories could travel and evolve just as goods and people did.

## Is there a historical candidate?

Over the years, researchers and popular writers have proposed various historical women as the "real" Queen of Sheba.

None has gained scholarly acceptance because every proposal encounters the same obstacle: there is no contemporary inscription linking any known woman to the biblical account.

Suggestions usually depend on one of three approaches:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* identifying an otherwise known Sabaean royal woman with the biblical queen;
* treating the queen as a symbolic representation of Saba itself;
* arguing that the story combines memories of several rulers and diplomatic contacts into a single literary figure.

</div>

Because the surviving evidence is so limited, no hypothesis has displaced the conclusion that the queen's historical identity remains unknown.

## Why the mystery still matters

The Queen of Sheba remains important not because historians have identified her, but because she illustrates how archaeology can both illuminate and frustrate the past.

The kingdom of Saba is one of the best-documented civilisations of ancient Arabia. Its cities, temples, engineering works and inscriptions reveal a prosperous state that played a major role in regional trade. Yet the figure who made Saba famous around the world exists only in literary and religious tradition.

For Yemen, this creates an unusual historical puzzle. The landscape associated with the Queen of Sheba is real, the civilisation behind the story is real, but the queen herself remains historically elusive. Unless new inscriptions or archaeological discoveries emerge from Marib or elsewhere in ancient South Arabia, the question of whether the Queen of Sheba was an actual Sabaean ruler is likely to remain unanswered.
