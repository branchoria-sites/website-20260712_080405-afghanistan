## Who ordered the murder of President Remón?

Remón, a former police commander and political strongman who had become president in 1952, was hit by gunfire during the attack and died in hospital. Yet the case did not stay solved. Miró later withdrew his confession, Guizado was eventually cleared, and no account of the conspiracy gained lasting credibility. The judicial proceedings were unusually extensive for Panama at the time, but their political setting made them difficult to separate from the struggle over who would control the government after Remón’s death. Scholarly work on the trials describes years of prosecutions, appeals and political drama rather than a clean evidential resolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/10100892.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dialnet.unirioja.es">[Dialnet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dialnet.unirioja.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Political Trials Captured in Cuba and Panamá, 1955-1959January 15, 2016 — by E Vierba · 2015 — In Panamá, the killing of strongman...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 15, 2016</span></span></span>

The basic facts are secure: Remón was ambushed, several people were shot, and the killing altered Panama’s political direction. What remains uncertain is who organised the attack and why. Suggested motives have included personal revenge, business disputes, gambling debts, internal political rivalry and opposition to Remón’s policies. None has been demonstrated convincingly enough to close the case.

The murder matters because Remón was not an ordinary office-holder. Before becoming president, he had exercised enormous influence over governments through the national police. His assassination therefore created an immediate problem of succession, but also a deeper question: was the killing a private plot, or the violent result of a political system in which armed power and presidential authority had become dangerously intertwined?

## Was Omar Torrijos’s plane crash an accident?

General Omar Torrijos, Panama’s dominant political leader and the central Panamanian figure in the treaties that arranged the eventual transfer of the Panama Canal, died on 31 July 1981. His aircraft crashed into a mountainside while travelling towards Coclesito in poor weather. Everyone aboard was killed. Contemporary reporting identified the destination and adverse flying conditions, while later summaries have continued to treat weather and navigational failure as plausible causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.upi.com/Archives/1981/08/02/Gen-Omar-Torrijos-the-Panamanian-strongman-who-negotiated-the/6533365572800/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upi.com">[upi.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Omar Torrijos, the Panamanian strongman who... - UPI2 Aug 1981 — Sources at the Panamanian presidential palace said Torrijos and his par...</span></span></span>

The official explanation was accidental. Accounts of the investigation state that mechanical damage was found but no evidence of explosives was detected. That conclusion, however, never ended public suspicion. Torrijos’s relatives and political admirers later raised the possibility of sabotage, while writers hostile to American foreign policy alleged that the United States Central Intelligence Agency had arranged his death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/omar-torrijos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ebsco.com">[EBSCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ebsco.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Omar Torrijos &#124; History &#124; Research StartersOn July 31, 1981, Torrijos and several of his aides were killed in a plane crash on a mou...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 31, 1981</span></span></span>

Those allegations are not supported by publicly available physical evidence. Claims about a concealed bomb or intelligence operation rely largely on later testimony, political interpretation and disputed memoirs rather than a verified forensic finding or authenticated operational record. Even critical commentary on the assassination theory has noted that some of its most widely repeated versions depend on narrators whose broader claims are difficult to corroborate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.debatmagazine.nl/the-scars-of-panama/3108/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: debatmagazine.nl">[DEBAT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">debatmagazine.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DEBATThe scars of Panama</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DEBATThe scars of Panama</span></span></span>

Suspicion nevertheless found fertile ground. Torrijos had negotiated with the United States, cultivated relations across ideological divides and built a political system centred heavily on his own authority. His death removed him abruptly and was followed by competition within the military establishment, from which Manuel Noriega eventually emerged as the country’s dominant figure.

The most defensible assessment is therefore uneven. A crash in dangerous weather is supported by the circumstances and by the reported absence of explosive traces. Assassination is possible in the abstract, especially given Cold War politics, but no strong public evidence has established it. The mystery survives because an unexpected death of enormous political consequence rarely feels accidental to those who believe the victim had powerful enemies.

## What happened to Kris Kremers and Lisanne Froon?

On 1 April 2014, Dutch visitors Kris Kremers, aged 21, and Lisanne Froon, aged 22, set out on the El Pianista trail near Boquete in Chiriquí Province. They failed to return. Border police, local residents, search teams and relatives spent weeks looking for them in steep cloud-forest terrain. Reuters photographs from April and May document both the official search and the families’ public appeals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/news/picture/editors-choice-idINRTR3L76V/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on reuters.com.</span></span></span>

The later discovery of a backpack, telephones, a camera, clothing and partial human remains transformed a missing-person search into an enduring forensic puzzle. Telephone records showed attempts to contact emergency services after the women had crossed beyond the trail’s summit. The camera contained ordinary daytime photographs followed, several nights later, by a sequence of images taken in darkness. Remains identified through DNA were eventually recovered along the river system beyond the continental divide, but not enough material was found to establish a precise cause of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deaths_of_Kris_Kremers_and_Lisanne_Froon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Deaths of Kris Kremers and Lisanne Froon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deaths of Kris Kremers and Lisanne Froon</span></span></span>

A Dutch forensic and search team concluded in 2015 that a fatal accident in the rugged area beyond the main path was the most likely explanation. Its reconstruction favoured a fall or incapacitating incident, followed by the movement and dispersal of remains through water and the surrounding environment. The team considered criminal violence unlikely, although it could not produce a complete account of the women’s final days.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nos.nl/artikel/2022695-kris-en-lisanne-een-zoektocht-van-een-jaar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nos.nl">[NOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nos.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2022695 kris en lisanne een zoektocht van een jaar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2022695 kris en lisanne een zoektocht van een jaar</span></span></span>

Several questions remain genuinely unanswered:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://medium.com/%40downwego89/a-decade-of-unanswered-questions-surrounding-lisanne-froon-and-kris-kremers-5a0a3e8b3710" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medium.com">[medium.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medium.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

* The exact place where either woman became injured or trapped has not been identified.
* The purpose of the night photographs is unknown. They may have been attempts to signal, illuminate the surroundings or record a location, but intention cannot be recovered from the images alone.
* The precise sequence of deaths cannot be reconstructed from the small amount of recovered material.
* Some handling of evidence and early witness accounts has been criticised, making later reconstruction more difficult.

</div>

These gaps have encouraged theories involving abduction, organised crime, local attackers or official concealment. Yet many internet accounts treat disputed details as settled facts, repeat unsupported claims about the bodies or infer criminal intent from any unusual feature. Newer publications continue to revisit the investigation, but even authors claiming access to case files generally stop short of presenting a definitive solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://christian-hardinghaus.de/en/book/still-lost-in-panama-kris-kremers-and-lisanne-froon/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: christian-hardinghaus.de">[christian-hardinghaus.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">christian-hardinghaus.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kris Kremers and Lisanne Froon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kris Kremers and Lisanne Froon</span></span></span>

The strongest evidence points towards a wilderness accident followed by prolonged survival attempts, not a demonstrated murder. That is not the same as saying every detail has been explained. The case remains haunting because the telephones and photographs preserve fragments of purposeful human activity while revealing almost nothing about the emergency itself.

## Where is Sir Francis Drake’s coffin?

English privateer and naval commander Sir Francis Drake died from illness in January 1596 during a failed expedition against Spanish possessions in the Caribbean. His final campaign had included operations around Nombre de Dios and Portobelo on Panama’s Atlantic coast. According to the traditional account, his body was placed in a lead-lined coffin and buried at sea near Portobelo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.indrakeswake.co.uk/Society/Research/nombrededios.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indrakeswake.co.uk">[indrakeswake.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indrakeswake.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on indrakeswake.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The coffin has never been found. Its location is often linked to the wrecks of two English vessels, the *Elizabeth* and the *Delight*, which were reportedly scuttled during the expedition. This association has encouraged repeated underwater searches, partly because identifying the ships might narrow the probable burial area.

In 2011, a team announced that it had located wreckage near Portobelo displaying features consistent with vessels of Drake’s era, including lead sheathing and period ceramics. Marine archaeologist James Delgado Sinclair described the finds cautiously as possible remains of the two ships rather than confirmed identifications. No coffin was recovered, and the claim did not amount to proof that Drake’s grave had been located.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2011-11-08/francis-drakes-ships-found-off-panama/3651322" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News Drake&#x27;s ships believed found off Panama</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC News Drake&#x27;s ships believed found off Panama</span></span></span>

The difficulty is not mysterious in itself. Portobelo Bay has experienced more than four centuries of storms, sediment movement, ship traffic, corrosion and human disturbance. A lead-covered coffin could have sunk into mud, broken apart, moved downslope or become indistinguishable from other debris. Historical descriptions also provide no exact coordinates.

Drake’s resting place occupies an unusual position in Panamanian memory. To English tradition, he was an explorer and naval hero. To Spanish America, he was an enemy raider who attacked ports, shipping and the treasure routes crossing the isthmus. The search for his coffin is therefore not simply a quest for a famous body. It is a submerged reminder that Panama was one of the strategic centres of the early modern world, where imperial wealth, forced labour, African resistance and European warfare intersected.

## What do the El Caño tombs reveal—and conceal?

The archaeological complex at El Caño in Coclé Province contains one of Panama’s richest records of pre-Columbian elite burial. Excavations have uncovered graves dating broadly to between the eighth and eleventh centuries, containing gold ornaments, ceramics, animal-tooth objects, musical instruments and the remains of multiple individuals. Smithsonian records describe elite graves discovered there with exceptionally rich burial goods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://repository.si.edu/handle/10088/21873" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.si.edu">[Smithsonian Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

A major tomb publicised in 2024 contained an important individual accompanied by gold jewellery, bells, decorated objects, bone flutes and the remains of numerous other people. Archaeologists interpreted it as a multiple burial associated with a powerful leader, although the final number of individuals and the precise relationships among them required further laboratory work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/this-1200-year-old-tomb-in-panama-contains-a-treasure-trove-of-artifacts-and-up-to-32-bodies-180983977/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

The mystery is not whether these burials existed; their physical evidence is substantial. The uncertainty concerns the society that created them. No surviving written records identify the people by name or explain their political titles, religious ideas or funerary decisions. Researchers must reconstruct meaning from the placement of bodies, chemical analysis of materials, object design, skeletal evidence and comparison with other sites.

Several interpretations remain open. Additional bodies may have been sacrificed attendants, captives, relatives or people whose deaths were connected to a single ceremonial event. Face-down burial positions and the layering of remains appear deliberate, but their symbolic meaning cannot simply be read from the ground. Gold objects show status and sophisticated craftsmanship, yet they do not tell us whether authority rested mainly on warfare, ancestry, ritual leadership, control of trade or a combination of these forces.

Scientific analysis has already replaced older assumptions. Research into organic residues and manufacturing materials indicates that funerary objects were technically complex rather than merely decorative. Continuing excavation also demonstrates that central Panama supported politically organised societies with their own regional traditions, not just communities occupying a passage between the better-known civilisations of Mexico and the Andes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440319301323" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

El Caño is therefore an archaeological mystery in the proper sense: evidence is steadily increasing, but certainty about identity and belief remains limited. It should not be presented as a “lost civilisation” suddenly discovered from nothing. The people were never absent from Panama’s past; much of their history became difficult to recover because conquest, disease, looting and the lack of surviving indigenous texts broke the chain through which their own explanations might have reached the present.

## Did Henry Morgan hide treasure in Panama?

The sack of Panama City by Henry Morgan’s privateers in 1671 generated one of the country’s most persistent treasure traditions. Morgan’s force crossed the isthmus, defeated Spanish defenders and occupied the city, which was destroyed by fire amid fighting and looting. Contemporary and later accounts describe searches of buildings, wells, surrounding countryside and concealed spaces for valuables.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Morgan%27s_Panama_expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Henry Morgan&#x27;s Panama expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Henry Morgan&#x27;s Panama expedition</span></span></span>

Morgan’s men later complained that their individual shares were much smaller than expected. This helped create the accusation that Morgan had secretly retained the best treasure before returning to Jamaica. From that grievance grew stories of hidden caches buried somewhere along the Chagres River, near ruined settlements or in the forests between old Panama City and the Caribbean.

There is no reliable evidence for a great undiscovered Morgan hoard in Panama. The expedition certainly seized valuables, prisoners and trade goods, but much of Panama City’s portable wealth had already been removed or concealed before the privateers arrived. The total booty also had to be divided among a very large force after deductions and expenses. A disappointing payment could therefore arise without a buried treasure or betrayal.

Morgan’s supposed cache is best classified as folklore built around a real dispute. Unlike Drake’s coffin, whose burial is described in historical accounts even though its position is unknown, the lost hoard lacks a dependable starting document specifying that treasure was buried at all. Stories of maps, deathbed confessions and secret locations should be treated as later legend unless supported by verifiable archival evidence.

The tale remains popular because Panama’s geography makes it feel plausible. For centuries, enormous quantities of American silver and other valuables crossed the isthmus through ports, rivers and mule roads. Real treasure traffic created ideal conditions for imagined treasure to flourish.

## Why Panama produces enduring mysteries

Panama’s historical enigmas share several features. The country is a narrow crossroads where ships, armies, migrants, merchants and intelligence services have repeatedly passed through. Its tropical terrain damages evidence quickly, while mountains, rivers and dense forests make searches difficult. Political upheaval has sometimes weakened investigations or encouraged suspicions that official conclusions serve those in power.

The surviving record is also uneven. European empires documented fortifications, shipping and taxable wealth more consistently than indigenous lives. Modern investigators can recover telephone logs or aircraft wreckage, but those materials may establish only what happened around a death, not its decisive moment. In political cases, abundant paperwork can create a different problem: records preserve accusations and institutional manoeuvring without identifying which witnesses were truthful.

That is why Panama’s mysteries should not all be judged in the same way. Remón’s assassination remains an unresolved criminal and political case. Torrijos’s death has a credible accidental explanation but a powerful conspiracy tradition. The deaths of Kremers and Froon are most consistent with an accident, although their final movements remain unknown. Drake’s missing coffin is a problem of underwater location. El Caño represents the limits of archaeological interpretation, while Morgan’s treasure belongs mainly to folklore.

## What the evidence allows us to say

Panama’s strangest historical cases rarely offer the satisfying choice between complete solution and total mystery. Instead, they occupy a middle ground in which some conclusions are much better supported than others.

A responsible reading separates five levels of confidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Documented fact:** an assassination occurred, an aircraft crashed, emergency calls were attempted or a tomb contained particular objects.
2. **Strong inference:** terrain, weather, forensic findings or political circumstances make one explanation more probable.
3. **Open question:** the exact sequence, motive, location or identity cannot be recovered.
4. **Disputed claim:** testimony or interpretation exists but lacks decisive corroboration.
5. **Legend:** a story is repeatedly told without a secure evidential foundation.

</div>

Seen this way, Panama’s mysteries become more interesting rather than less. Their value lies not in forcing every gap into a conspiracy, but in showing how history is reconstructed from incomplete traces—and how politics, grief, national identity and popular storytelling determine which uncertainties refuse to disappear.
