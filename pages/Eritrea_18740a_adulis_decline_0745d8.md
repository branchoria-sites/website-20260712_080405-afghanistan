## Introduction

The disappearance of Adulis as a major Red Sea port was not the result of a single dramatic catastrophe. Instead, the best available evidence points to a gradual decline caused by several interacting forces. Adulis remained an active and prosperous trading centre into the sixth and probably the early seventh century, serving as the maritime gateway of the Aksumite kingdom. Yet within a relatively short period it vanished from the historical record as an international port. The enduring mystery lies not in whether Adulis declined, but in why no single explanation fully accounts for its disappearance. Archaeology, ancient texts and environmental studies each illuminate part of the story, while leaving important gaps.

## The port that connected Aksum to the world

For centuries, Adulis occupied a strategic position near the Gulf of Zula on the Eritrean coast. Goods from the African interior—including ivory, tortoiseshell, rhinoceros horn, gold and enslaved people—passed through the port, while imported ceramics, glass, metals, textiles and luxury goods arrived from the Roman world, Arabia, Persia and India. The first-century *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* already described Adulis as one of the Red Sea's principal commercial centres, and archaeological excavations have confirmed extensive overseas connections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.ur.edu.pl/anabasis/article/view/10428" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.ur.edu.pl">[journals.ur.edu.pl]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.ur.edu.pl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 22, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 22, 2022</span></span></span>

Recent excavations have also demonstrated that the settlement remained wealthy long after the Roman Empire reached its height. Large churches, imported marble and fine ceramics indicate that Adulis continued to flourish during the Christian Aksumite period. Radiocarbon dating and architectural evidence suggest significant activity into the sixth and perhaps the early seventh century, making any theory of an earlier collapse difficult to sustain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/abs/rise-and-fall-of-axum-ethiopia-a-geoarchaeological-interpretation/B5B3D127F93D1C975C3D3C0CCD3BF773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentRise and Fall of Axum, Ethiopia: A Geo-Archaeological Interpretation &#124; American Antiquity &#124; Cambri...</span></span></span>

This chronology is important because it rules out many simplistic explanations. The port did not suddenly disappear when Rome weakened, nor does the archaeological evidence support an immediate abandonment after Aksum's conversion to Christianity.

## War, trade shifts and environmental change

Most historians now favour a combination of political, economic and environmental pressures rather than a single decisive event.

### Changing Red Sea politics

The sixth and seventh centuries transformed the political landscape around the Red Sea. Byzantine and Sasanian rivalry disrupted long-established trading networks before the rapid expansion of the early Islamic Caliphate reshaped maritime commerce. Rather than "closing" the Red Sea, these changes altered who controlled ports, shipping routes and customs revenues.

Aksum increasingly lost its privileged position within these networks. As commercial traffic became concentrated in ports controlled by new regional powers, Adulis appears to have become less central to long-distance exchange. Recent scholarship therefore views the port's decline as part of a wider geopolitical reorganisation rather than the consequence of one military defeat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unifind.unior.it/resource/item/78914?language=en-US" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unifind.unior.it">[unior.it]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unifind.unior.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UNIFIN D</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIOR - The decline and collapse of the Kingdom of Aksum (6th-7th CE: an environmental disaster or the end of a political process?...</span></span></span>

### A shrinking Aksumite economy

Evidence from the Aksumite heartland suggests broader economic problems that would inevitably have affected its principal seaport.

Researchers have identified several indicators of weakening state capacity:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* declining quality and purity of coinage during the sixth century;
* reduced monumental construction;
* abandonment of major quarrying operations;
* decreasing population around the royal capital;
* contraction of long-distance commercial activity.

</div>

None of these developments proves that Adulis itself failed at a particular moment, but together they show that the kingdom supporting the port was under increasing strain. A major trading hub cannot prosper indefinitely if the inland state that depends upon it is experiencing economic contraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/foundations-of-an-african-civilisation/decline-and-transformation-of-the-aksumite-state/8C1A37C8398CBF51E84819918A6A5550" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentDecline and transformation of the Aksumite state (Chapter 16) - Foundations of an African Civilisa...</span></span></span>

### Environmental pressures

Ports can become obsolete without being destroyed.

Adulis depended upon navigable access between its settlement, anchorage and the Red Sea. Over centuries, sediment carried by rivers, changing coastlines and gradual silting can reduce harbour capacity. Archaeologists have suggested that environmental changes may have made shipping progressively more difficult, even if the town itself remained occupied.

Broader environmental change has also been proposed. Earlier scholarship often argued that deforestation, soil exhaustion and climatic deterioration caused Aksum's collapse. More recent research treats these ideas more cautiously. Environmental degradation probably contributed to economic stress, but few specialists now regard it as the sole or dominant explanation. Instead, ecological pressures are increasingly understood as one factor interacting with political and commercial change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/abs/rise-and-fall-of-axum-ethiopia-a-geoarchaeological-interpretation/B5B3D127F93D1C975C3D3C0CCD3BF773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentRise and Fall of Axum, Ethiopia: A Geo-Archaeological Interpretation &#124; American Antiquity &#124; Cambri...</span></span></span>

## Why there is no convincing "final blow"

Popular accounts sometimes attribute Adulis' disappearance to a single invasion or sack. Several historical episodes have been proposed, including Arab naval operations in the early eighth century and earlier regional conflicts.

The difficulty is archaeological. Excavations have not uncovered a clear destruction layer showing that one catastrophic assault ended the city. If a major military attack occurred, it has yet to leave an unmistakable archaeological signature comparable to the destruction horizons seen at some other ancient cities.

Equally, written sources from the period are sparse. After the early seventh century, references to Adulis become increasingly rare. This silence makes it difficult to distinguish between gradual decline, repeated episodes of disruption, temporary recovery or final abandonment.

As a result, historians generally avoid claiming certainty where the evidence does not support it. ## What archaeology still cannot explain

Despite decades of excavation, several important questions remain unresolved.

The most significant uncertainties include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **When was the port finally abandoned?** Archaeology establishes occupation into late antiquity, but the exact date of permanent abandonment remains uncertain.
* **Did maritime activity move elsewhere?** Trade may have shifted gradually to alternative Red Sea anchorages rather than ending altogether.
* **How important was harbour silting?** Geological studies suggest environmental change was possible, but its precise effect on navigation is still debated.
* **Was there an undocumented conflict?** Written evidence for warfare around the Red Sea survives only in fragments, leaving open the possibility that political violence accelerated an existing decline.
* **How representative are the excavated areas?** Only parts of ancient Adulis have been investigated, meaning later occupation levels may still lie undiscovered beneath unexcavated sections of the site.

</div>

These uncertainties explain why Adulis remains one of [Eritrea]({{ 'what-secrets-still-survive-in-eritrea/' | relative_url }})'s most intriguing archaeological puzzles. The mystery is sustained less by sensational theories than by the absence of decisive evidence for the port's final years.

## The most likely explanation

Current scholarship increasingly converges on a cumulative explanation. Adulis did not disappear because of one battle, one flood or one political revolution. Instead, its fortunes were tied to those of the Aksumite kingdom and the wider Red Sea economy.

As regional power shifted, overseas commerce was reorganised, state revenues declined and environmental pressures accumulated, the advantages that had made Adulis one of the ancient world's great ports gradually disappeared. Without those commercial networks, the city's role diminished until it ceased to function as the international gateway it had once been.

The enduring mystery is therefore not whether these forces existed—they are well documented—but exactly how they combined, in what sequence, and over what span of years to bring one of the Red Sea's greatest ports to an end.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/abs/rise-and-fall-of-axum-ethiopia-a-geoarchaeological-interpretation/B5B3D127F93D1C975C3D3C0CCD3BF773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentRise and Fall of Axum, Ethiopia: A Geo-Archaeological Interpretation &#124; American Antiquity &#124; Cambri...</span></span></span>
