## Introduction

The strongest cases are not simply strange stories. They reveal larger uncertainties in Japan’s history: where political unification began, how the Allied occupation reshaped public institutions, whether courts convicted the wrong person, and how a seemingly perfect crime can become national folklore. In several cases, popular retellings have also outrun the evidence. The so-called underwater ruins at [Yonaguni]({{ 'yonaguni/' | relative_url }}), for example, look extraordinarily architectural, but geological research provides a much less dramatic explanation. What follows separates established fact from plausible interpretation and modern myth.

## Where was Queen Himiko’s kingdom?

One of the oldest unresolved questions in Japanese history concerns Queen [Himiko]({{ 'himiko/' | relative_url }}) and the kingdom called Yamatai in third-century Chinese records. Those records describe a female ruler who governed a federation of communities, communicated with the Chinese state of Wei and received official recognition and gifts. They are among the most important written sources for Japan before the appearance of substantial surviving domestic chronicles.

The central problem is geographical. The Chinese account gives a sequence of journeys to Yamatai, but its directions and distances are difficult to reconcile with the Japanese landscape. Scholars have therefore argued for two principal locations: northern Kyushu, which fits the earlier stages of the recorded route, and the Kinai region around modern Nara, which later became the political heart of the Japanese state. Archaeological evidence indicates that both areas supported large and increasingly complex communities in the late Yayoi period, so neither candidate can be dismissed easily. One academic synthesis concludes that one of these regional confederacies was probably Yamatai, while acknowledging that the Chinese compiler may have combined or confused geographical information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp/journal/6/article/827/pdf/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp">[nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">both Japanese and English. for Yamatai may be most reasonable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rice, Bronze, and Chieftains —An Archaeology of Yayoi...December 31, 1600 — Japanese archaeologists now believe the Yayoi ended by about...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 1600</span></span></span>

The Kinai case has been strengthened by discoveries at the large settlement of Makimuku in Nara Prefecture and by the nearby Hashihaka burial mound, both dating broadly to the period associated with Himiko. Their scale suggests the presence of a powerful political centre. Yet identifying a major centre is not the same as proving that it was Yamatai, and the mound cannot simply be excavated at will because it is administered as an imperial burial site.

Mirrors found in ancient tombs have become another part of the argument. Chinese sources state that Himiko received bronze mirrors from Wei, and some scholars have connected these gifts with triangular-rimmed mirrors decorated with gods and animals. The Tokyo National Museum’s collection notes that this identification has been proposed, but the mirrors’ place of manufacture and their precise connection to Himiko remain disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://emuseum.nich.go.jp/detail?content_base_id=100030&amp;content_part_id=1&amp;content_pict_id=0&amp;langId=en&amp;webView=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: emuseum.nich.go.jp">[emuseum.nich.go.jp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">emuseum.nich.go.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">historical sources as having been presented to Queen Himiko of the Yamatai...Read more...</span></span></span>

The mystery therefore survives not because there is no evidence, but because different forms of evidence point in different directions. The written route appears to favour Kyushu in some readings; the concentration of political and monumental archaeology around Nara supports Kinai. A securely identified inscription naming Himiko or Yamatai would transform the debate. Until then, the kingdom remains a test of how cautiously historians should combine foreign chronicles, archaeology and later national traditions.

## Is the Yonaguni formation a lost monument?

Off Yonaguni, Japan’s westernmost inhabited island, divers can visit a vast submerged rock formation containing flat terraces, straight edges, steps and channels. Since it was brought to wider attention in the 1980s, some observers have argued that it is the remnant of a human-built ceremonial complex or even evidence of an unknown civilisation.

Its appearance explains the fascination. Underwater photographs can make the formation resemble a monumental staircase or a ruined platform. Geologist Masaaki Kimura has argued that various features were shaped or built by people, while alternative-history writers have linked the site to lost-continent traditions. These claims have given Yonaguni the durable but misleading nickname “Japan’s Atlantis”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.popularmechanics.com/science/environment/a71748136/japan-atlantis-yonaguni-monument/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: popularmechanics.com">[Popular Mechanics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">popularmechanics.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Popular Mechanics Scientists Found a Baffling Structure Deep Under the Ocean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">One Scientist Thinks It Marks Japan&#x27;s Atlantis.2 days ago — In 1986, diving tour guide Kihachiro Aratake discovered a mysterious underwat...</span></span></span>

The geological explanation is considerably stronger. The structure consists of bedrock rather than assembled masonry. Its sandstone contains horizontal bedding planes and vertical joints that encourage it to split into broad slabs and angular faces. Earthquakes, strong currents and long-term erosion can enhance these regular-looking fractures. Similar stepped formations are visible above water on Yonaguni itself, and geological fieldwork has concluded that the island’s characteristic forms can be produced by weathering and erosion acting along natural bedding and joints.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonaguni_Monument" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Yonaguni Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yonaguni Monument</span></span></span>

This does not rule out every trace of human activity. Ancient residents could have visited, modified or used natural coastal formations when sea levels permitted access. But that is very different from demonstrating that the entire structure was designed and constructed. No accepted assemblage of tools, pottery, burials, foundations or datable cultural material has established it as an archaeological site. Japanese cultural authorities do not recognise it as a protected ancient monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonaguni_Monument" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Yonaguni Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yonaguni Monument</span></span></span>

Yonaguni is best understood as an example of a mystery that is visually compelling but evidentially weak. The unresolved question is not whether the rock looks artificial—it plainly does from certain angles—but whether there is positive archaeological proof of construction. At present, the balance of evidence favours a striking natural formation, perhaps with limited later human alteration, rather than a drowned city.

## What happened to the Honjo Masamune?

The Honjo Masamune was one of Japan’s most celebrated swords. Traditionally attributed to the master swordsmith Masamune, it passed through elite warrior ownership and eventually became closely associated with the Tokugawa shogunal family. In 1939 it was designated a national treasure. Within a few years, however, it had disappeared.

Its last known chapter occurred after Japan’s surrender in 1945, when occupation authorities required weapons to be handed over. Tokugawa Iemasa surrendered family swords, including the Honjo Masamune, at a Tokyo police station in December. Records later indicated that the collection was handed to a supposed Allied sergeant whose recorded name does not correspond clearly to any known official. The sword has not been reliably identified since.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.popularmechanics.com/science/archaeology/a71773714/honjo-masamune/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: popularmechanics.com">[Popular Mechanics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">popularmechanics.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The sword’s mysterious disappearance began after Japan&#x27;s surrender in World War II. In December 1945, Tokugawa Iemasa surrendered it to A...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1945</span></span></span>

Several outcomes are possible. It may have been destroyed with weapons considered militarily dangerous, misclassified as an ordinary blade, taken as an unofficial souvenir or transported to the United States. It might also remain in a private collection whose owner does not understand its significance. Attribution makes the search harder: Masamune’s surviving blades are not usually identified by a simple engraved signature, so authentication depends on expert examination of their workmanship, condition and provenance.

Stories periodically circulate that the sword has been found, but none has produced a publicly verified chain of ownership and expert agreement. A different lost Masamune blade, associated with the Shimazu family, resurfaced in a private collection and was recognised in the 2010s, showing that forgotten masterpieces can reappear. That discovery offers hope, but it does not provide evidence about the Honjo Masamune itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.popularmechanics.com/science/archaeology/a71773714/honjo-masamune/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: popularmechanics.com">[Popular Mechanics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">popularmechanics.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The sword’s mysterious disappearance began after Japan&#x27;s surrender in World War II. In December 1945, Tokugawa Iemasa surrendered it to A...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1945</span></span></span>

The case matters beyond the value of a single sword. During the occupation, weapons control, disarmament and cultural preservation often collided. Large numbers of blades ranged from mass-produced military weapons to irreplaceable works of art, and inconsistent handling made losses possible. The Honjo Masamune’s disappearance is consequently both a treasure mystery and an archival problem created by the disorder of transition after war.

## Was Sadanori Shimoyama murdered?

On 5 July 1949, Sadanori Shimoyama, the recently appointed president of Japanese National Railways, disappeared while travelling to work in Tokyo. His remains were found on a railway line the following day. The basic facts were clear; the manner of his death was not.

Shimoyama’s position made the case politically explosive. Japan National Railways was preparing enormous staff reductions under occupation-era economic reforms, and labour conflict was intense. His death was followed that summer by two further railway disasters, the Mitaka and Matsukawa incidents, creating an atmosphere in which rumours of sabotage, political violence and covert intervention flourished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tokyoweekender.com/japan-life/news-and-opinion/the-three-big-rail-mysteries-that-defined-japans-summer-of-1949/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tokyoweekender.com">[Tokyo Weekender]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tokyoweekender.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tokyo Weekender The Three Big Rail Mysteries that Defined Japan&#x27;s Summer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tokyo Weekender The Three Big Rail Mysteries that Defined Japan&#x27;s Summer</span></span></span>

Forensic disagreement lay at the heart of the mystery. Some specialists interpreted the injuries and blood evidence as indicating that Shimoyama was alive when struck by the train, supporting suicide. Others argued that signs on the body suggested he had died before being placed on the track, implying murder. The disagreement became public and political, but the investigation never produced a conclusion accepted across the police, medical and legal communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimoyama_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shimoyama incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shimoyama incident</span></span></span>

Theories have implicated left-wing militants, right-wing groups, organised criminals, railway interests and foreign intelligence personnel. None has been established by decisive evidence. The discovery decades later of investigation memoranda revived interest in the case but did not reveal a conclusive solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2002/07/04/national/shimoyama-incident-memos-uncovered/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: japantimes.co.jp">[The Japan Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">japantimes.co.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">shimoyama incident memos uncovered</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">shimoyama incident memos uncovered</span></span></span>

The responsible verdict remains open. Suicide is possible, particularly given the pressure surrounding Shimoyama’s role, but the disputed forensic findings prevent it from becoming a settled explanation. Murder is also possible, yet claims about a specific organisation or intelligence conspiracy generally rely on inference, incomplete testimony or later reconstruction. The Shimoyama case endures because it sits at the point where personal tragedy, labour unrest and Cold War politics overlap—and because the original inquiry failed to close the evidential gap.

## Did the wrong man die in prison for the Teigin poisonings?

On 26 January 1948, a man entered a branch of the Teikoku Bank in Tokyo and presented himself as a health official responding to a dysentery outbreak. He persuaded employees and customers to drink what he described as preventive medicine. Twelve people died from poisoning, and the attacker stole money before leaving.

Police eventually arrested the painter Sadamichi Hirasawa. He confessed during interrogation, later withdrew his confession and was convicted. The courts sentenced him to death, but successive justice ministers declined to authorise his execution. He remained imprisoned until his death in 1987.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fccj.or.jp/number-1-shimbun-article/toxic-enigma" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fccj.or.jp">[Foreign Correspondents Club of Japan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fccj.or.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">toxic enigma</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">toxic enigma</span></span></span>

Doubts about the conviction have persisted for several reasons. The physical evidence did not securely connect Hirasawa to the bank. Witness identification was inconsistent, despite numerous survivors having seen the perpetrator. Questions were also raised about whether Hirasawa possessed the technical confidence required to administer poison in a controlled sequence while maintaining the appearance of a medical procedure.

The poison itself remains important. The trial treated it as a readily obtainable cyanide compound, while critics argued that the symptoms and delayed effects were more consistent with a specialised military poison. That possibility encouraged theories involving a person connected to Japan’s wartime biological or chemical programmes. However, the proposition that a former military specialist committed the crime has never been proved, and it should not be treated as a solved alternative merely because the case against Hirasawa appears weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadamichi_Hirasawa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sadamichi Hirasawa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sadamichi Hirasawa</span></span></span>

Hirasawa’s family and supporters repeatedly sought a retrial. Their efforts kept attention on the quality of the confession, witness evidence and toxicological interpretation, but they did not obtain a posthumous acquittal. The most defensible conclusion is therefore uncomfortable: Hirasawa remains legally convicted, yet substantial evidential doubts make the identity of the poisoner a genuine historical question.

## Who carried out the 300 million yen robbery?

On 10 December 1968, a man dressed as a motorcycle police officer stopped a car carrying company bonus money in western Tokyo. He warned the occupants that the vehicle might contain a bomb, produced smoke beneath it and persuaded them to move away. He then drove off with nearly 300 million yen without firing a weapon or physically attacking anyone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/300_million_yen_robbery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">300 million yen robbery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">300 million yen robbery</span></span></span>

The robbery became famous because it combined theatrical simplicity with extensive preparation. The offender exploited public trust in police uniform, knowledge of the cash transfer and fear created by earlier threats against the company. Investigators recovered a large quantity of material believed to be associated with the operation, including vehicles, clothing and props. Yet much of it consisted of common or second-hand items that generated leads rather than a unique identification.

The inquiry grew into one of the largest in Japanese policing history, but the sheer number of clues became a weakness. Thousands of people were questioned, numerous suspects were considered and public speculation attached guilt to individuals who were never charged. The criminal limitation period eventually expired, leaving no possibility of prosecution under the law then in force.

Popular accounts often claim that investigators knew who the robber was but lacked proof. That remains speculation. Various suspects may fit portions of the evidence, but no complete, independently verified case has shown who planned the robbery, whether the disguised officer acted alone or what ultimately happened to the money.

Its place in public memory is unusual because nobody was physically harmed. The affair became less a story of violence than of performance: a thief used costume, timing and authority to make four people surrender control of a fortune. That elegance has encouraged films, novels and romanticised depictions, sometimes obscuring the fact that the case also involved false accusations and an investigation whose failures could never be repaired.

## Why does the Setagaya case remain unsolved despite abundant evidence?

During the night of 30–31 December 2000, Mikio and Yasuko Miyazawa and their two children were killed in their home in the Setagaya district of Tokyo. The perpetrator remained inside for some time and left behind clothing, fingerprints, DNA and other physical traces. The Tokyo Metropolitan Police still publicly seeks information in the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.keishicho.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/multilingual/english/safe_society/wanted/seijo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: keishicho.metro.tokyo.lg.jp">[keishicho.metro.tokyo.lg.jp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">keishicho.metro.tokyo.lg.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lg.jp.</span></span></span>

The case appears, at first, to contradict a basic expectation of forensic investigation: more evidence should mean a greater chance of identification. In reality, forensic material is most powerful when it can be matched to a known person or a searchable database. A complete genetic profile does not automatically provide a name. Fingerprints are similarly limited when the person has no corresponding record available to investigators.

Some evidence has suggested that items worn or carried by the offender had unusual manufacturing or distribution histories. Such details can narrow possibilities, but consumer products travel through resale, personal exchange and international markets. Claims that they prove a particular nationality, military background or place of residence often go beyond what the evidence can sustain.

The killings are recent compared with the other mysteries on this page, but they have acquired historical significance through their duration, the scale of the investigation and the extraordinary contrast between the traces left behind and the absence of an arrest. The case may still be solvable: new database matches, family-based genetic investigation where legally permissible, or credible witness information could create a breakthrough. Until that happens, responsible discussion should keep the victims at the centre rather than treating the offender’s behaviour as entertainment.

## The historical picture around Japan's historical mysteries
Japan’s unresolved cases survive for different reasons. Yamatai is obscured by the distance between ancient texts and archaeological interpretation. The Honjo Masamune disappeared during an administrative upheaval in which accurate records were not always preserved. The Shimoyama and Teigin cases were shaped by the instability of occupied Japan and by investigations whose conclusions remain contested. The 300 million yen robbery produced too many weak clues rather than one decisive clue, while the Setagaya investigation demonstrates the limits of forensic evidence without a matching identity.

They also show why “mystery” should not be confused with “conspiracy”. Several cases invite dramatic explanations involving intelligence agencies, secret wartime units or suppressed discoveries. Such possibilities cannot always be ruled out, but possibility is not proof. The most reliable assessment begins by separating three categories:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Documented uncertainty:** the records or physical evidence genuinely support more than one conclusion, as in the location of Yamatai or the manner of Shimoyama’s death.
* **Unconfirmed but plausible explanation:** a lost sword entering a private collection, or a natural formation receiving limited human modification.
* **Modern embellishment:** claims of a technologically advanced civilisation beneath Yonaguni or confident identification of a criminal without testable evidence.

</div>

That distinction does not make the mysteries less interesting. It reveals what is most compelling about them: each marks a boundary between what Japan’s surviving evidence can establish and what history has withheld.
