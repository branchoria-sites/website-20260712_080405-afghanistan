## Introduction

Gonur Depe is one of the most important archaeological discoveries in Central Asia, yet it also poses one of the region's greatest historical puzzles. Buried for millennia in the Murghab River delta of modern [Turkmenistan]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-turkmenistans/' | relative_url }}), the site was the largest known urban centre of the Bronze Age society now called the Oxus Civilisation, or the Bactria–Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC). Excavations revealed monumental buildings, carefully planned fortifications, elite tombs, sophisticated irrigation and evidence of long-distance trade. What they did not reveal was the one thing historians normally rely on most: written records explaining who these people were, what language they spoke or how they understood their world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonur_Depe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gonur Depe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gonur Depe</span></span></span>

That absence of texts is what makes Gonur Depe such an enduring historical mystery. Archaeologists can reconstruct daily life from buildings, burials, tools and human remains, but the civilisation's identity, political organisation and religious beliefs remain matters of interpretation rather than certainty. The site therefore occupies an unusual place in the history of Turkmenistan: it is both one of the country's best-understood archaeological landscapes and one of its least understood ancient societies.

## Who Built the Desert City of Gonur Depe?

Gonur Depe lies about 60 kilometres north of modern Mary in what was once a fertile branch of the Murghab delta. Around 2400 BC, irrigation transformed this desert oasis into an agricultural landscape capable of supporting a substantial urban population. At its height the settlement covered roughly 55 hectares and contained a fortified central complex, residential districts, workshops and an extensive cemetery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonur_Depe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gonur Depe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gonur Depe</span></span></span>

The site came to international attention through the work of the Greek-Russian archaeologist Viktor Sarianidi, who began systematic excavations in the 1970s. His discoveries overturned older assumptions that Bronze Age Central Asia was merely a corridor between better-known civilisations in Mesopotamia, Iran and the Indus Valley. Instead, Gonur showed that southern Central Asia possessed its own large urban centres, skilled craftspeople and extensive exchange networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonur_Depe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gonur Depe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gonur Depe</span></span></span>

Despite these discoveries, the civilisation still lacks a historical name of its own. "Oxus Civilisation" refers to the nearby Oxus River (the Amu Darya), while "Bactria–Margiana Archaeological Complex" is simply an archaeological label describing the region where similar sites have been found. Neither tells us what the inhabitants called themselves.

### A crossroads rather than an isolated kingdom

Finds from Gonur demonstrate remarkable connections across Asia. Archaeologists have uncovered materials and artistic influences linking the settlement with:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* the Iranian Plateau;
* the Indus Valley civilisation;
* Mesopotamia;
* regions around the Persian Gulf;
* pastoral societies of the Eurasian steppe.

</div>

Objects such as seals, precious stones, metals, ivory and distinctive ceramics indicate sustained exchange rather than occasional contact. Elephant ivory, for example, almost certainly reached Gonur through long-distance trade extending into South Asia, illustrating that this was an active participant in Bronze Age commercial networks rather than a remote desert outpost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonur_Depe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gonur Depe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gonur Depe</span></span></span>

These connections complicate attempts to identify the civilisation with any single ethnic group. Modern scholarship increasingly sees the Oxus world as a meeting place where settled farmers, travelling pastoralists and merchants interacted over centuries instead of a society created by one migrating people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374387130_QUESTIONING_THE_OXUS_CIVILIZATION_OR_BACTRIA-_MARGIANA_ARCHAEOLOGICAL_CULTURE_BMAC_An_overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">QUESTIONING THE OXUS CIVILIZATION OR BACTRIAOctober 3, 2023 — 3 Oct 2023 — This chapter gives an overview of the Middle Bronz...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 3, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why the civilisation's identity remains uncertain

The central mystery is simple to describe but extraordinarily difficult to solve: nobody knows what language the inhabitants of Gonur spoke.

Unlike Egypt or Mesopotamia, the Oxus Civilisation has produced no archive of decipherable inscriptions that identifies rulers, cities or religious beliefs. Archaeologists therefore rely almost entirely on physical evidence:

* architecture;
* burial customs;
* pottery styles;
* metallurgy;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* food remains;
* irrigation systems;
* ancient DNA;
* imported goods.

</div>

Each category provides valuable clues, but none can answer the most basic historical questions directly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/91117038/The_World_of_the_Oxus_Civilization" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The World of the Oxus CivilizationThis collection of essays presents a synthesis of current research on the Oxus Civilizati...</span></span></span>

Ancient DNA has added another layer to the discussion. Genetic studies suggest that many people buried at Gonur were closely related to earlier farming populations of the Iranian Plateau while also showing evidence of interaction with neighbouring populations, including individuals connected to South Asia. These findings point towards a diverse population shaped by long-term contact rather than a single isolated ethnic community. Genetics, however, cannot identify language or cultural identity on its own, so the archaeological mystery remains unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonur_Depe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gonur Depe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gonur Depe</span></span></span>

## Competing ideas about identity and religion

Religion is perhaps the most debated aspect of Gonur Depe.(#endnote-1 "Endnote 1")<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonur_Depe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gonur Depe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gonur Depe</span></span></span>

Sarianidi argued that several monumental buildings functioned as temples containing sacred fire altars, ritual vessels and installations associated with ceremonial drinks. Because later Iranian religions, especially Zoroastrianism, also emphasised fire and ritual practices, he proposed that Gonur preserved an early stage of that religious tradition and even suggested Margiana might have been its birthplace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Sarianidi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Viktor Sarianidi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Viktor Sarianidi</span></span></span>

The idea attracted considerable public attention but has never achieved broad scholarly agreement.

Several reasons explain the caution:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the buildings predate the earliest secure evidence for Zoroastrianism by many centuries;
* fire altars are not unique to one religion;
* ritual architecture can serve many different ceremonial traditions;
* no inscriptions identify the deities worshipped there.

</div>

As a result, many specialists regard the temple interpretation as plausible in parts but reject firm identification with historical Zoroastrianism. Instead, they argue that Gonur represents a distinctive Bronze Age religious tradition whose beliefs cannot yet be reconstructed with confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/91117038/The_World_of_the_Oxus_Civilization" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[academia.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The World of the Oxus CivilizationThis collection of essays presents a synthesis of current research on the Oxus Civilizati...</span></span></span>

Burials reinforce this uncertainty. Thousands of graves have been excavated, ranging from modest interments to elaborate elite tombs containing wagons, jewellery, weapons and animal offerings. Some royal burials display remarkable wealth, suggesting pronounced social hierarchy, yet they reveal little about the names, myths or gods honoured by those communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonur_Depe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gonur Depe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gonur Depe</span></span></span>

## Why Gonur declined and was abandoned

Unlike many famous ancient cities, Gonur does not appear to have ended in a single dramatic catastrophe.

Archaeological evidence indicates several stages of occupation, rebuilding and gradual decline rather than sudden destruction. The palace suffered a major fire but was repaired and continued in use before eventually losing its elite function. Later occupation became less intensive before the settlement was finally abandoned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonur_Depe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gonur Depe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gonur Depe</span></span></span>

Most archaeologists believe environmental change played the leading role. The prosperity of the city depended entirely on irrigation supplied by branches of the Murghab River. As those channels shifted naturally across the delta, water became less reliable, agriculture became harder to sustain and populations gradually relocated to better-watered areas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonur_Depe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gonur Depe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gonur Depe</span></span></span>

Environmental pressures probably interacted with wider regional changes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* shifting trade routes;
* changing relationships with neighbouring populations;
* movements of steppe pastoralists;
* political reorganisation across Bronze Age Central Asia.

</div>

## What the mystery tells us about Bronze Age Central Asia

The greatest significance of Gonur Depe lies not in proving a particular historical theory but in demonstrating how much of ancient Central Asia remains undocumented.

The site shows that a sophisticated urban civilisation flourished in the deserts of present-day Turkmenistan at the same time as better-known Bronze Age cultures elsewhere. It possessed monumental architecture, organised agriculture, specialised craft production and international trade, yet left no written account explaining its society. That unusual combination means every new excavation has the potential to reshape current understanding.

Research over the past two decades has increasingly moved away from viewing the Oxus Civilisation as an isolated or mysterious "lost kingdom". Instead, it is seen as an important participant in a network linking Iran, Afghanistan, the Indus region and the Eurasian steppes. Even so, fundamental questions remain unanswered: what language its inhabitants spoke, how they organised political authority, what names they gave their gods and how they understood their own place in the Bronze Age world. Those unresolved questions ensure that Gonur Depe remains one of Turkmenistan's most compelling archaeological mysteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/91117038/The_World_of_the_Oxus_Civilization" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[academia.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The World of the Oxus CivilizationThis collection of essays presents a synthesis of current research on the Oxus Civilizati...</span></span></span>
