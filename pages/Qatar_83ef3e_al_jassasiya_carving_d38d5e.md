## Introduction

Al Jassasiya is [Qatar]({{ 'what-does-qatars-past-still-refuse-to/' | relative_url }})'s largest and best-known concentration of rock carvings, yet it remains one of the country's greatest archaeological puzzles. Hundreds of petroglyphs have been carved into low limestone outcrops near the north-eastern coast, including rows of cup-shaped hollows, rosettes, boats, animals and abstract symbols. Although the site has been studied for decades, archaeologists still disagree about when many of the carvings were made, who created them and what several of the designs were intended to do. The mystery matters because Al Jassasiya preserves rare evidence of everyday life on the Qatari peninsula in periods that left very few written records. Instead of pointing to a lost civilisation or hidden code, the site presents a more challenging historical question: how should archaeologists interpret a landscape that may have been used and re-used over many centuries?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://qm.org.qa/en/visit/heritage-sites/al-jassasiya-rock-art-site/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qm.org.qa">[Qatar Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qm.org.qa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Qatar Museums Al Jassasiya Rock Art Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Qatar MuseumsAl Jassasiya Rock Art Site - Qatar Museums...</span></span></span>

## What Is Carved Into the Limestone?

Al Jassasiya lies roughly 60 kilometres north of Doha on a series of low limestone hills overlooking the coast. Since its discovery in the 1950s and systematic recording in the 1970s, archaeologists have catalogued about 874 carvings, making it by far the richest rock-art site known in Qatar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://qm.org.qa/en/visit/heritage-sites/al-jassasiya-rock-art-site/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qm.org.qa">[Qatar Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qm.org.qa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Qatar Museums Al Jassasiya Rock Art Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Qatar MuseumsAl Jassasiya Rock Art Site - Qatar Museums...</span></span></span>

The carvings are remarkably varied. The most common are cup-shaped depressions, known as cup marks or cupules, cut into the limestone either individually or arranged in rows, circles, stars and rosette-like patterns. Alongside them are carvings of:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Boats viewed from above, often with rows of oars.
* Fish, turtles, ostriches and other animals.
* Footprints and geometric designs.
* Abstract signs whose meaning has never been convincingly explained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://qm.org.qa/en/visit/heritage-sites/al-jassasiya-rock-art-site/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qm.org.qa">[Qatar Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qm.org.qa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Qatar Museums Al Jassasiya Rock Art Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Qatar MuseumsAl Jassasiya Rock Art Site - Qatar Museums...</span></span></span>

</div>

The boat carvings are especially distinctive because they are unlike most other Gulf rock art. Some even appear to have been altered after they were first carved, with features such as anchors or rigging added later. This suggests that at least some images were modified over time rather than remaining untouched after their creation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology_of_Qatar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archaeology of Qatar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archaeology of Qatar</span></span></span>

One of the site's important characteristics is that not every carving appears to belong to the same artistic tradition. Differences in style, weathering and subject matter raise the possibility that Al Jassasiya accumulated markings over generations rather than being produced during a single episode.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1281?articlesBySimilarityPage=6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>

## Why the Petroglyphs Are So Hard to Date

The greatest obstacle to understanding Al Jassasiya is chronology. Unlike a buried settlement containing layers of pottery or organic remains, the carvings sit exposed on bare rock. They cannot simply be assigned an age from the surrounding soil.

For many years guidebooks and popular accounts suggested that the carvings might date to the Neolithic or several thousand years before the present. However, those claims rested largely on comparison rather than direct dating. Later scientific work attempted to date mineral deposits associated with some carvings using radiocarbon techniques. Instead of producing ancient dates, the analysed samples yielded minimum ages of only a few hundred years, with the oldest around 235 years before present. The researchers concluded that the results did not support claims that the tested carvings were several millennia old, while also acknowledging that the method could not definitively establish the age of every carving on the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273991074_Determining_the_age_of_Qatari_Jabal_Jassasiyah_Petroglyphs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">273991074 Determining the age of Qatari Jabal Jassasiyah Petroglyphs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Determining the age of Qatari Jabal Jassasiyah Petroglyphs &#124; Request PDFJanuary 1, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2012</span></span></span>

Other evidence points in a similar direction. Some specialists argue that the forms of the boats resemble vessels used after roughly the tenth to thirteenth centuries rather than prehistoric craft. Pottery recovered nearby has also been interpreted as consistent with activity extending into the early modern period. Together, these observations have led many archaeologists to favour medieval or later dates for at least part of the site, although they stop short of claiming that every carving belongs to that era.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1281?articlesBySimilarityPage=6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>

The result is an unusual situation in which the site's importance is undisputed but its timeline remains uncertain. Different carvings may have been made centuries apart.

## Games, Boats and Other Competing Explanations

Because there are no surviving written descriptions of the carvings, archaeologists must infer their purpose from their form, location and comparisons with similar sites elsewhere.

### Were the cup marks game boards?

The most widely discussed interpretation is that many of the cup-mark arrangements functioned as boards for traditional counting games related to mancala. Similar layouts occur elsewhere across Africa and Asia, and traditional versions of these games survived in Qatar under names such as Al Haloosa or Al Huwaila. Regular rows of evenly spaced hollows fit this explanation reasonably well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://qm.org.qa/en/visit/heritage-sites/al-jassasiya-rock-art-site/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qm.org.qa">[Qatar Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qm.org.qa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Qatar Museums Al Jassasiya Rock Art Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Qatar MuseumsAl Jassasiya Rock Art Site - Qatar Museums...</span></span></span>

However, this theory cannot explain every carving. Some cup marks form unusual geometric arrangements that would not work as conventional game boards, suggesting multiple purposes or different traditions.

### Could they be linked to pearling?

Another suggestion connects the cup marks with Qatar's historic pearl-diving economy. According to this idea, the hollows may have been used to sort, count or temporarily hold pearls.

Although attractive because of Qatar's long maritime history, there is little direct archaeological evidence supporting this interpretation. No associated tools or written accounts confirm that pearl merchants used carved limestone in this way. As a result, the idea remains speculative rather than established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iwh.icesco.org/al-jassasiya-rock-carving-site/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iwh.icesco.org">[Heritage Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iwh.icesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heritage Portal Al Jassasiya Rock Carving Site – Heritage Portal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heritage Portal Al Jassasiya Rock Carving Site – Heritage Portal</span></span></span>

### Why carve so many boats?

The boat carvings almost certainly reflect the importance of seafaring to coastal communities. Fishing, pearling and maritime trade dominated life along Qatar's shores for centuries, making boats an obvious subject.

Yet even here questions remain. Some boats are carved with striking technical detail, including rows of oars, while others are far simpler. A few contain cup marks within the outline of the vessel itself, a combination whose meaning is unknown. Researchers have suggested they might commemorate particular voyages, represent ownership, express religious or symbolic ideas, or simply record familiar vessels, but no explanation commands broad agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ossl.alecso.org/WebMaster/Main_Module/img/resources/Jassassieh2012%20copie.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ossl.alecso.org">[ossl.alecso.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ossl.alecso.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jassassieh2012 copie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jassassieh2012 copie</span></span></span>

### Ritual, navigation or something else?

Less widely accepted theories propose that some carvings had ceremonial, territorial or navigational functions. The site's elevated position overlooking the coast has encouraged suggestions that it may have served as a lookout or landmark, although this does not explain why the carvings themselves were made. Likewise, proposals linking the patterns to astronomy or complex symbolic systems have attracted attention but lack convincing supporting evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitqatar.com/intl-en/things-to-do/art-culture/heritage-sites/al-jassasiya-rock-art-site" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitqatar.com">[Visit Qatar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitqatar.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Qatar Al Jassasiya Rock Art Site Carvings &#124; Visit Qatar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Qatar Al Jassasiya Rock Art Site Carvings &#124; Visit Qatar</span></span></span>

## What the Evidence Actually Supports

The strongest conclusion is also the most cautious. Archaeologists can confidently say that Al Jassasiya is a genuine archaeological landscape created by people living along Qatar's coast. They can also identify recurring themes—especially games, boats and maritime life—that fit the peninsula's historical economy.

What they cannot yet demonstrate is that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* all the carvings were made at the same time;
* they were created by a single community;
* every cup-mark pattern had the same function;
* or the site belonged to one coherent ritual or symbolic programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1281?articlesBySimilarityPage=6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>

</div>

Modern archaeological surveys increasingly treat Al Jassasiya as a palimpsest: a place where successive generations added new carvings to an already meaningful landscape. This approach explains why different styles, subjects and apparent dates coexist better than theories seeking one universal explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1281?articlesBySimilarityPage=6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>

## Why the Mystery Endures

Al Jassasiya remains compelling because it illustrates the limits of archaeological interpretation. The carvings themselves survive in remarkable numbers, but the people who made them left almost no written record explaining their intentions. Scientific dating has narrowed some possibilities without resolving the debate, while stylistic comparisons provide clues but not certainty.

Rather than pointing towards a forgotten civilisation or hidden message, the site's enduring mystery lies in the ordinary activities it may preserve: games played on coastal limestone, boats familiar to fishermen and pearl divers, and marks whose original meaning was obvious to their creators but has been lost through time. In Qatar's historical landscape, Al Jassasiya stands not as a solved puzzle but as an invitation to keep testing evidence against new discoveries, accepting that some of its carvings may never yield a single definitive explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1281?articlesBySimilarityPage=6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>
