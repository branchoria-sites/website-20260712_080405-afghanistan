## Is Shisr really Oman’s lost city of Ubar?

The best-known Omani mystery concerns a place said to have vanished beneath the sands. Modern books, documentaries and travel publicity have often called it Ubar or the “Atlantis of the Sands”, linking it with traditions about a wealthy Arabian settlement destroyed after its inhabitants became corrupt. The story acquired new force in the early 1990s, when satellite imagery helped researchers trace old desert routes converging near Shisr, an oasis on the edge of the Empty Quarter in southern Oman.

Excavation revealed a fortified settlement built around a water source. Its walls and towers had been reconstructed over time, while imported objects indicated connections beyond the immediate desert. Part of the structure had collapsed into a cavity created by the dissolution of limestone beneath the site. These findings made Shisr a convincing caravan station and helped establish its place within Oman’s UNESCO-listed Land of Frankincense, alongside ancient ports and frankincense-growing areas in Dhofar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1010/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLand of FrankincenseThe frankincense trees of Wadi Dawkah and the remains of the caravan oasis of Shisr/Wubar...</span></span></span>

What the excavations did not reveal was a vast, fabulously wealthy city. The archaeological remains are those of an oasis settlement and trading post, not a desert metropolis. Even members of the original investigation later treated “Ubar” less as the name of one city than as a possible region, people or trading territory. Older geographical traditions also placed the supposed land of Ubar in widely differing locations, making any exact match uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/502589555/34-09-Edgell-Ubar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[scribd.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">H. The dramatic claim that Shisr was destroyed suddenly by an earthquake is also too neat. A section of the fort did collapse into the ground, but the settlement’s history extended across many centuries. Its decline may have reflected changing trade routes, reduced demand for overland frankincense transport, pressure on the water supply and gradual abandonment rather than one catastrophic night.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore limited but still important: Shisr was a genuine desert oasis associated with long-distance trade, and its collapse or abandonment may have contributed to later stories of a settlement swallowed by the earth. It has not been proved to be the single legendary city described in modern retellings. UNESCO itself identifies it as the caravan oasis of Shisr or Wubar rather than presenting the ruins as the confirmed remains of a spectacular lost civilisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1010/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLand of FrankincenseThe frankincense trees of Wadi Dawkah and the remains of the caravan oasis of Shisr/Wubar...</span></span></span>

The mystery survives because three separate ideas are frequently merged: a real archaeological site, medieval geographical traditions about Ubar and moral tales about destroyed peoples. Shisr gives the legend a physical setting, but archaeology has not confirmed every part of the legend.

## Why did Bronze Age Oman build enormous towers?

At Bat, Al-Khutm and nearby sites in northern Oman, archaeologists have uncovered circular stone monuments dating mainly to the third millennium BC. Some were approximately 20 metres or more in diameter, with massive exterior walls and complex internal spaces. They stood among settlements, tombs, agricultural land and ancient water systems. UNESCO describes the wider Bat landscape as an exceptional group of Early Bronze Age villages, funerary buildings, monumental towers and dams.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/434/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreArchaeological Sites of Bat, Al-Khutm and Al-AynFor centuries, the tombs were used and reused, thus preservin...</span></span></span>

Calling these structures “towers” can be misleading. They were broad monumental buildings rather than narrow medieval watchtowers, and many survive only as low circular foundations. Their scale required organised labour, access to stone and an authority capable of coordinating construction. The central mystery is what that effort was intended to achieve.

Proposed functions have included fortification, water management, food storage, ceremonial activity, control of trade and the public display of community power. Evidence from wells, channels and surrounding agricultural landscapes supports a relationship between some towers and water. Their positions may also have allowed communities to oversee routes connecting mines, oases and settlements. Yet no single explanation accounts for every tower, and the buildings changed over their long histories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329425201_The_Bronze_Age_Towers_at_Bat_Sultanate_of_Oman_Research_by_the_Bat_Archaeological_Project" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) The Bronze Age Towers at Bat, Sultanate of Oman</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) The Bronze Age Towers at Bat, Sultanate of Oman</span></span></span>

Recent research has shifted attention away from the search for one universal function. Archaeologist Stephanie Döpper’s study of towers across the Oman Peninsula emphasises their long chronology and varied settings. The earliest examples may date to the end of the fourth millennium BC, while substantial building continued into the early second millennium BC. A monument used for gathering, storage or water control in one period might later have been rebuilt or given a different social meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.casemateacademic.com/author/dr-stephanie-dopper/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: casemateacademic.com">[casemateacademic.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">casemateacademic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dr stephanie dopper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dr stephanie dopper</span></span></span>

One increasingly plausible interpretation is that the towers were landmarks of identity: durable structures through which communities demonstrated attachment to a place, controlled important resources and negotiated relations with neighbours. That does not exclude practical uses. A tower could have guarded water, supported communal activity and proclaimed local authority at the same time.

The associated “beehive” and communal tombs are less mysterious than popular accounts suggest. They were funerary monuments, and UNESCO treats the Bat landscape as evidence for the development of burial practices in Bronze Age Oman. Human remains are not well preserved in every structure, partly because tombs were robbed, disturbed and repeatedly reused. Claims that the complete absence of bodies proves the monuments were temples or observatories exaggerate an archaeological preservation problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/434/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreArchaeological Sites of Bat, Al-Khutm and Al-AynFor centuries, the tombs were used and reused, thus preservin...</span></span></span>

The genuine puzzle lies with the monumental non-funerary towers. Excavation has narrowed the possibilities, but no surviving inscription explains who ordered them built or what activities took place inside them.

## Was ancient Oman the land called Magan?

Mesopotamian texts from the third millennium BC describe a distant land called Magan, known as a source of copper and other valued materials. Because Oman contains extensive copper deposits, ancient mines and evidence of large-scale smelting, scholars have long identified Magan with the Oman Peninsula, probably including parts of both present-day Oman and the United Arab Emirates.

Scientific analysis has strengthened the connection. Chemical and lead-isotope comparisons have linked some copper found in Mesopotamia with ore sources in Oman. Archaeological discoveries at settlements and production sites also demonstrate that communities in Oman mined, smelted and exported copper during the period when Mesopotamian records mention imports from Magan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229992337_Lead_isotope_and_chemical_signature_of_copper_from_Oman_and_its_occurrence_in_Mesopotamia_and_sites_on_the_Arabian_Gulf_coast" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate Lead isotope and chemical signature of copper from Oman</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate Lead isotope and chemical signature of copper from Oman</span></span></span>

The remaining uncertainty concerns boundaries and political organisation. “Magan” may not have referred to a nation in the modern sense. It could have described a broad production region, a network of coastal and inland communities, or a kingdom whose territory changed over time. Archaeologists cannot simply assign every third-millennium site in Oman to one centrally governed state.

Bat’s towers and elaborate tombs are often interpreted within this Magan world. They show social coordination and wealth, but they do not identify a capital or name a ruler. Mesopotamian documents provide names and trading relationships from an outside perspective; Omani archaeology provides settlements, graves, mines and tools without a large native written archive. The two bodies of evidence overlap without fitting together perfectly.

It is therefore reasonable to say that much of ancient Magan was located in the Oman Peninsula and that Omani copper played a significant part in Gulf trade. It is less certain whether Magan was a unified Omani kingdom, a shifting geographical label or a collection of connected communities. This is an unresolved historical geography problem, not evidence of a vanished empire deliberately removed from history.

## What did Dhofar’s unread inscriptions say?

Caves, rock shelters and dry riverbeds across Dhofar preserve inscriptions written in distinctive pre-Islamic alphabets. Many were painted rather than carved. Researchers documented the material systematically in the late twentieth century and separated it into several related script types, but the signs could not be read with confidence. Without bilingual inscriptions or known names, scholars lacked the equivalent of a Rosetta Stone.

A major breakthrough came in research published in 2025 by linguist Ahmad Al-Jallad. He recognised that three short inscriptions were alphabet lists: sequences of letters arranged in a traditional order rather than ordinary sentences. Once identified, these lists provided values for many of the signs in the main script variety. The method allowed previously mysterious sequences to be sounded out and showed that the writing represented a previously unattested local language related to the modern languages of southern Arabia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/129778573/Al_Jallad_2025_The_Decipherment_of_the_Dhofari_Script_Three_hal%E1%B8%A5am_abecedaries_and_the_first_glimpses_into_the_corpus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[academia.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

The decipherment also challenged the assumption that the script was simply a provincial form of the better-known writing systems used in ancient Yemen. Al-Jallad argued that its main variety developed from a northern Arabian alphabetic tradition that had been adapted locally. If correct, this suggests more complicated movements of writing and people across ancient Arabia than a simple north-south divide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/129778573/Al_Jallad_2025_The_Decipherment_of_the_Dhofari_Script_Three_hal%E1%B8%A5am_abecedaries_and_the_first_glimpses_into_the_corpus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

However, “deciphered” does not mean that every inscription is now fully understood. Knowing the likely sound of a sign is only the first step. Many texts are extremely short, spelling conventions are uncertain, and the language itself is poorly known. Some unusual signs are absent from the alphabet lists, while other varieties within the wider Dhofari group require separate analysis.

The surviving texts appear more personal and local than monumental. They may include names, brief statements, invocations or records left by people moving through the landscape. Further readings could reveal aspects of ordinary life that are almost invisible in royal inscriptions and foreign accounts.

This mystery has therefore changed form. The question is no longer simply, “What alphabet is this?” It is now, “Who wrote these messages, when did they write them, and what can their lost language tell us about pre-Islamic Dhofar?” The 2025 work supplied a key, but much of the archive remains to be interpreted.

## Where are the missing dead of the Dhofar War?

The most painful Omani mystery is not ancient. Between the 1960s and 1970s, southern Oman was the centre of an insurgency commonly known as the Dhofar War or Dhofar Revolution. A movement that began with regional grievances developed into a Marxist-led rebellion. The Omani government, supported most importantly by British forces and later by troops from Iran and Jordan, defeated it during the reign of Sultan Qaboos.

The broad military history is documented through British archives, memoirs and scholarship. Less clear is the fate of some captured or surrendered insurgents. Anthropologist Alice Wilson has recorded accounts concerning revolutionaries who were executed after the conflict and whose burial places were not disclosed to their families. She describes this as a “double disappearance”: the individuals were physically absent, while public discussion of their lives and deaths was also restricted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://allegralaboratory.net/double-disappearance-after-omans-dhufar-war-stateless-displaced-disappeared/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: allegralaboratory.net">[Allegra Lab]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">allegralaboratory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Allegra Lab Double disappearance after Oman&#x27;s Dhufar war</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Allegra Lab Double disappearance after Oman&#x27;s Dhufar war</span></span></span>

This is not a conventional missing-person case in which nobody knows whether the people survived. The central questions concern the number and identities of those killed, the circumstances of particular executions and the locations of their graves. Families’ knowledge, former combatants’ testimony and official narratives do not form a complete shared record.

The evidential difficulty is considerable. Oral testimony can preserve information excluded from official archives, but memories may differ after decades. British records provide extensive material about military operations while offering only a partial view of Omani detention and punishment. Restricted access to local state records makes independent reconstruction harder.

Former revolutionaries nevertheless preserved memories through family relationships, private storytelling and social networks. Later scholarship has shown how political ideas and personal loyalties survived even when the defeated movement disappeared from authorised public history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.openedition.org/arabianhumanities/11265" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.openedition.org">[openedition.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.openedition.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on openedition.org.</span></span></span>

The responsible position is neither to dismiss these accounts nor to present every allegation as fully verified. There is credible scholarly documentation that families sought information about missing graves and that the subject remained politically sensitive. Precise case-by-case conclusions require access to records, named testimony and physical locations that are not presently available in the public historical record.

Unlike the debate over Ubar, this uncertainty has direct human consequences. Locating graves would not reveal a fabulous treasure, but it could provide recognition and mourning for families while filling a major gap in Oman’s twentieth-century history.

## How legend, evidence and silence create mysteries

Oman’s historical puzzles fall into distinct categories, and treating them as though they are all the same produces confusion.

**Shisr is a case of identification.** The site is real and important, but its equation with a legendary city remains unproved. The mystery grew because archaeology, religious tradition, geographical names and modern publicity were blended into one story.

**The Bronze Age towers are a problem of function.** Their dates, materials and locations can be investigated, yet their builders left no readable explanation. Several interpretations may be partly correct because the monuments probably served more than one purpose.

**Magan is a problem of historical geography.** Texts and scientific evidence strongly connect it with the Oman Peninsula, while its borders and political form remain uncertain.

**The Dhofari inscriptions are a problem of partial decipherment.** Researchers can now read much of the alphabet, but understanding the language and social setting will require a larger body of securely interpreted texts.

**The missing graves of the Dhofar War are a problem of records and power.** The obstacle is not merely the erosion of ancient evidence. It is also the limited public documentation of a politically sensitive period.

These distinctions reveal why some mysteries are likely to shrink with excavation or linguistic analysis, while others require archival openness and acknowledgement. They also separate documented uncertainty from modern mythmaking. Oman does possess lost settlements, enigmatic monuments and unread voices from the past, but its history becomes more interesting—not less—when the evidence is allowed to remain complicated.

  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
