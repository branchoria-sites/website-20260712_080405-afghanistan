## Introduction

Ngundeng Bong’s sacred staff, usually called the **dang**, is one of [South Sudan]({{ 'what-still-haunts-south-sudans-history/' | relative_url }})’s most politically charged historical objects. More than a ceremonial stick, it has long been regarded by many Nuer people as a visible sign of prophetic authority, spiritual legitimacy and the right to lead. Its history combines well-documented events with deeply held religious traditions, making it an unusual case where an artefact itself has become part of an ongoing political debate rather than merely a museum object.

The central mystery is not whether the staff existed—it unquestionably did—but why its ownership has repeatedly been treated as a source of authority. Since its seizure by British colonial forces, rediscovery in Britain and return to South Sudan in 2009, arguments over who should possess it and what it symbolises have become entwined with questions of national identity, ethnic politics and the interpretation of Ngundeng's prophecies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sssuk.org/sites/default/files/sudanStudies/SS40.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sssuk.org">[sssuk.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sssuk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SUDAN STUDIESApril 19, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 19, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why Does Ngundeng's Staff Still Hold Power?

### Ngundeng, prophecy and the shrine at Waat

Ngundeng Bong (c.1830–1906) emerged during the nineteenth century as the most influential prophet among the eastern Nuer. According to historical research and Nuer oral tradition, he claimed inspiration from the divine being Deng and used songs, rituals and symbolic objects to call for peace among rival communities while also warning of future conflict. His followers gathered at his great ceremonial mound, or **Bieh**, at Weideang near Waat, which became an important religious centre attracting visitors from several neighbouring peoples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/47843/chapter-abstract/422538910" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicNgundeng: Prophetic Inspiration on the Eastern Frontier &#124; Nuer Prophets: A History Of Prophecy From The Upper Nile In The Nin...</span></span></span>

The staff formed part of a wider collection of sacred objects used during ceremonies. Unlike an ordinary walking stick, the dang represented Ngundeng's prophetic office. Douglas H. Johnson, whose research on Nuer prophecy remains the principal scholarly study of the subject, compares its symbolic role to a parliamentary mace: not valuable because of its material alone, but because it embodied recognised authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sssuk.org/sites/default/files/sudanStudies/SS40.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sssuk.org">[sssuk.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sssuk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SUDAN STUDIESApril 19, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 19, 2026</span></span></span>

For believers, the staff cannot easily be separated from Ngundeng's prophecies themselves. Many traditions hold that authority over the object carries spiritual responsibility rather than simple ownership. This distinction explains why debates surrounding the dang have continued long after Ngundeng's death.

## How did the staff travel from a shrine to a political symbol?

The physical history of the dang is unusually well documented compared with many sacred African objects.

The staff was fashioned from the root of a tamarind tree and wrapped with copper wire, making it distinctive in appearance. Historical accounts record that it broke during the Battle of Pading in the late nineteenth century, after which Ngundeng himself reportedly referred to it as his "broken dang". The break became part of its symbolic history rather than diminishing its importance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sssuk.org/sites/default/files/sudanStudies/SS40.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sssuk.org">[sssuk.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sssuk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SUDAN STUDIESApril 19, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 19, 2026</span></span></span>

After Ngundeng's death, the staff passed to his son Guek Ngundeng. During British military campaigns against Nuer resistance in 1929, Guek was killed and colonial forces removed several of Ngundeng's sacred objects as trophies. The dang subsequently disappeared from public knowledge for decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sssuk.org/sites/default/files/sudanStudies/SS40.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sssuk.org">[sssuk.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sssuk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SUDAN STUDIESApril 19, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 19, 2026</span></span></span>

Its rediscovery came unexpectedly. Historian Douglas H. Johnson located the staff at an auction in Britain, purchased it, researched its provenance and organised its return to southern Sudan in 2009. The journey itself became a major public event, attracting thousands of spectators in Juba and widespread media attention because many regarded its return as the fulfilment of one of Ngundeng's prophecies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sssuk.org/sites/default/files/sudanStudies/SS40.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sssuk.org">[sssuk.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sssuk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SUDAN STUDIESApril 19, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 19, 2026</span></span></span>

This sequence—from sacred relic to colonial trophy to repatriated cultural object—is well established. What remains disputed is what the staff's return should have meant politically.

## Why is ownership so controversial?

The greatest disagreement concerns who should possess the dang and whether it represents the Nuer community, Ngundeng's descendants or the South Sudanese nation as a whole.

When the staff returned in 2009 it was officially received by South Sudan's vice-president, Riek Machar, himself a prominent Nuer politician. Some viewed this as an appropriate cultural gesture because of Machar's ethnic background and political position. Others believed the staff should instead have gone to Ngundeng's descendants or into a future national museum where it would belong to all South Sudanese.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.uj.ac.za/index.php/ujsrad/article/download/3489/1958/11235" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.uj.ac.za">[UJ Press Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.uj.ac.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UJ Press JournalsJournal for the Study of the Religions of Africa and its Diaspora 3.1 (October 2017): 60-77...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2017</span></span></span>

Johnson has written that his original expectation was that the dang would ultimately become part of South Sudan's national heritage rather than the possession of any individual political figure. Instead, the object's association with Machar altered public perceptions of it, making it increasingly difficult to distinguish between religious symbolism and contemporary politics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.uj.ac.za/index.php/ujsrad/article/download/3489/1958/11235" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.uj.ac.za">[UJ Press Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.uj.ac.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UJ Press JournalsJournal for the Study of the Religions of Africa and its Diaspora 3.1 (October 2017): 60-77...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2017</span></span></span>

The staff therefore illustrates a broader historical question common in post-colonial societies: when sacred objects return after colonial removal, should they be restored to descendants, religious custodians or the state?

## Prophecy, politics and competing interpretations

Ngundeng's prophecies survive primarily through oral tradition and ritual songs rather than written manuscripts. Their poetic language allows differing interpretations, which has contributed to the staff's continuing political importance.

Many supporters of Riek Machar have interpreted certain prophecies as describing his future leadership. Particular attention has focused on descriptions of a future Nuer leader whose physical characteristics are said by believers to resemble Machar. Critics argue that these interpretations read present-day politics back into older prophetic traditions, while historians caution that prophetic language is inherently flexible and often acquires new meanings as circumstances change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e2300925846a694731e7627bb7a2f04e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The stick, believed by many to possess mystical power, is associated with a prophecy foretelling the rise of a messianic Nuer leader—trai...</span></span></span>

This uncertainty is central to the mystery. No historical document definitively links the prophecies to any modern politician. Instead, their influence depends on how communities understand them. As historian Christopher Tounsel has noted, the enduring power of prophecy lies less in proving literal predictions than in how people believe and act upon them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e2300925846a694731e7627bb7a2f04e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The stick, believed by many to possess mystical power, is associated with a prophecy foretelling the rise of a messianic Nuer leader—trai...</span></span></span>

Far less certain are questions that cannot be answered by historical documents alone:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Whether Ngundeng intended the staff to legitimise any future political leader.
* Whether the return of the dang fulfilled specific prophecies.
* Whether possession of the staff carries genuine spiritual authority or functions primarily as a powerful cultural symbol.
* Where the object should permanently reside as part of South Sudan's national heritage.

</div>

These are matters of religious belief, political interpretation and cultural identity rather than questions that archaeology or archival research can conclusively settle.

## Why the staff still matters

Unlike many historical artefacts that lose political significance once they enter museums, Ngundeng's staff remains active in public life. It continues to be invoked in discussions about leadership, legitimacy and the future of South Sudan. Recent reporting has shown that its symbolism still influences perceptions among some political supporters and armed groups, even though historians emphasise the need to distinguish religious tradition from historical evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e2300925846a694731e7627bb7a2f04e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The stick, believed by many to possess mystical power, is associated with a prophecy foretelling the rise of a messianic Nuer leader—trai...</span></span></span>

Its importance therefore lies not in any proven supernatural power but in the remarkable ability of a single nineteenth-century ceremonial object to bridge religion, colonial history, national memory and modern politics. Few artefacts in South Sudan demonstrate so clearly how historical objects can continue to shape political imagination long after the people who first used them have disappeared.
