## Introduction

Six cases stand out because they reveal different ways that the past becomes uncertain. The Nok terracottas and Igbo-Ukwu finds raise questions about societies known mainly through objects. The deaths of Mungo Park, journalist Dele Giwa and presidential-election winner Moshood Abiola show how disputed testimony and political mistrust can outlive official explanations. The unresolved fate of many [Chibok girls]({{ 'chibok-girls/' | relative_url }}) demonstrates that a national mystery can remain a continuing human tragedy rather than a safely distant historical puzzle.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Nigeria_9742d0-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Nigeria" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

In each case, the useful question is not simply, “What strange thing happened?” It is: which facts are secure, what evidence is missing, and which popular explanations go further than the record allows?

## The Nok figures whose stories were dug away

The terracotta heads and figures associated with the Nok culture are among the oldest known large-scale sculptures from sub-Saharan Africa. They were produced in what is now central Nigeria, beginning more than two thousand years ago. Many are hollow, built from coils of clay and distinguished by elaborate hairstyles, jewellery and triangular or pierced eyes. Yet the people who made them left no known written account explaining whom the figures represented or how they were used.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/restitution-cultural-property-nok-civilization" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">restitution cultural property nok civilization</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The statuette was stored at the National Museum of...Read more...</span></span></span>

That absence does not mean Nok society itself was inexplicable. Archaeological work has linked its settlements with farming, iron production and sophisticated ceramic traditions. Researchers have also revised the culture’s chronology as better excavations and dating methods have become available. The genuine mystery concerns the meaning of the sculptures and the relationship between the scattered communities grouped under the modern label “Nok”. Were the figures ancestors, rulers, spirits, mourners, ritual participants or several different kinds of subject? Their poses and attributes invite interpretation, but no single answer has been demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/nok-terracottas-500-b-c-200-a-d" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtNok Terracottas (500 B.C.–200 A.D.)1 Oct 2000 — Most Nok sculpture is hollow and coil-built like pottery. F...</span></span></span>

The greatest obstacle is looting. A sculpture found during a controlled excavation can be studied alongside soil layers, buildings, charcoal, pottery and other objects. A figure dug out for the art market arrives with little more than a claimed place of origin. Its surrounding evidence—the very material that might explain its date and purpose—has been permanently destroyed. Nok sites have suffered extensive illicit digging, and the ownership histories of some sculptures that reached European collections have been unclear or contested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://plone.unige.ch/art-adr/cases-affaires/three-nok-and-sokoto-sculptures-2013-nigeria-and-france" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: plone.unige.ch">[plone.unige.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">plone.unige.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Three Nok and Sokoto Sculptures – Nigeria and FranceHowever, the looting of archaeological sites in Africa has been a serious problem for...</span></span></span>

This has also encouraged forgery. Because complete Nok figures command far higher prices than fragments, restorers and dealers may combine ancient pieces, add modern sections or create convincing imitations. Scientific testing can help establish the age of fired clay, but even an authentically ancient fragment may have been misleadingly reconstructed. The result is a mystery partly manufactured by the market: scholars are asked to interpret objects after their archaeological identities have been erased.

The most responsible conclusion is therefore modest. Nok art proves the existence of highly skilled communities with complex symbolic lives. It does not prove the sweeping claims sometimes made about a single lost kingdom, an unknown empire or unexplained foreign visitors. The missing answer lies mainly in damaged archaeological context, not in evidence of a vanished super-civilisation.

## Igbo-Ukwu and the civilisation no one expected

In 1938, Isaiah Anozie discovered metal objects while digging a cistern in his compound at Igbo-Ukwu in present-day Anambra State. Later excavations led by archaeologist Thurstan Shaw uncovered an extraordinary collection: intricately cast copper-alloy vessels and ornaments, beads, textiles, pottery and a richly furnished burial. The workmanship was so accomplished that some early observers struggled to accept that it could have been produced in the region before European contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1956" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishmuseum.org.</span></span></span>

That disbelief was rooted less in the evidence than in colonial assumptions about African technological history. Subsequent research established that the assemblage was genuinely ancient and belonged within a West African setting. The bronzes were made using sophisticated lost-wax casting, while imported beads showed that the community participated in long-distance exchange networks reaching far beyond south-eastern Nigeria. The discoveries transformed scholarly understanding of early metalworking and social complexity in the region.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9640786/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Igbo-Ukwu at 50: A Symposium on Recent Archaeological...by SK McIntosh · 2022 · Cited by 12 — This article reviews the history of the...</span></span></span>

Yet important questions remain. The excavated areas—known as Igbo Isaiah, Igbo Richard and Igbo Jonah—appear to have served different purposes, including storage, ritual activity and burial. It is still uncertain whether they were all used at precisely the same time. Improved radiocarbon analysis has suggested that activity at Igbo-Ukwu may have extended over a longer period than Shaw’s original ninth-century dating implied. Direct dating of surviving textiles has also complicated the chronology rather than reducing it to one neat date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9640786/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Igbo-Ukwu at 50: A Symposium on Recent Archaeological...by SK McIntosh · 2022 · Cited by 12 — This article reviews the history of the...</span></span></span>

The identity of the person buried with lavish goods at Igbo Richard is likewise unresolved. The individual is often described as a priest-king or senior ritual figure, but the title is an interpretation rather than an inscription found at the site. Nor can researchers yet reconstruct exactly how political authority worked in the community or who controlled the acquisition of copper, glass beads and other valued materials.

The mystery, then, is not “Who secretly taught the Igbo to make bronze?” There is no need for an outside civilising force, and claims of lost Europeans or wandering Middle Eastern craftsmen arose largely from outdated expectations. The better question is how local specialists, religious institutions and trade networks combined to produce such concentrated wealth and technical brilliance. New excavation and dating may answer parts of that question, but the first accidental discovery disturbed the site before modern archaeological recording began, leaving some relationships permanently uncertain.

## What happened to Mungo Park at Bussa?

Scottish explorer Mungo Park disappeared during his second Niger expedition in 1806 near Bussa, an area now associated with Kainji Lake in north-western Nigeria. By then, most of his European companions had died from disease during the journey. Park continued down the Niger in a specially constructed boat with a small surviving party, hoping to establish the river’s course. None of the European expedition members returned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/mungo-park" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ebsco.com">[EBSCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ebsco.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mungo park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">mungo park</span></span></span>

The commonly accepted account came through Isaaco, a guide sent later to investigate, and Amadi Fatouma, who had travelled with Park for part of the expedition. According to the reported testimony, Park’s boat became trapped or endangered in rapids near Bussa. Local people attacked from the riverbanks, and Park and his remaining companions drowned after jumping or being thrown into the water. The account was transmitted indirectly, however, and Park’s body was not recovered and identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.africabib.org/rec.php?RID=185869440" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africabib.org">[AfricaBib]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africabib.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on africabib.org.</span></span></span>

Why an attack occurred is less clear. Some versions portray it simply as hostility towards strangers. Others suggest that Park’s conduct contributed to the disaster. His party was heavily armed and had reportedly fired upon people during its passage downriver, sometimes believing that approaching canoes were threatening. From the perspective of communities along the Niger, the expedition may have looked less like neutral geographical research and more like an armed intrusion.

Local political conditions also mattered. Park entered a landscape of sovereign communities, trade routes and authorities whose permission could not be assumed. Accounts that describe the expedition as if it travelled through empty, unknown territory reflect a European exploration tradition rather than the lived geography of the Niger.

The broad outline—violent confrontation followed by drowning—is plausible and widely accepted. The precise sequence, the motives of the attackers and the final actions of Park’s party remain uncertain. Later retellings have sometimes embroidered the story with recovered weapons, manuscripts or dramatic last stands. Such claims require caution unless they can be connected to early testimony or securely documented objects.

The case endures because it marks the collision between two histories. In British writing, Park became a heroic explorer lost while solving a geographical puzzle. In a Nigerian setting, his death can also be understood as the fate of an armed foreign expedition that failed to negotiate local power, communication and fear.

## Who sent the bomb that killed Dele Giwa?

On 19 October 1986, Newswatch editor and co-founder Dele Giwa received a parcel at his home in Ikeja, Lagos. It exploded when he opened it, killing him and injuring journalist Kayode Soyinka, who was nearby. Giwa became Nigeria’s most famous victim of a parcel-bomb assassination, and no one has been convicted of his murder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cpj.org/2001/08/former-dictator-refuses-to-testify-in-journalists/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cpj.org">[Committee to Protect Journalists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cpj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cpj.org.</span></span></span>

The timing immediately aroused suspicion. Giwa had recently been questioned by security officials during the military government of General Ibrahim Babangida. Newswatch had established a reputation for investigating political power, economic policy and government manoeuvring. The apparent sophistication of the device, combined with Giwa’s encounters with state agencies, led colleagues, campaigners and much of the public to suspect official involvement.

Suspicion, however, is not the same as a completed criminal case. Investigations became entangled in political accusations, disputes over evidence and attempts by lawyer Gani Fawehinmi to pursue a private prosecution. Years later, Babangida and former intelligence officials resisted appearing before Nigeria’s Human Rights Violations Investigation Commission when it examined the case. The Committee to Protect Journalists argued that their testimony was necessary to resolve allegations surrounding the killing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cpj.org/2001/08/former-dictator-refuses-to-testify-in-journalists/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cpj.org">[Committee to Protect Journalists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cpj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cpj.org.</span></span></span>

One persistent theory connected Giwa’s death with the earlier Gloria Okon affair. Okon had reportedly been arrested on suspicion of drug smuggling and was said to have died in custody, prompting rumours that she had survived and been secretly removed from Nigeria because of links to powerful figures. Later stories claimed that Giwa had discovered the truth. The difficulty is that the theory depends heavily on hearsay, contradictory accounts and repeated allegations rather than a publicly demonstrated chain of evidence.

Other uncertainties concern the parcel itself: who delivered it, how it passed through the household, what forensic findings were preserved and whether the original case file remains complete. Retired investigators have subsequently offered conflicting recollections, but recollections given decades later cannot replace contemporaneous forensic records and tested testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.premiumtimesng.com/news/top-news/192176-ex-dig-omeben-opens-up-on-dele-giwas-assassination.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: premiumtimesng.com">[Premium Times Nigeria]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">premiumtimesng.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">192176 ex dig omeben opens up on dele giwas assassination</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">192176 ex dig omeben opens up on dele giwas assassination</span></span></span>

The most defensible assessment is that Giwa was deliberately assassinated and that the political circumstances justified serious investigation of state actors. It has not been proved in court who ordered, assembled or delivered the bomb. The failure to establish those facts is itself historically important. The case became a symbol of the vulnerability of Nigerian journalists under military rule and of how an investigation can be rendered inconclusive when the institutions responsible for examining power are answerable to that power.

## Did Moshood Abiola die naturally?

Moshood Kashimawo Olawale Abiola was widely regarded as the winner of Nigeria’s presidential election of 12 June 1993. The military government annulled the result, creating a prolonged political crisis. After declaring himself president, Abiola was arrested in 1994 and remained in detention through the rule of General Sani Abacha.

Abacha died suddenly on 8 June 1998. One month later, on 7 July, Abiola became ill during a meeting with a United States delegation in Abuja. He collapsed and died shortly afterwards, just as negotiations over his release and Nigeria’s return to civilian rule were gathering momentum. The proximity of the two deaths, and the enormous political stakes attached to Abiola, made suspicion almost inevitable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adst.org/2015/12/the-stolen-victory-and-mysterious-death-of-moshood-abiola/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adst.org">[Diplomatic Studies &amp; Training]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on adst.org.</span></span></span>

The most famous allegation was that Abiola had been poisoned through a cup of tea. Others claimed that he had been assaulted or deliberately denied adequate medical care. The tea story became powerful because it offered a concrete object and an obvious group of final witnesses. Yet repetition has often blurred an important distinction: Abiola’s illness occurred during the meeting, but that does not establish that anything served to him caused it.

An international team of pathologists examined the death. The official account reported substantial pre-existing heart disease and concluded that rapid deterioration of his diseased heart caused his death. The United States government stated that the findings did not support poisoning. First-hand diplomatic accounts have likewise described an abrupt medical emergency rather than observable foul play.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/statements/1998/ps980711.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 1997-2001.state.gov">[State.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">1997-2001.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gov7/11/98: Nigeria: Autopsy Results-Chief M.K.O. Abiola</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gov7/11/98: Nigeria: Autopsy Results-Chief M.K.O. Abiola</span></span></span>

Why, then, has the question persisted? Partly because Abiola had spent four years in state custody, where independent scrutiny of his treatment and health was limited. A natural mechanism of death does not settle every question about whether imprisonment, stress or deficient medical care contributed to his decline. More broadly, Nigerians had strong reasons to distrust official narratives after years of military secrecy, political violence and the annulment of an election whose complete results were never formally declared by the state.

The poisoning theory remains unproved and is inconsistent with the published medical conclusion. At the same time, treating the autopsy as if it erased the political context is too simple. Abiola’s death was medically attributed to heart disease, but it occurred after prolonged detention imposed by a regime that had denied him the office voters apparently chose him to hold. The enduring mystery is therefore partly forensic and partly institutional: many citizens did not trust the authorities to tell the truth, even when those authorities offered a medically plausible explanation.

## The Chibok girls who remain unaccounted for

During the night of 14–15 April 2014, Boko Haram fighters attacked the Government Girls Secondary School in Chibok, Borno State, and abducted 276 pupils. Some escaped soon afterwards. Others were released through negotiations, rescued by military operations or emerged from captivity years later. The kidnapping became internationally known through the Bring Back Our Girls campaign.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/decade-tragedy-nigerias-chibok-girls-endures-outside-spotlight-2024-04-11/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A decade on, tragedy of Nigeria&#x27;s Chibok Girls endures outside the spotlight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A decade on, tragedy of Nigeria&#x27;s Chibok Girls endures outside the spotlight</span></span></span>

For many families, however, the central question remains unanswered: is their daughter alive, and where is she?

Exact totals have varied because organisations use different dates and classifications. In April 2024, Amnesty International said that 82 of the girls remained in captivity or unaccounted for. Reuters reported that roughly 90 were still missing and noted assessments that some had died from illness, childbirth, hunger, snakebite or military strikes while in captivity. These are informed estimates, not complete individual records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2024/04/nigeria-decade-after-boko-haram-attack-on-chibok-82-girls-still-in-captivity/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Nigeria: Decade after Boko Haram attack on Chibok, 82</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Nigeria: Decade after Boko Haram attack on Chibok, 82</span></span></span>

The uncertainty is not evidence that the girls simply vanished without trace. Testimony from returnees has established that captives were separated, moved between camps, forced into marriages, subjected to coercion and caught in the changing fortunes of Boko Haram factions. Some women had children during captivity. Others may have died without their deaths being independently documented. The fragmentation of the insurgency and the remoteness of its former territories make a single definitive list difficult to produce.

Several popular claims require care. Not every missing girl can safely be presumed alive, but neither can unverified reports of death provide families with certainty. Women found with children or former fighters should not automatically be treated as willing members of the group; years of coercion, dependency and survival under captivity complicate ideas of consent. Nor did the occasional recovery of an abductee mean the broader case had been solved. A Chibok woman was found alive with children in 2024, a decade after the raid, demonstrating why categorical assumptions can be wrong.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/apr/18/nigeria-woman-rescued-10-years-after-kidnap-boko-haram-chibok" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

Chibok also became part of a wider national pattern. Mass abductions from schools continued after 2014, carried out by insurgents or armed criminal groups seeking money, leverage and publicity. The original kidnapping therefore matters not only as an unresolved case but as a warning that was never fully converted into effective protection for vulnerable schools.

This is the most sensitive of Nigeria’s national mysteries because its subjects are not historical characters onto whom speculation can safely be projected. They are women with families, personal histories and legal rights. The responsible approach is to preserve distinctions between confirmed returnees, confirmed deaths, credible reports and people whose fate remains genuinely unknown.

## Why so many Nigerian mysteries remain open

These cases are very different, but they share a recurring problem: the institutions that preserve and test evidence have often been weakened by extraction, conflict, political interference or neglect.

For ancient history, looting removes objects from the soil and breaks the relationship between an artefact and its surroundings. Earlier excavations may also have used recording and dating methods that later research can improve but cannot entirely redo. This is why the Nok and Igbo-Ukwu puzzles are gradually clarified by scientific work while some lost context can never be restored.

For political cases, secrecy is the central obstacle. Military rule placed security agencies beyond effective public scrutiny, while witnesses and suspects could avoid questioning or outlast commissions of inquiry. In such conditions, an official explanation may be correct, partly correct or self-protective; public distrust grows because the process cannot be independently tested. The Giwa and Abiola cases show two different results of that distrust: one killing remains judicially unresolved, while one death has a strong medical explanation that has never fully displaced suspicion.

Nigeria’s record-keeping difficulties deepen the problem. Archives have suffered from underfunding, fragile paper collections, restricted access and incomplete transfer of government records. Nigerian cultural organisations have recently pursued new preservation schemes, including placing digital copies of historical and cultural material in the Arctic World Archive, partly because so much documentary memory remains vulnerable to physical and digital loss.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/mar/17/arctic-world-archive-nigeria-history-culture-svalbard" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Led by historian Nze Ed Emeka Keazor and supported by figures like Ugoma Ebilah and Dr. Chima Korieh, the project responds to Nigeria’s a...</span></span></span>

That does not justify filling gaps with conspiracy. A useful hierarchy is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, physical evidence or multiple independent witnesses.
* **Strong inference:** the explanation that best fits the surviving evidence, although some links remain unproved.
* **Disputed allegation:** a claim made by identifiable witnesses or investigators but challenged or never tested.
* **Legend or modern embellishment:** a dramatic story repeated without traceable early evidence.
* **Demonstrably misleading claim:** an account contradicted by strong forensic, archaeological or documentary findings.

</div>

Applying those distinctions does not make Nigeria’s past less intriguing. It makes the real mysteries clearer. The Nok sculptures are puzzling because looting destroyed knowledge, not because aliens or forgotten outsiders made them. Igbo-Ukwu is remarkable because local achievement exceeded colonial expectations, not because its technology had no African explanation. Mungo Park’s death is uncertain in detail but not an inexplicable disappearance. Abiola’s poisoning remains an allegation, while his treatment by the state is established history. Giwa’s assassination is known to have occurred, but responsibility has never been proved. The missing Chibok girls are not material for folklore at all; they represent an unfinished obligation to establish individual fates and account for institutional failure.

## What these enigmas reveal about Nigeria

Nigeria’s enduring mysteries are ultimately struggles over who controls memory. Archaeological objects were removed from communities and circulated as anonymous art. Colonial exploration narratives often made African witnesses secondary in their own landscapes. Military governments controlled records and investigations. Insurgent violence scattered victims across inaccessible territory. In every case, uncertainty was produced by human decisions as much as by the passage of time.

The best evidence rarely supports the most spectacular theory. It instead reveals something more consequential: lost archaeological context, unequal power, fragile archives, failed investigations and families denied reliable answers. That is why these cases continue to matter. Solving them, where solution is still possible, depends less on imaginative speculation than on preserving sites, opening records, protecting witnesses, applying modern scientific methods and treating uncertainty honestly.
