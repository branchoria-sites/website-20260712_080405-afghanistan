## Introduction

The clearest examples include the concealment and later desecration of the Asante [Golden Stool]({{ 'golden-stool/' | relative_url }}), the dispersal of royal treasures looted from Kumasi, the unfinished archaeological record of [Begho]({{ 'begho/' | relative_url }}), the disputed Kulungugu assassination case, the 1982 murder of three judges and a retired officer, and the killing of the King of Dagbon in 2002. These cases matter because uncertainty has shaped public memory almost as strongly as the confirmed facts. They also show that “unresolved” can mean several different things: the evidence may be missing, politically contested, scattered across foreign collections, or simply unable to prove responsibility beyond reasonable doubt.

## The Golden Stool the British never captured

The Golden Stool is the sacred symbol of the Asante nation and the authority of the Asantehene, or Asante king. Asante tradition holds that it embodies the collective spirit of the nation rather than functioning as an ordinary throne. The king does not sit on it. Its religious and political meaning helps explain why a British demand concerning the Stool provoked such an explosive response in 1900.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pdfproc.lib.msu.edu/?file=%2FDMC%2FAfrican+Journals%2Fpdfs%2FInstitue+of+African+Studies+Research+Review%2F1968v4n3%2Fasrv004003002.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pdfproc.lib.msu.edu">[PDF Processing at MSU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pdfproc.lib.msu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF Processing at MSUTHE GOLDEN STOOL AND THE OATHS TO THE KING...by GP Hagan · Cited by 19 — To Ashantis, the Golden Stool is the sourc...</span></span></span>

In March 1900, the colonial governor, Frederick Hodgson, demanded that the Golden Stool be surrendered and asked why he had not been permitted to sit on it. The demand helped trigger the last major Anglo-Asante war, commonly called the War of the Golden Stool or the Yaa Asantewaa War. British forces eventually suppressed the resistance and incorporated Asante more firmly into the Gold Coast colony, but they did not capture the sacred object they had sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gs.uwpress.org/content/wpgs/3/1/61.full-text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gs.uwpress.org">[gs.uwpress.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gs.uwpress.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">61.full text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9, “Desecration of Golden Stool, 1921–. 1928”; PRO, CO.96/627, “The Golden Stool Case: 1921”; RH, Mss. Brit. Emp...Read more...</span></span></span>

The genuine mystery is not whether the Stool survived. It did. The unresolved element is how its guardians concealed it so successfully, who knew its location at different moments, and how later accounts of its hiding were shaped by secrecy and political necessity. Historian T. C. McCaskie’s study of colonial records shows officials following uncertain leads and dealing with informants who claimed knowledge of the hiding place but sometimes refused to reveal it. The archival trail records the colonial search more clearly than the Asante system that defeated it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gs.uwpress.org/content/wpgs/3/1/61.full-text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gs.uwpress.org">[gs.uwpress.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gs.uwpress.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">61.full text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9, “Desecration of Golden Stool, 1921–. 1928”; PRO, CO.96/627, “The Golden Stool Case: 1921”; RH, Mss. Brit. Emp...Read more...</span></span></span>

In 1921, labourers working near the hidden Stool found it and removed some of its gold ornaments. The discovery produced another crisis. Asante authorities regarded the act as desecration and tried the men under customary law. Colonial officials intervened against death sentences, and the offenders were ultimately banished. The Stool then emerged from concealment after the British administration promised not to interfere with it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gs.uwpress.org/content/wpgs/3/1/61.full-text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gs.uwpress.org">[gs.uwpress.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gs.uwpress.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">61.full text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9, “Desecration of Golden Stool, 1921–. 1928”; PRO, CO.96/627, “The Golden Stool Case: 1921”; RH, Mss. Brit. Emp...Read more...</span></span></span>

Popular retellings sometimes claim that the original Golden Stool remains lost and that only a replica survives. The historical evidence does not support that dramatic version. The stronger conclusion is more interesting: the Stool was successfully hidden during the colonial conquest, was accidentally exposed two decades later, and then returned to protected ceremonial life. What remains obscure is the full network of people, places and decisions that kept it beyond British reach.

## Where did the looted Asante treasures go?

When British troops entered Kumasi during the Anglo-Asante wars of 1873–74 and 1895–96, they seized royal and ceremonial property from the Asante court. Some pieces were treated as official military loot, some were sold at auction, and others entered private collections through less transparent routes. The British Museum acknowledges that important objects in its Asante collection were taken during these campaigns, including regalia associated with the royal palace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/about-us/british-museum-story/contested-objects-collection/asante-gold-regalia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">asante gold regalia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British MuseumAsante Gold RegaliaIt was looted during the Anglo-Asante war of 1873–4 and possibly formed part of the indemnity payment ex...</span></span></span>

There is therefore no mystery about whether looting occurred. The puzzle is one of scale and dispersal. No complete public inventory appears to account for everything removed from Kumasi. Objects passed through military units, auction houses, dealers, museums and family collections. Provenance records — the documented history of an object’s ownership — are often partial. A ceremonial piece may be identifiable as Asante while its precise palace use, original owner or route out of Ghana remains uncertain.

One revealing case is the British Museum’s Asante Ewer, a medieval English vessel that had acquired a new ceremonial life in the Asante court before British forces seized it in 1896. Its history challenges the simple idea that museum objects belong neatly to one place or period: it was made in Europe, absorbed into Asante ritual culture and then carried back to Britain as colonial loot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/about-us/press/press-releases/announcing-asante-ewer-exhibition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">announcing asante ewer exhibition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">announcing asante ewer exhibition</span></span></span>

The search for dispersed royal property has recently produced significant returns. In 2024, the British Museum and Victoria and Albert Museum sent 32 Asante objects to the Manhyia Palace Museum under renewable loan agreements. Seven objects from the Fowler Museum in California were returned separately. By November 2024, Manhyia reported receiving a further 28 pieces through AngloGold Ashanti, bringing the number of newly returned or restituted objects displayed by the kingdom to 67.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e5aa77f9c07ab8952e507dd01e983c76" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

These developments do not close the case. Loans leave legal ownership with the lending museums, while permanent restitution transfers it. British national museums face statutory restrictions on removing objects from their collections, which is why the 2024 agreement was structured as a loan rather than an unconditional return. Other Asante objects remain in military, institutional and private holdings, some of them rarely accessible to the public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/jan/26/first-edition-ghana-asante-gold" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The enduring question is consequently broader than “where is the treasure?” It is which objects survive, whether their histories can be reconstructed, and who should control them. Each rediscovered catalogue entry or private collection may restore another piece of Asante history, but a complete accounting of the palace treasures taken during the colonial wars remains unlikely.

## Begho and the archaeological mystery in the archives

Near the present-day community of Hani in Ghana’s Bono Region lie the remains of Begho, a major precolonial market town. Archaeological and historical evidence links it to long-distance trade connecting the forest zones of modern Ghana with commercial centres around the Middle Niger. Finds from the site indicate craft production, metalworking and interaction among communities with different cultural and religious traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ugspace.ug.edu.gh/items/c5d9177c-c2cf-4017-a3e5-b7ac167e5480" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ugspace.ug.edu.gh">[UGSpace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ugspace.ug.edu.gh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.gh.</span></span></span>

University of Ghana archaeologists led by Merrick Posnansky investigated Begho extensively between 1970 and 1979. Their work identified several settlement quarters and produced field notes, maps, photographs and thousands of artefacts. Yet much of this material was not fully published or integrated into an accessible final account. Scholars began referring to the situation as the “Begho mystery”: an important West African site had been excavated, but its archaeological record remained difficult to evaluate because the documentation and objects were dispersed through storerooms and archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/378223818_Begho_Ghana_revisited_examining_archaeological_archives_and_%27excavating%27_museum_storages_Universite_Toulouse_-_Jean_Jaures_2023" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This is not a mystery in the sense of a vanished civilisation without evidence. Begho’s ruins are known, its trading importance is supported by archaeology, and research continues. The problem is that the original excavations generated more information than was made readily available. Without the complete stratigraphic records — the documentation showing how objects and structures relate to layers of occupation — later researchers could not easily test early conclusions about the town’s age, population and development.

Recent work has begun to recover that lost context. Archaeologist Daniel Kumah examined more than 3,000 archival records and reconnected them with artefacts held at the University of Ghana’s Museum of Archaeology. A broader project is now addressing how Ghana can preserve archaeological archives so that excavated materials, field records and later interpretations remain linked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0067270X.2024.2316481" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span>

Important questions nevertheless remain. When did Begho reach its greatest extent? Were its apparently separate quarters occupied at the same time? Did warfare cause a sudden collapse, or did commercial routes and populations shift gradually? Oral traditions, historical references and excavated evidence do not always point to a single date or explanation.

Begho demonstrates how an archaeological mystery can be created after excavation rather than before it. The evidence was not buried solely in the ground; much of it became buried in institutional storage. Recovering the city now requires archival detective work as much as digging.

## Who was behind the Kulungugu bombing?

On 1 August 1962, a bomb exploded during President Kwame Nkrumah’s visit to Kulungugu in northern Ghana, near the border with what was then Upper Volta. Nkrumah was injured, and other people were killed or wounded. The attack occurred during a period of increasing conflict between his Convention People’s Party government and political opponents, some of whom were in exile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kulungugu_bomb_attack" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kulungugu bomb attack</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kulungugu bomb attack</span></span></span>

The investigation soon focused on senior figures within Nkrumah’s own administration. Tawia Adamafio, Ako Adjei and H. H. Cofie-Crabbe were among those accused of involvement. Their prosecution became one of the most controversial legal episodes of Ghana’s First Republic.

A special court headed by Chief Justice Sir Arku Korsah acquitted the principal defendants. Nkrumah’s government rejected the outcome, detained the acquitted men, removed Korsah from office and changed the constitutional arrangements so that another court could try the case. The defendants were then convicted and sentenced to death, although their sentences were later commuted. They were released after Nkrumah was overthrown in 1966.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/archive/6872035/ghana-outrage-at-law/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on time.com.</span></span></span>

Those events make the convictions difficult to treat as a satisfactory solution. The first court was not persuaded that the prosecution had proved its case. The second trial took place after direct political intervention in the judiciary. Later accounts have also attributed contradictory views to Nkrumah himself, including the suggestion that he privately doubted Adamafio’s guilt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ghanalawhub.com/the-adamafio-treason-trial-and-the-fall-of-ghanas-first-republic/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ghanalawhub.com">[:: Ghana Law Hub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ghanalawhub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the adamafio treason trial and the fall of ghanas first republic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the adamafio treason trial and the fall of ghanas first republic</span></span></span>

Several possibilities remain open. The attack may have been organised by exiled opponents, by conspirators within the government or security services, or by a network crossing those categories. Foreign involvement has often been alleged, but allegations are not proof. Cold War intelligence activity in West Africa makes external participation conceivable, yet no publicly available documentary chain conclusively identifies the organiser who supplied and authorised the bomb.

The Kulungugu mystery therefore has two layers. The first is the original question of who planned the assassination attempt. The second is how the government’s determination to secure convictions damaged the possibility of obtaining a trusted answer. Political interference turned a criminal investigation into a constitutional crisis, leaving later generations uncertain whether the condemned men were conspirators, scapegoats or some combination of insiders with differing levels of knowledge.

## The judges’ murders and the missing chain of command

On the night of 30 June 1982, High Court judges Fred Poku Sarkodee, Cecilia Koranteng-Addow and Kwadwo Agyei Agyepong were abducted from their homes. Retired army officer Major Sam Acquah was also taken. Their bodies were later found at the Bundase military range outside Accra. The killings occurred during the early months of the Provisional National Defence Council, or PNDC, led by Jerry Rawlings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://judicial.gov.gh/jsweb/index.php/about-the-judiciary/martyrs-of-the-rule-of-law" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: judicial.gov.gh">[Judicial Service of Ghana]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">judicial.gov.gh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on judicial.gov.gh.</span></span></span>

The victims were not random. The judges had handled politically sensitive cases involving people dismissed or punished during earlier military interventions. Their murders shocked Ghana because they appeared to be an attack on the judiciary itself.

A Special Investigation Board headed by former Chief Justice Samuel Azu Crabbe was established under PNDC Law 15. Its inquiry identified direct participants, and several defendants were eventually convicted. Joachim Amartey Kwei, a PNDC member, and other accused men were executed. One convicted participant, Lance Corporal Kwaku Amedeka, escaped custody and was tried in his absence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ghalii.org/akn/gh/act/pndcl/1982/15/eng%401982" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ghalii.org">[Ghalii]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ghalii.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ghalii.org.</span></span></span>

The convictions established that identifiable individuals carried out the abductions and killings. They did not settle the most politically important question: whether the operation was ordered, encouraged or knowingly protected by people higher in the state or security structure.

The board examined allegations concerning Captain Kojo Tsikata, a senior PNDC security official, but the subsequent handling of the findings became deeply controversial. The National Reconciliation Commission, established two decades later to investigate abuses under Ghana’s previous governments, revisited testimony and competing claims surrounding the case. Yet no later judicial process produced a universally accepted account of a wider chain of command.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hmcwordpress.humanities.mcmaster.ca/Truthcommissions/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Ghana.NRC_.Report-FULL.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hmcwordpress.humanities.mcmaster.ca">[mcmaster.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hmcwordpress.humanities.mcmaster.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ghana.NR C.Report FULL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ghana.NR C.Report FULL</span></span></span>

The mystery should not be overstated. The victims, location and several perpetrators are known. It is not an entirely unsolved crime. What remains unresolved is the boundary between the convicted group and the political system around them: whether they acted independently, interpreted a hostile political climate as permission, or followed instructions that were never documented or proved.

Ghana marks 30 June as Martyrs’ Day in honour of the judges. The commemoration has made the case part of the country’s civic memory, symbolising the danger faced by courts when political power treats independent judgements as disloyalty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://judicial.gov.gh/jsweb/index.php/about-the-judiciary/martyrs-of-the-rule-of-law" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: judicial.gov.gh">[Judicial Service of Ghana]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">judicial.gov.gh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on judicial.gov.gh.</span></span></span>

## Who killed the King of Dagbon?

Yaa Naa Yakubu Andani II, the King of Dagbon, was killed in Yendi on 27 March 2002 after several days of fighting between supporters of Dagbon’s Andani and Abudu royal families. Numerous other people died during the violence, and the king’s palace was attacked and burned. His death was not simply another casualty of communal disorder: he was killed and decapitated while state security forces were present in or around a town already subject to emergency restrictions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/48732470" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

The succession dispute between the two royal families had roots extending far beyond 2002. Governments had repeatedly attempted to regulate festivals, funerals and access to the kingship, while party politics added another layer of distrust. The violence therefore emerged from a long conflict, but its precise organisation remains disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ugspace.ug.edu.gh/bitstreams/fbf28b93-f626-400d-af11-255779040c70/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ugspace.ug.edu.gh">[UGSpace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ugspace.ug.edu.gh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.gh.</span></span></span>

The Wuaku Commission investigated the events and reconstructed a detailed chronology of the fighting. Its report described escalating confrontations around the Gbewaa Palace, failures of security and the roles of named participants. Investigation, however, did not translate into definitive criminal responsibility for the king’s death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.modernghana.com/news/30184/executive-summary-of-the-wuaku-commission-report.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: modernghana.com">[Modern Ghana]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">modernghana.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Modern Ghana Executive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Modern Ghana Executive</span></span></span>

An early prosecution failed when the court concluded that the state had proved the Yaa Naa was dead but had not proved that the accused man killed him. Later proceedings against other suspects also ended without convictions that resolved the central question. The difficulties included confused battlefield conditions, disputed witness accounts, political accusations and the challenge of assigning individual responsibility within a large armed confrontation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ghanaweb.com/GhanaHomePage/NewsArchive/Court-frees-Ya-Na-Andani-s-alleged-killer-40222" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ghanaweb.com">[GhanaWeb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ghanaweb.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ghanaweb.com.</span></span></span>

Traditional mediation eventually achieved what the courts had not: a path out of the succession crisis. The completion of disputed royal funerals allowed a new Yaa Naa to be installed in 2019, easing a conflict that had paralysed Dagbon’s kingship. Reconciliation, however, did not identify the person or group that planned and executed the 2002 regicide.

The case remains sensitive because peace and justice are not identical. Reconciliation required communities to accept a political and customary settlement, while the criminal question remained open. Claims that one party, government or security agency certainly orchestrated the killing continue to circulate, but they go beyond what the available judicial record has proved.

## What Ghana’s mysteries have in common

These cases arose in very different periods, but the uncertainty surrounding them follows recurring patterns.

**Power controlled the evidence.** Colonial administrators documented their search for the Golden Stool, but not the complete Asante network that protected it. Political authorities influenced the Kulungugu prosecutions. Security institutions possessed information relevant to the judges’ murders and the violence in Yendi.

**Records became separated from their meaning.** Begho’s artefacts survived, but many could not readily answer larger questions until researchers reunited them with field notes, plans and photographs. Asante objects in overseas collections also became detached from the ceremonies, people and locations that once explained them.

**A conviction did not necessarily solve the case.** Men were convicted over the Kulungugu bombing and the judges’ murders, yet political interference or uncertainty about higher responsibility left the public unconvinced that the full truth had emerged.

**Reconciliation sometimes overtook investigation.** Dagbon restored its kingship through mediation without securing a conclusive criminal judgement over the Yaa Naa’s death. The settlement reduced the danger of renewed conflict, but it did not make the historical record complete.

The most responsible way to approach Ghana’s historical mysteries is therefore to resist both extremes. Official findings should not automatically be treated as the whole truth, especially when institutions were politically compromised. Equally, gaps in the record do not prove elaborate conspiracies. The strongest conclusions distinguish what is established from what is merely possible.

The Golden Stool survived concealment; it is not a permanently lost relic. British forces demonstrably looted Asante royal property, although the complete fate of that property is unknown. Begho was a real trading centre, not a mythical vanished city, but its history remains partly obscured by an incomplete archaeological archive. The Kulungugu attack, the judges’ murders and the death of the Yaa Naa all occurred under circumstances that are substantially documented, while responsibility beyond the immediate actors remains contested.

That mixture of knowledge and uncertainty is why the cases endure. They are not blank spaces waiting for a dramatic invention. They are fractured histories in which archives, objects, court records and living memory preserve different parts of the truth.
