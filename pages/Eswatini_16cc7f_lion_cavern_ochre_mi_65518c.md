## Introduction

Lion Cavern, on the Ngwenya Massif in western [Eswatini]({{ 'what-remains-unsolved-in-eswatinis-past/' | relative_url }}), preserves one of the world's most remarkable archaeological puzzles. There is now strong evidence that people deliberately extracted rich red haematite ochre from the cavern around 48,000 years ago, making it the oldest known site of intensive ochre mining yet identified. What remains unknown is who those miners were, how they organised the work, why this particular source mattered so much, and how far the pigment travelled. Recent scientific research has settled the question of whether purposeful mining took place, but it has also revealed a much more intriguing mystery: the social world behind one of humanity's earliest mining landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

Unlike later mining communities, the people who worked Lion Cavern left no written records. Archaeologists must reconstruct their activities from excavation traces, dating methods, stone tools, chemistry and the movement of ochre across the landscape. Each new discovery answers one question while raising several others.

## Who mined Lion Cavern 48,000 years ago?

The simple answer is that nobody knows.

The miners lived during the later Middle Stone Age, tens of thousands of years before modern kingdoms, ethnic identities or national borders existed in southern Africa. Any attempt to identify them with present-day peoples goes far beyond what the archaeological evidence can support. Instead, researchers speak of hunter-gatherer communities who possessed detailed knowledge of local geology and repeatedly returned to the Ngwenya deposit over long periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

What makes Lion Cavern exceptional is not merely that ochre was collected there. Humans had gathered naturally exposed ochre elsewhere much earlier. Lion Cavern shows something more organised: people deliberately entered the deposit and extracted large quantities of high-quality mineral pigment from underground rather than simply picking up loose fragments on the surface. That distinction is why archaeologists describe it as the earliest known evidence of intensive ochre mining.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

## How archaeologists identified purposeful mining

Early excavations during the twentieth century suggested great antiquity, but questions remained. Some of the oldest radiocarbon dates came from charcoal rather than directly from mining activity, and modern iron ore extraction damaged parts of the original archaeological deposits. Critics therefore asked whether Lion Cavern represented sustained mining, occasional visits or several unrelated episodes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

Recent research has greatly strengthened the case through several independent lines of evidence.

### Improved dating

Instead of relying only on charcoal, researchers applied optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dating. This technique estimates when buried sediments were last exposed to sunlight, providing a more direct chronology for deposits associated with mining activity. The new dates reaffirm intensive extraction around 48,000 years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

### Physical traces of extraction

Excavations revealed worked underground areas, mining debris and evidence that people removed substantial quantities of dense haematite from within the deposit. The scale of disturbance is inconsistent with casual collection from natural exposures alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

### Chemical fingerprinting

Perhaps the strongest new evidence comes from geochemical analysis. Scientists examined 173 ochre specimens from fifteen archaeological sites and compared their elemental compositions with geological sources across Eswatini. High-grade ochre from the Ngwenya Massif possesses distinctive chemical signatures that allow many archaeological pieces to be matched back to Lion Cavern and nearby outcrops.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

Because these conclusions rely on dating, excavation evidence and geochemical sourcing rather than a single technique, confidence in the interpretation has increased considerably.

## What the movement of ochre reveals about ancient communities

The chemical study transformed Lion Cavern from an isolated archaeological site into the centre of a much larger story.

Researchers found that high-quality Ngwenya ochre appeared repeatedly at numerous Stone Age sites across western and eastern Eswatini. In many places, people consistently selected Ngwenya material even though other pigment sources existed closer by. This pattern suggests deliberate preference rather than simple convenience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

Several possibilities could explain this repeated choice:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* The pigment may have produced brighter or more durable colours than local alternatives.
* Particular colours or textures may have held symbolic or social importance.
* Communities may have maintained regular journeys to Ngwenya over generations.
* Ochre may have circulated through exchange networks linking different groups.

</div>

The evidence clearly demonstrates movement of material across the landscape. What it cannot yet determine is the mechanism behind that movement. Archaeologists cannot distinguish with certainty between trade, gift exchange, seasonal travel, marriage connections or wider population mobility using ochre alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why was the ochre so valuable?

This is one of the enduring mysteries.

Ochre served many purposes in prehistoric societies worldwide, and several may have applied simultaneously at Lion Cavern.

Practical uses include preparing animal hides, mixing adhesives for stone tools and creating protective coatings. Other evidence from African archaeological sites shows ochre associated with personal decoration, rock art, ritual activities and symbolic expression. None of these uses excludes the others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

The Ngwenya deposit itself offers clues. Its iron-rich haematite produced an intense red pigment with relatively few impurities, making it especially suitable for grinding into fine powder. Such material would have been attractive both for practical applications and for activities in which colour quality mattered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

Yet no archaeological method can reveal exactly what individual miners believed about the pigment. The symbolic meanings attached to colour, if any, remain beyond direct recovery.

## The questions that archaeology still cannot answer

Although recent discoveries resolved the debate over whether intensive mining occurred, the most human questions remain unanswered.

### Who controlled access?

There is no evidence showing whether particular families, bands or wider communities managed the mine, or whether access was open to anyone who knew the location.

### How was mining organised?

The extraction of hard iron-rich rock probably required cooperation and technical knowledge. Archaeologists suspect that mining methods and geological knowledge were passed between generations, but the details of that learning process remain invisible in the archaeological record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

### How extensive were exchange networks?

Chemical sourcing demonstrates that Ngwenya ochre travelled, but not whether it changed hands through direct exchange, seasonal movement or population mobility. Future sourcing studies beyond Eswatini may reveal even wider networks.

### Why did traditions endure?

The repeated preference for Ngwenya pigment across thousands of years suggests remarkable cultural continuity. Whether this reflects practical superiority, social identity, ritual importance or several overlapping factors remains one of the site's most compelling unanswered questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why Lion Cavern still matters

Lion Cavern changes how archaeologists think about the abilities of Middle Stone Age people. Rather than exploiting whatever resources happened to lie nearby, they appear to have recognised the special qualities of particular mineral deposits, organised repeated extraction, transported selected materials across considerable distances and maintained that knowledge over many generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53050-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature Communications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ochre communities of practice in Stone Age Eswatini &#124; Nature CommunicationsOctober 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2024</span></span></span>

The mystery is therefore no longer whether early mining took place. The evidence strongly supports that conclusion. The unresolved puzzle concerns the people themselves: their identities, beliefs, social relationships and reasons for investing so much effort in obtaining a mineral whose value extended far beyond its simple geological origin. That unanswered human story is what makes Lion Cavern one of Eswatini's most significant archaeological mysteries.
