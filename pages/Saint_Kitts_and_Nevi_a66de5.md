## Introduction

Saint Kitts and Nevis has one outstanding historical mystery: what really happened to [Jamestown]({{ 'jamestown/' | relative_url }}), the supposedly sunken capital of Nevis. Around it sit other, darker puzzles — especially the disputed colonial story used to justify the 1626 massacre of the Kalinago at [Bloody Point]({{ 'bloody-point/' | relative_url }}), and the remarkably durable legend of a pirate said to have escaped when an earthquake swallowed a town.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Saint_Kitts_and_Nevi_a66de5-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Saint Kitts and Nevis" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

These are not equally mysterious. Archaeology has substantially explained Jamestown, although it has not settled every question about the earthquake and disturbed sea of 1690. The killing at Bloody Point is historically well established, but the alleged Indigenous conspiracy that preceded it rests largely on accounts produced by the colonising side. The pirate “[Red Legs]({{ 'red-legs/' | relative_url }})” Greaves, meanwhile, appears to belong more to twentieth-century storytelling than to documented seventeenth-century history. Together, the cases show how disasters, colonial archives, missing voices and tourism-friendly legends can turn uncertain events into national folklore.

## Did Jamestown really sink beneath the sea?

The familiar story is dramatic. Jamestown, an early commercial settlement on the west coast of Nevis, was supposedly destroyed without warning by an earthquake and tsunami in April 1690. Buildings collapsed, the sea swept over the town and the surviving inhabitants abandoned the site. Later retellings transformed this disaster into the image of an entire Caribbean town lying beneath the water.

There was certainly a serious earthquake in the Leeward Islands in April 1690. Historical disaster records report heavy damage on Saint Kitts and Nevis, ground fissures on Saint Kitts and disturbed sea conditions around Nevis. The exact date varies between modern catalogues, partly because seventeenth-century documents used inconsistent calendars and because later writers copied one another. The evidence therefore supports a real seismic event, but not every detail of the popular Jamestown narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uwi.edu/ekacdm/saint-kitts-and-nevis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwi.edu">[uwi.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwi.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">saint kitts and nevis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Kitts and Nevis &#124; EKACDMOn the 6th April 1690 Earthquake caused serious damage throughout St. Kitts &amp; Nevis. The most important challenge to the sunken-city legend came from the Nevis Heritage Project, a long-running archaeological and historical investigation associated with the University of Southampton. Researchers examined maps, colonial papers, surface remains and excavated material around Morton’s Bay and the coastal area traditionally identified as Jamestown. Their work located an early settlement, fortifications and commercial activity, but found that the site’s history was more complicated than a prosperous capital suddenly disappearing into the sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.southampton.ac.uk/assets/imported/transforms/content-block/UsefulDownloads_Download/4B532860420C4E4EA83B3291B94EBA40/nevis_2002_Theme_two.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southampton.ac.uk">[University of Southampton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southampton.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of SouthamptonJAMESTOWN, MORTON&#x27;S BAY AND JAMES FORT: MYTH...By 1676, Morton&#x27;s Bay was referred to as the second biggest town...</span></span></span>

### A town, a port or an exaggerated label?

One basic puzzle is what Jamestown actually was. Seventeenth-century records more often refer to Morton’s Bay than to a clearly defined town called Jamestown. In 1676 the place was described as Nevis’s second-largest settlement after Charlestown, yet it was also said to contain relatively few houses. That sounds less like a substantial capital than a modest port, anchorage and trading community whose importance fluctuated over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.southampton.ac.uk/assets/imported/transforms/content-block/UsefulDownloads_Download/4B532860420C4E4EA83B3291B94EBA40/nevis_2002_Theme_two.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southampton.ac.uk">[University of Southampton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southampton.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of SouthamptonJAMESTOWN, MORTON&#x27;S BAY AND JAMES FORT: MYTH...By 1676, Morton&#x27;s Bay was referred to as the second biggest town...</span></span></span>

The archaeological evidence supports occupation but does not neatly match later descriptions of a grand lost city. Researchers identified building remains and artefacts associated with trade and domestic life, while documentary work reconstructed the relationship between Morton’s Bay, James Fort and the supposed town. The resulting picture is of a dispersed coastal settlement rather than a densely built Caribbean equivalent of Pompeii.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/1BDAC4CABE0ABC290D8A17AADCC7F686/9781787446021c9_p177-196_CBO.pdf/jamestown-nevis-fact-fiction-and-fable.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resolve.cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resolve.cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment9 Jamestown, Nevis: Fact, Fiction, and Fable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment9 Jamestown, Nevis: Fact, Fiction, and FableSUMMARY: The destruction of Jamestown, Nevis, in 1690...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because “lost city” stories tend to expand whenever the original settlement is poorly mapped. A few buildings, warehouses, defensive works and landing places can become a single large town in later memory. Coastal erosion then supplies visible clues — fragments of brick or masonry appearing after storms — which seem to confirm that streets and houses are hidden offshore.

### What happened in 1690?

The best-supported conclusion is that an earthquake occurred and the sea behaved unusually, but the scale and precise effect of any tsunami at Jamestown remain uncertain. One strand of archaeological work reported possible physical evidence consistent with a tsunami. Other research emphasised that no convincing documentary record of Jamestown’s wholesale destruction had been located, despite the survival of extensive paperwork about later disasters and military attacks on Nevis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.southampton.ac.uk/assets/imported/transforms/content-block/UsefulDownloads_Download/CA3C2F36F5704DC0A555C265164368A8/nevis_2001_Theme_three_part2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southampton.ac.uk">[University of Southampton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southampton.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on southampton.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That silence is striking. When the French attacked Nevis in 1706, officials, landowners and merchants produced claims, damage reports and petitions. The 1692 destruction of Port Royal in Jamaica also generated numerous descriptions. If Nevis’s principal town had been suddenly erased in 1690, historians might reasonably expect a similar paper trail. Its absence does not prove that no destructive wave occurred, but it weakens the strongest versions of the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.southampton.ac.uk/assets/imported/transforms/content-block/UsefulDownloads_Download/4B532860420C4E4EA83B3291B94EBA40/nevis_2002_Theme_two.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southampton.ac.uk">[University of Southampton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southampton.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of SouthamptonJAMESTOWN, MORTON&#x27;S BAY AND JAMES FORT: MYTH...By 1676, Morton&#x27;s Bay was referred to as the second biggest town...</span></span></span>

Researchers have also found evidence that settlement in the area continued after 1690. A town completely abandoned at the moment of catastrophe should produce a relatively sharp archaeological ending. Instead, Jamestown appears to have declined through a mixture of environmental change, shifting trade, coastal instability and the growth of Charlestown. The “one terrible afternoon” explanation is therefore too simple.

Modern scholarship describes the case as a collision between fact, fiction and fable. A real earthquake became attached to an imperfectly understood settlement; hurried reports were enlarged by Victorian writing, local histories and popular archaeology; eventually Jamestown was remembered as a complete town resting beneath the Caribbean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/1BDAC4CABE0ABC290D8A17AADCC7F686/9781787446021c9_p177-196_CBO.pdf/jamestown-nevis-fact-fiction-and-fable.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resolve.cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resolve.cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment9 Jamestown, Nevis: Fact, Fiction, and Fable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment9 Jamestown, Nevis: Fact, Fiction, and FableSUMMARY: The destruction of Jamestown, Nevis, in 1690...</span></span></span>

### The most plausible verdict

Jamestown was real, although its size and status have often been overstated. The 1690 earthquake was also real, and some form of abnormal sea movement may have affected Nevis. What remains unproved is the familiar image of a thriving capital entirely engulfed by a tsunami.

The likeliest explanation is gradual decline accelerated by disaster rather than instant obliteration. Earthquake damage, shoreline change and coastal erosion probably disturbed an already modest settlement. Later generations compressed that complicated history into a more memorable story: the lost city of Nevis.

## What really happened at Bloody Point?

The mystery at Bloody Point is not whether mass killing occurred. English and French colonists killed a large number of Kalinago people on Saint Kitts in 1626, near the place now known as Bloody Point or Bloody River. The unresolved question is whether the colonists were preventing an imminent Indigenous attack, as colonial narratives claimed, or whether the supposed plot served as a convenient justification for removing the island’s original inhabitants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicstkitts.kn/places/bloody-point" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicstkitts.kn">[historicstkitts.kn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicstkitts.kn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicstkitts.kn.</span></span></span>

European settlement on Saint Kitts had begun only a few years earlier. English colonists led by Thomas Warner and French settlers associated with Pierre Belain d’Esnambuc occupied different parts of the island. Their plantations required land, and the expanding European presence posed an obvious threat to Kalinago independence.

Later accounts claimed that the Kalinago leader commonly identified as Tegremante or Tegremond planned a coordinated attack on the settlers. According to the colonial story, an Indigenous woman warned Warner and d’Esnambuc. The Europeans then struck first, killing the leader and others before pursuing a much larger Kalinago force towards Bloody Point. Estimates repeated in later histories often suggest that roughly 2,000 Kalinago died, although exact numbers cannot be established securely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalinago_genocide_of_1626" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kalinago genocide of 1626</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kalinago genocide of 1626</span></span></span>

The central problem is the source base. The surviving narrative was written and preserved by Europeans who benefited directly from the outcome. The people accused of plotting the attack left no comparable written testimony. Historian Melanie Newton has argued that such conquest stories drew upon a wider colonial image of Indigenous Caribbean people as treacherous and inherently dangerous. In this reading, the alleged conspiracy cannot simply be accepted as neutral intelligence; it must be examined as part of the language by which conquest was explained and legitimised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.utoronto.ca/research-publications/faculty-publications/race-leapt-sauteurs-genocide-narrative-and-indigenous" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.utoronto.ca">[utoronto.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on utoronto.ca.</span></span></span>

A Kalinago attack was not inherently impossible. Indigenous communities across the Lesser Antilles resisted European settlement, and the rapid seizure of land gave them every reason to fear the newcomers. Yet possibility is not proof. The colonists’ claim of an imminent massacre may have reflected genuine alarm, exaggerated rumours or a deliberately constructed pretext. The surviving documents do not allow historians to distinguish confidently between those alternatives.

What is much clearer is the result. European forces broke organised Kalinago power on Saint Kitts, and survivors were killed, enslaved, displaced or driven towards other islands. The massacre opened more land to colonial settlement and helped Saint Kitts become a base for further English and French expansion in the eastern Caribbean. The event is therefore not merely an unsolved military episode; it marks a decisive transfer of land and power.

The landscape itself complicates the memory. Bloody River contains Indigenous rock carvings, or petroglyphs, demonstrating that the place formed part of a much older human landscape before it became associated with massacre. More than a century ago, observers recorded numerous carvings there, while recent archaeological fieldwork has treated Bloody Point, Old Road and the petroglyphs as a connected Indigenous and colonial landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.universiteitleiden.nl/binaries/content/assets/customsites/nexus1492/field/fieldworkreport_stkitts2014_2015.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: universiteitleiden.nl">[Universiteit Leiden]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">universiteitleiden.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fieldworkreport stkitts2014 2015</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fieldworkreport stkitts2014 2015</span></span></span>

The responsible conclusion is that the massacre is documented but the colonists’ justification remains unverified. Statements that the Kalinago were unquestionably preparing to kill every European go further than the surviving evidence permits. Equally, claiming that no resistance was planned would also exceed the record. The mystery survives because the archive preserves the fears and explanations of the victors far more clearly than the intentions of those they attacked.

## Was Red Legs Greaves a real pirate?

“Red Legs” Greaves is said to have been a Barbados-born pirate who captured valuable prizes, behaved more honourably than his rivals and eventually retired to Nevis. In the most spectacular version of the tale, he was recognised, arrested and sentenced to death, only for an earthquake and tidal wave to destroy the town where he was imprisoned. He escaped, became a pirate hunter and ended his life as a respectable philanthropist.

Almost every element is perfectly shaped for adventure fiction. Greaves rises from an abused childhood, overthrows a cruel captain, adopts a humane pirate code, captures Spanish treasure, survives a natural disaster and wins redemption. The problem is that researchers have not found him in the expected seventeenth-century records. There is no convincing contemporary trail through pirate trials, Spanish accounts, colonial correspondence or records of his supposed attack on Margarita Island.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

The story appears in print only much later. Its earliest known substantial version is associated with A. Hyatt Verrill’s 1923 writing about buccaneers. Philip Gosse repeated the story in his 1924 *Pirates’ Who’s Who*, giving it wider circulation. Neither supplied the kind of primary documentation that would establish Greaves as a historical person.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

The earthquake escape also shifts location according to the teller. Some versions place Greaves at Port Royal in Jamaica during the catastrophic earthquake of 1692. Others transfer him to Nevis and connect him to the supposed destruction of Jamestown in 1680 or 1690. This flexibility is a warning sign. Historical events normally become clearer when independent evidence accumulates; folklore often becomes more adaptable.

Greaves may have originated in oral tradition, or his story may combine several real pirates and disasters. It is also possible that twentieth-century writers created most of the biography. Until a contemporary record appears, the safest assessment is that Red Legs Greaves is an unverified or fictional pirate attached to a genuine Caribbean world of privateering, forced labour, earthquakes and colonial conflict.

His connection with Nevis nevertheless matters. Once Jamestown became known as a town swallowed by the sea, it provided an ideal setting for a miraculous prison escape. The pirate legend and the lost-town legend strengthened one another: the disaster made Greaves’s escape seem plausible, while his escape made the disaster seem more vivid.

## Why do the mysteries remain so powerful?

The leading mysteries of Saint Kitts and Nevis survive for different reasons, but each exposes a problem in how Caribbean history was recorded.

**Natural disasters leave uneven evidence.** Earthquakes, hurricanes and coastal erosion can destroy both settlements and their records. Later observers then try to reconstruct a sudden event from scattered letters, altered shorelines and buried structures. Jamestown shows how easily a real disaster can become a total-destruction legend.

**Colonial archives are not neutral.** The written record of Bloody Point was produced mainly by Europeans engaged in conquest. It preserves their claims, fears and justifications while leaving Kalinago motives largely inaccessible. The absence of an Indigenous written account is not evidence that Indigenous perspectives did not exist; it is evidence of unequal archival survival.

**Place names make stories feel tangible.** Names such as Bloody Point and Bloody River appear to preserve direct testimony. They do mark a genuine history of violence, but dramatic embellishments — such as claims that the river ran red for days — are far harder to verify. A powerful name can keep an event alive while also encouraging later additions.

**Popular retellings reward clean endings.** A town that declined through erosion, economic change and earthquake damage is less memorable than a city swallowed in an afternoon. A colonial massacre rooted in land seizure and fear is harder to narrate than a simple story of a foiled plot. An undocumented pirate is more appealing when given a complete moral journey from victim to outlaw to hero.

These cases should not be treated as interchangeable folklore. Jamestown is a genuine archaeological and documentary problem whose sensational version has been weakened by research. Bloody Point is a documented atrocity surrounded by uncertainty about motive, warning and justification. Red Legs Greaves is a colourful tale for which contemporary evidence is presently absent.

## What the best evidence supports

The most defensible reading of Saint Kitts and Nevis’s enigmatic past is cautious but not dismissive.

Jamestown was not invented. Archaeologists have identified the settlement and reconstructed parts of its commercial and defensive landscape. A major earthquake struck in 1690, and disturbed sea conditions may have caused local damage. The claim that a large capital vanished completely beneath the sea, however, is not supported by the strongest archaeological and archival work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/1BDAC4CABE0ABC290D8A17AADCC7F686/9781787446021c9_p177-196_CBO.pdf/jamestown-nevis-fact-fiction-and-fable.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resolve.cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resolve.cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment9 Jamestown, Nevis: Fact, Fiction, and Fable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment9 Jamestown, Nevis: Fact, Fiction, and FableSUMMARY: The destruction of Jamestown, Nevis, in 1690...</span></span></span>

The Kalinago massacre at Bloody Point was also real. What remains uncertain is the reliability of the European account of an imminent Indigenous conspiracy. The killing’s political consequence — clearing resistance to colonial occupation — is more securely established than the story used to defend it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicstkitts.kn/places/bloody-point" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicstkitts.kn">[historicstkitts.kn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicstkitts.kn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicstkitts.kn.</span></span></span>

Red Legs Greaves is the weakest historical claim of the three. His adventures appear in sources written centuries after the period in which he supposedly lived, and the absence of contemporary corroboration makes it more likely that he is a literary or folkloric creation than a forgotten pirate captain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery is therefore not simply whether a city sank, a conspiracy existed or a pirate escaped. It is how uncertain fragments became settled public stories. In Saint Kitts and Nevis, archaeology and critical historical research have not stripped the past of mystery. They have revealed a more interesting one: the long process by which disaster, conquest and imagination reshape what a country remembers.
