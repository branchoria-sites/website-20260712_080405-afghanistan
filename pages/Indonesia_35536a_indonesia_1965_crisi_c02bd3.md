## Introduction

The events that unfolded in [Indonesia]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-indonesias/' | relative_url }}) between the night of 30 September 1965 and the transfer of power to Major-General Suharto remain among the country's most consequential and contested historical episodes. The broad outline is well established: a small group of military officers abducted and killed six senior army generals, Suharto rapidly regained control of Jakarta, the Indonesian Communist Party (PKI) was blamed, and the crisis became the justification for mass repression and a fundamental shift in political power. Yet the central historical mystery has never been fully resolved. Historians still disagree over who directed the 30 September Movement, how much PKI leaders knew, whether factions within the army manipulated events, and under what circumstances President Sukarno lost effective authority. The debate matters because the answers shaped decades of official history and legitimised one of the twentieth century's largest campaigns of political violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/0185ffd8-047a-4e2f-aee2-a865730b12d6/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecommons.cornell.edu">[Cornell eCommons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecommons.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornell eCommonsJohn Roosa. ## What happened on 30 September 1965?

Late on 30 September and during the early hours of 1 October 1965, soldiers led by Lieutenant Colonel Untung, a commander within the presidential guard, abducted six senior army generals from their homes in Jakarta. The officers were killed and their bodies later recovered from a well at Lubang Buaya. A seventh intended target, General Abdul Haris Nasution, escaped, although his young daughter and an aide were killed during the attack. The group announced itself over state radio as the "30 September Movement", claiming that it had acted to prevent an alleged "Council of Generals" from overthrowing President Sukarno.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d149" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

The movement collapsed with remarkable speed. Major-General Suharto, commander of the Army Strategic Reserve (KOSTRAD), assumed operational control after the disappearance of Army Commander Ahmad Yani. By the evening of 1 October, army units loyal to Suharto had retaken key installations in Jakarta, and within days the movement had effectively been defeated. Even contemporary intelligence assessments acknowledged that many essential facts remained unclear, particularly Sukarno's role, the extent of PKI involvement and the movement's planning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d149" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

The speed of the movement's failure is itself one reason the episode remains so debated. It never demonstrated the coordinated command structure expected of a carefully organised nationwide coup. Instead, it appeared fragmented, with inconsistent public announcements, uncertain military support outside Jakarta and no coherent plan for governing after the generals had been removed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/0185ffd8-047a-4e2f-aee2-a865730b12d6/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecommons.cornell.edu">[Cornell eCommons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecommons.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornell eCommonsJohn Roosa. ## Competing accounts of the movement's leadership

The greatest unresolved question is simple to ask but difficult to answer: who actually directed the 30 September Movement?

### The New Order's official explanation

Under Suharto's New Order government, the official narrative presented the movement as a centrally organised PKI conspiracy intended to seize state power. This interpretation became compulsory in schools, museums and official commemorations, portraying the army as having rescued Indonesia from a Communist revolution.

Many historians now regard that version as too simple. Investigations after Suharto's fall found that several dramatic elements of the official account, including sensational stories about the torture and mutilation of the murdered generals, were unsupported by medical evidence and appear to have been part of wartime propaganda intended to mobilise public opinion against the PKI.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/0185ffd8-047a-4e2f-aee2-a865730b12d6/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecommons.cornell.edu">[Cornell eCommons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecommons.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornell eCommonsJohn Roosa. ### A limited conspiracy rather than a party coup?

One influential interpretation, developed most fully by historian John Roosa, argues that the movement emerged from cooperation between a small circle of PKI leaders and sympathetic military officers rather than from the Communist Party as a whole. In this view, the conspiracy centred on a handful of individuals who expected limited military support but failed to secure broader backing within either the army or the party.

This explanation attempts to account for the movement's confused planning, contradictory announcements and rapid collapse. It rejects both the New Order claim of a disciplined nationwide Communist coup and theories that portray the PKI leadership as entirely uninvolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/0185ffd8-047a-4e2f-aee2-a865730b12d6/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecommons.cornell.edu">[Cornell eCommons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecommons.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornell eCommonsJohn Roosa. ### Was it primarily an internal army struggle?

Another line of interpretation gives greater weight to divisions within the Indonesian Army itself. According to this perspective, rival military networks, personal loyalties and disputes over Sukarno's future created conditions in which a relatively small group of officers acted independently or in loose coordination with civilians.

Evidence supporting this view includes the limited participation of army units, conflicting testimony from surviving participants and the movement's inability to establish effective command beyond a handful of locations. It does not necessarily exclude some PKI involvement but suggests that military politics played a much larger role than the official narrative admitted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/0185ffd8-047a-4e2f-aee2-a865730b12d6/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecommons.cornell.edu">[Cornell eCommons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecommons.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornell eCommonsJohn Roosa. ### Did Suharto know beforehand?

Perhaps the most controversial theory is that Suharto possessed prior knowledge of the operation or allowed events to develop in order to strengthen his own position.

No convincing documentary evidence has demonstrated that Suharto organised the movement. However, historians continue to examine intriguing circumstances, including his personal relationships with several participants and the extraordinary speed with which he assumed command. These observations have generated debate but have not produced scholarly consensus. Most professional historians distinguish between evidence that Suharto skilfully exploited the crisis—which is well supported—and claims that he secretly masterminded it, which remain unproven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/0185ffd8-047a-4e2f-aee2-a865730b12d6/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecommons.cornell.edu">[Cornell eCommons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecommons.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornell eCommonsJohn Roosa. ## The missing order that strengthened Suharto

The second enduring mystery concerns the legal mechanism through which Suharto converted military authority into political power.

On 11 March 1966, President Sukarno reportedly signed an instruction authorising Suharto to restore security and protect the government. Suharto immediately used this authority to ban the PKI, arrest ministers considered loyal to Sukarno and steadily dismantle the president's remaining influence.

The difficulty for historians is that the original document has never been found. Only differing copies survive, with variations in wording and presentation. As a result, scholars cannot establish with certainty precisely what Sukarno authorised, whether the order granted temporary emergency powers or much broader authority, or whether it reflected Sukarno's genuine intentions. The disappearance of the original document leaves one of the decisive moments in modern Indonesian history dependent on imperfect archival evidence.

Questions also remain about the circumstances under which Sukarno signed the order. Some accounts describe him acting voluntarily amid political chaos, while others suggest he faced heavy military pressure. Because eyewitness testimony conflicts and the original signed document is missing, historians cannot resolve these competing accounts conclusively.

## Why uncertainty still surrounds the crisis

Several factors explain why historians continue to debate the events despite decades of research.

First, many key participants died, were executed or spent years in prison before giving testimony. Their surviving accounts often contradict one another.

Second, official investigations during the New Order were conducted within a political system that discouraged challenges to the government's version of events. Documents remained inaccessible, while alternative interpretations could carry serious personal risk.

Third, intelligence records from Indonesia and foreign governments demonstrate that outside observers themselves struggled to understand what had happened in real time. Contemporary diplomatic reporting repeatedly acknowledged major uncertainties concerning Sukarno's knowledge, the movement's leadership and the extent of Communist participation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d149" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

Finally, the political consequences of the crisis were so enormous that later participants had powerful incentives to defend or reshape their own roles. Historical reconstruction therefore depends on comparing incomplete archives, contested memoirs and fragmentary military records rather than on a single definitive source.

## The historical picture around 1965 Crisis
The unresolved questions surrounding the 1965 crisis are inseparable from Indonesia's modern political history. The official explanation of the 30 September Movement became the ideological foundation of Suharto's New Order, justifying the destruction of the PKI, widespread imprisonment without trial and mass killings during 1965–66 that claimed hundreds of thousands of lives according to most scholarly estimates. Because the legitimacy of the new regime rested heavily on its interpretation of the crisis, disagreement over who organised the movement remains more than an academic debate.

Since Suharto's fall in 1998, historians have gained greater freedom to examine previously neglected evidence and challenge official narratives. Even so, the central mystery has not [disappeared]({{ 'disappeared/' | relative_url }}). The surviving evidence supports neither a simple Communist coup nor a proven conspiracy orchestrated by Suharto.
