## Introduction

The central mystery of the Plain of Jars is no longer whether the giant stone vessels were connected with the dead. Decades of excavation have shown that they formed part of an extensive funerary landscape. The unanswered questions are more specific and, in many ways, more intriguing: who built this remarkable complex, exactly how were the jars used in burial rituals, and why did later communities continue to bury their dead around – and sometimes inside – monuments that were already centuries old? These questions matter because the Plain of Jars preserves the largest known megalithic funerary landscape in mainland Southeast Asia, yet the people who created it left no written records and cannot be identified with confidence. Archaeological discoveries continue to refine the picture, but they have not removed the mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Megalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMegalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2019</span></span></span>

## What excavations have revealed

The strongest evidence comes not from the jars themselves but from the ground around them. Since the pioneering excavations of French archaeologist Madeleine Colani in the 1930s, researchers have uncovered cremated bone fragments, human teeth, pottery, iron tools, glass beads, ceramic burial vessels and carefully prepared burial pits surrounding many of the jars. These discoveries overturned earlier ideas that the vessels were simply for storing food, water or rice wine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41826-023-00067-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secondary burial practice at megalithic jar site 1, Plain of Jars Laos &#124; Asian Archaeology &#124; Springer Nature LinkApril 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 3, 2023</span></span></span>

Modern excavations have greatly expanded that picture. Work by Lao and international teams has identified several forms of burial at the same sites, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Secondary burials, in which bones were collected after decomposition and reburied.
* Ceramic jar burials using much smaller earthenware containers.
* Burial pits marked by stone slabs.
* Occasional cremated remains deposited inside the giant stone jars themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41826-023-00067-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secondary burial practice at megalithic jar site 1, Plain of Jars Laos &#124; Asian Archaeology &#124; Springer Nature LinkApril 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 3, 2023</span></span></span>

</div>

The variety of burial methods suggests that the Plain of Jars was not used for a single ritual performed in a single era. Instead, it appears to have been a ceremonial landscape whose mortuary practices evolved over many centuries.

Excavations have also identified nearby quarries where many jars were carved before being transported, sometimes over considerable distances, to hilltops and ridges. The engineering required to shape and move sandstone blocks weighing several tonnes indicates an organised society with specialised knowledge, even though its political structure remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Megalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMegalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2019</span></span></span>

## How the jars may have been used

Although archaeologists broadly agree that the jars belonged to a funerary tradition, the exact sequence of burial remains debated because different sites appear to preserve different practices.

One influential interpretation proposes that bodies were initially placed inside the large stone jars until soft tissue decomposed. The cleaned bones would then have been transferred into nearby burial pits or ceramic containers in a secondary burial ceremony. This would explain why complete skeletons are generally absent from the jars while burial deposits surround them. The idea also fits secondary burial traditions known elsewhere in Southeast Asia, although direct proof has long been limited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41826-023-00067-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secondary burial practice at megalithic jar site 1, Plain of Jars Laos &#124; Asian Archaeology &#124; Springer Nature LinkApril 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 3, 2023</span></span></span>

Another possibility is that many jars served mainly as ritual monuments rather than containers. Under this view, the jars marked important burial grounds while the dead were interred beside them instead of inside them. Archaeological evidence showing numerous surrounding graves but relatively little material inside many jars supports this interpretation at several sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41826-023-00067-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secondary burial practice at megalithic jar site 1, Plain of Jars Laos &#124; Asian Archaeology &#124; Springer Nature LinkApril 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 3, 2023</span></span></span>

A third possibility is increasingly accepted: there may never have been one universal practice. Different communities, or the same communities across different centuries, may have used the jars in different ways.

## The discovery that changed the debate

A particularly important discovery came from Site 75, where archaeologists excavated an unusually large jar over several field seasons between 2022 and 2024. Unlike most previously studied examples, this vessel still contained substantial undisturbed deposits.

Researchers identified the disarticulated remains of at least 37 people inside the jar together with beads, pottery, iron artefacts and a bronze bell. Radiocarbon dating showed that these remains accumulated over roughly AD 890 to 1160, indicating that the jar functioned as a collective mortuary container over many generations rather than during a single burial event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/death-jar-a-new-mortuary-tradition-at-the-plain-of-jars-lao-pdr/D0A49363A484C2C891205FF638A5B5E6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe death jar: a new mortuary tradition at the Plain of Jars, Lao PDR &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Core...</span></span></span>

The arrangement of the bones is especially revealing. Long bones clustered around the edges, while many delicate bones were absent, suggesting that bodies had decomposed elsewhere before selected remains were placed into the jar. This strongly supports the idea of secondary burial for at least this particular monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/death-jar-a-new-mortuary-tradition-at-the-plain-of-jars-lao-pdr/D0A49363A484C2C891205FF638A5B5E6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe death jar: a new mortuary tradition at the Plain of Jars, Lao PDR &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Core...</span></span></span>

At the same time, the researchers stressed an important limitation. Site 75 represents one jar at one location. It cannot automatically explain every jar across the vast Plain of Jars landscape, where more than 2,100 vessels are distributed across numerous archaeological sites with differing layouts and histories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/death-jar-a-new-mortuary-tradition-at-the-plain-of-jars-lao-pdr/D0A49363A484C2C891205FF638A5B5E6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe death jar: a new mortuary tradition at the Plain of Jars, Lao PDR &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Core...</span></span></span>

## Why the builders remain unidentified

The greatest mystery is not the burial ritual itself but the identity of the people who created it.

UNESCO dates the principal jar-building tradition to roughly 500 BC to AD 500, although some activity may have continued somewhat later. Yet no historical texts identify the builders, and no surviving inscriptions have been found on the jars themselves. Archaeologists therefore rely almost entirely on material evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Megalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMegalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2019</span></span></span>

Several factors make identification difficult:

* The culture left no known written records.
* Organic materials that might preserve DNA or other evidence survive poorly in the tropical environment.
* Many sites suffered damage during twentieth-century conflict, including bombing during the Indochina wars.
* Not every site has been excavated, and unexploded ordnance still limits archaeological work in some areas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Megalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMegalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2019</span></span></span>

Researchers believe the jar builders belonged to an Iron Age society linked to regional trade networks connecting mainland Southeast Asia. Grave goods such as glass beads, iron tools and ornaments indicate contact beyond the immediate region, but these objects have not allowed archaeologists to assign the builders to a specific historically known kingdom or ethnic group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Megalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMegalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2019</span></span></span>

## Why later communities returned to the jars

One of the most surprising findings from recent research is that many burials post-date the construction of the jars themselves.

At Site 1, excavations revealed secondary burials dating from roughly the eighth to thirteenth centuries AD, centuries after many researchers believe the jars were originally erected. Site 75 produced a similar medieval chronology for the remains placed inside its large jar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41826-023-00067-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secondary burial practice at megalithic jar site 1, Plain of Jars Laos &#124; Asian Archaeology &#124; Springer Nature LinkApril 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 3, 2023</span></span></span>

This suggests that later populations treated the ancient monuments as meaningful places long after the original builders had disappeared. Whether these later people understood who created the jars is unknown. They may have inherited traditions explaining their significance, or they may simply have recognised them as ancient sacred landmarks suitable for continued mortuary use.

This long sequence of reuse complicates every archaeological interpretation. Artefacts and human remains found together do not necessarily belong to the same cultural phase, making it difficult to reconstruct the earliest funerary practices with certainty.

## What remains uncertain

Despite nearly a century of investigation, several central questions remain unresolved.

**Who built the jars?** No securely identified culture, kingdom or ethnic group can yet be linked to their construction.

**Was there a single burial ritual?** Evidence increasingly suggests there was not. Mortuary practices probably varied between regions and changed over time.

**Were all jars intended to hold bodies?** Recent discoveries show that at least some certainly did, but many others may have served symbolic or ceremonial roles instead.

**Why were these enormous monuments built?** Their size implies considerable labour and social organisation, but archaeologists still debate whether they reflected elite status, ancestor worship, territorial identity or a combination of functions.

## Why the mystery still matters

The Plain of Jars demonstrates how archaeology can narrow a mystery without completely solving it. The old question—whether the jars were connected with burial—has largely been answered. The evidence overwhelmingly indicates that they formed part of an elaborate mortuary landscape. Yet the more detailed questions have become even richer.

Each excavation reveals a society capable of quarrying, transporting and positioning immense stone vessels across mountain ridges, practising sophisticated funerary rituals and maintaining sacred places over centuries. At the same time, every new discovery highlights how much remains unknown about the people themselves. Their names, language, political organisation and beliefs have all vanished, leaving only the monumental jars and the careful treatment of the dead as enduring clues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Megalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMegalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang – Plain of Jars - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 6, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2019</span></span></span>
