## Introduction

Did anyone reach [Cape Verde]({{ 'what-secrets-still-haunt-cape-verdes/' | relative_url }}) before the Portuguese? The short answer is that nobody knows for certain—but there is currently no accepted evidence that anyone settled the islands before the mid-fifteenth century. Portuguese navigators reported finding an uninhabited archipelago around 1460, and permanent settlement began soon afterwards on Santiago. Modern archaeology has uncovered rich evidence of the earliest colonial communities, yet it has not produced a securely dated pre-Portuguese settlement, burial, or artefact assemblage. That gap between possibility and proof is what makes the question one of Cape Verde's most enduring historical debates. While occasional earlier landfalls by African, Mediterranean or other sailors cannot be ruled out, the evidence available today supports the view that the islands were probably uninhabited when Portuguese colonisation began.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1310/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## What the Portuguese records actually claim

The earliest Portuguese accounts describe the Cape Verde islands as newly encountered and without an established human population. Different navigators disputed who deserved credit for the "discovery" of particular islands, but they broadly agreed on one important point: they did not report finding villages, cultivated land, organised political communities or existing ports.

This distinction matters because fifteenth-century European use of the word "discovery" has often been misunderstood. In Portuguese royal and navigational records, discovering a place generally meant bringing it into the knowledge and authority of the Portuguese Crown. It did not necessarily prove that no human had ever seen or briefly landed on the islands before.

Within only a few years of the first voyages, Portuguese settlers founded Ribeira Grande (now Cidade Velha) on Santiago. UNESCO identifies it as the first European colonial town built in the tropics and the starting point of a new Atlantic society that combined European settlers with enslaved and free Africans. The documentary record begins at this moment of colonisation rather than preserving any independent evidence of an earlier resident population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1310/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## Why earlier visitors remain possible

Although permanent occupation before the Portuguese has not been demonstrated, historians hesitate to claim that nobody ever reached the islands.

Cape Verde lies roughly 600 kilometres west of the African mainland. That is a considerable distance, but not an impossible one for experienced sailors or vessels driven offshore by storms. Several possibilities are regularly discussed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **West African fishermen or traders** may have reached one or more islands accidentally or on exploratory voyages.
* **Storm-driven vessels** from the nearby African coast could have landed temporarily without establishing settlements.
* **Ancient or medieval mariners** familiar with Atlantic navigation may have sighted islands without leaving archaeological traces.

</div>

These possibilities are plausible because temporary visits often leave remarkably little evidence. A small landing party spending only a few days ashore might leave nothing that survives centuries of erosion, volcanic activity and later colonial occupation.

The crucial point is that possibility is not proof. No accepted archaeological site currently demonstrates repeated human use of the islands before the Portuguese arrival. The absence of evidence does not prove earlier visits never occurred, but it does prevent historians from treating them as established fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1310/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## Archaeological evidence and its limits

The strongest evidence in this debate comes from archaeology rather than from later traditions or speculation.

Excavations on Santiago and particularly around the earliest colonial settlements have revealed extensive remains dating from the first decades after Portuguese settlement. These include imported European goods, locally produced pottery, animal remains and evidence of West African cultural practices introduced during the formation of the colony.

Importantly, these discoveries show that African people were present from the very beginning of colonial Cape Verde. They do **not** demonstrate that independent African communities had lived on the islands before Portuguese colonisation.

Archaeologists also face practical challenges:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Early colonial construction may have disturbed older deposits if they existed.
* Coastal erosion can destroy archaeological sites near landing places.
* Temporary camps without stone buildings or cemeteries are inherently difficult to detect.
* Many islands have seen relatively limited archaeological investigation compared with longer-inhabited Atlantic regions.

</div>

These limitations explain why scholars remain cautious. The current archaeological record is incomplete, but it is still the best available evidence—and at present it supports first permanent settlement in the Portuguese period rather than earlier occupation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1310/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## Why theories about ancient settlers remain unconvincing

From time to time, claims appear suggesting that Phoenicians, Carthaginians, Arabs or forgotten African kingdoms established colonies in Cape Verde long before the fifteenth century.

These ideas usually rely on indirect arguments rather than archaeological proof. Typical examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* references to ancient geographical texts that may describe Atlantic islands without identifying Cape Verde specifically;
* assumptions that capable ancient sailors must have reached the archipelago;
* isolated artefacts lacking secure archaeological context;
* speculative interpretations repeated in popular books or online discussions.

</div>

Professional historians distinguish carefully between navigation capability and demonstrated settlement. It is entirely possible that ancient mariners possessed the technical ability to reach the islands. That alone does not establish that they actually did so, still less that they founded enduring communities.

No securely excavated pre-1460 settlement, cemetery or cultural layer has gained acceptance within mainstream scholarship. Until such evidence appears, theories of forgotten colonies remain speculative rather than evidentially supported.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1310/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## What would prove an earlier human presence?

The debate could change dramatically if convincing new evidence were discovered. Archaeologists would be looking for finds that satisfy several conditions simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* artefacts recovered from an undisturbed archaeological layer;
* reliable scientific dating to before approximately 1460;
* evidence clearly attributable to human activity rather than natural processes;
* enough material to demonstrate repeated or sustained human presence rather than a single uncertain object.

</div>

Examples might include hearths containing charcoal suitable for radiocarbon dating, tools found in secure context, food remains showing human processing, or burials predating Portuguese settlement.

A single loose artefact discovered on the surface would rarely be enough. Archaeology depends not only on what is found but also on exactly where and how it is found.

## Why the mystery still matters

The question of who first reached Cape Verde is about more than assigning priority to one group of navigators.

If convincing evidence of pre-Portuguese visitors were ever discovered, it would reshape understanding of Atlantic navigation before European expansion and illuminate maritime connections between West Africa and the open Atlantic. It would also change the story of one of the Atlantic world's most influential colonial crossroads.

For now, however, the evidence points in a different direction. Portuguese settlement appears to represent the beginning of permanent human occupation, while the possibility of earlier fleeting visitors remains an open but unproven historical question. That combination of documentary certainty, archaeological silence and geographical plausibility explains why the first settlement debate continues to occupy historians without overturning the conventional account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1310/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira Grande - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>
