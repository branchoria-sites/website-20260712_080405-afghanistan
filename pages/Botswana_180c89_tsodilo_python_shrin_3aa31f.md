## Introduction

The debate over the so-called Tsodilo Python Shrine centres on one of the most striking claims in African archaeology: that a cave in [Botswana]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-botswanas/' | relative_url }})'s Tsodilo Hills preserves evidence of organised ritual dating back around 70,000 years, making it the oldest known ritual site in the world. The claim attracted worldwide attention in 2006 because it appeared to push the origins of symbolic religious behaviour back by tens of thousands of years. Yet it has remained controversial ever since. The mystery is not whether Tsodilo Hills is an exceptional archaeological landscape—it unquestionably is—but whether one particular cave, often called Rhino Cave or the Python Cave, really contains convincing evidence of a deliberate shrine rather than an imaginative interpretation of ambiguous archaeological remains. The debate illustrates how archaeology often depends not only on discoveries, but also on how those discoveries are interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/offerings-to-a-stone-snak/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scientificamerican.com">[Scientific American]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scientificamerican.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientific AmericanOfferings to a Stone Snake Provide the Earliest Evidence of Religion &#124; Scientific AmericanDecember 1, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2006</span></span></span>

## Why the rock was called a python

The disputed feature lies within Rhino Cave, part of the Tsodilo Hills, a UNESCO World Heritage landscape famous for thousands of rock paintings and evidence of human occupation stretching back many tens of thousands of years. The cave contains a large natural rock formation whose outline resembles the head of a python when viewed from a particular angle. Archaeologist Sheila Coulson and her colleagues argued that hundreds of deliberately made depressions, known as cupules, had been carved into its surface to imitate snake scales. When illuminated by flickering firelight, they suggested, the rock appears almost alive, reinforcing the impression of a giant serpent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/offerings-to-a-stone-snak/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scientificamerican.com">[Scientific American]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scientificamerican.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientific AmericanOfferings to a Stone Snake Provide the Earliest Evidence of Religion &#124; Scientific AmericanDecember 1, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2006</span></span></span>

The interpretation went further than the rock itself. Excavations recovered carefully worked stone spear points, many made from colourful raw materials transported from considerable distances. Several appeared to have been deliberately heated or burned. The researchers proposed that these objects had been brought into the cave as offerings and intentionally destroyed during ceremonies rather than being discarded after everyday use. They also highlighted a hidden recess behind the python-shaped rock where a person could remain unseen while speaking, creating the impression that the stone serpent itself had a voice. Together, these features were presented as evidence for a ritual shrine rather than an ordinary habitation site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261595980_Ritualized_Behavior_in_the_Middle_Stone_Age_Evidence_from_Rhino_Cave_Tsodilo_Hills_Botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Coulson also argued that the interpretation gained support from the long-standing symbolic importance of pythons in the beliefs of San communities associated with the region. Although modern traditions cannot simply be projected back 70,000 years, the persistence of snake symbolism suggested to her that the cave might represent a very ancient expression of beliefs that survived in altered forms into recent history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/offerings-to-a-stone-snak/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scientificamerican.com">[Scientific American]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scientificamerican.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientific AmericanOfferings to a Stone Snake Provide the Earliest Evidence of Religion &#124; Scientific AmericanDecember 1, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2006</span></span></span>

## What the archaeology actually shows

Some parts of the evidence are widely accepted. Archaeologists agree that Tsodilo Hills contains an extraordinary archaeological record extending deep into the Middle Stone Age, that Rhino Cave preserves Middle Stone Age artefacts, and that the site was repeatedly visited over extremely long periods rather than during a single episode. The cave is unquestionably important within Botswana's prehistoric record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://botswanatourism.co.bw/explore/tsodilo-hills" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: botswanatourism.co.bw">[Botswana Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">botswanatourism.co.bw</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Botswana Tourism Tsodilo Hills &#124; Botswana Tourism Organisation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Botswana Tourism Tsodilo Hills &#124; Botswana Tourism Organisation</span></span></span>

The disagreement begins when interpreting the specific features inside the cave.

Supporters of the shrine hypothesis point to several unusual observations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a rock that strongly resembles a python's head;
* numerous cupules that appear intentionally made;
* concentrations of finely crafted stone points rather than everyday domestic debris;
* evidence that some points were burned;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303960833_Late_Quaternary_archaeological_and_palaeoenvironmental_data_from_sediments_at_Rhino_Cave_Tsodilo_Hills_Botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* use of colourful stone imported from distant sources;
* the hidden chamber behind the rock that could have enhanced ceremonial performances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261595980_Ritualized_Behavior_in_the_Middle_Stone_Age_Evidence_from_Rhino_Cave_Tsodilo_Hills_Botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

Critics do not necessarily dispute the existence of these individual features. Instead, they question whether they truly form a single coherent pattern pointing to ritual behaviour.

Several archaeologists involved in earlier investigations argued that many cupules differ greatly in preservation. Some are heavily weathered while others appear comparatively fresh, suggesting they were created over very different periods rather than during one ancient ritual tradition. Similar cupules also occur elsewhere around Tsodilo without forming obvious animal figures. From this perspective, the "python" may owe as much to modern pattern recognition as to prehistoric design.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsodilo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The cave's paintings have also become part of the debate. Coulson's interpretation relied partly on the apparent prominence of certain animals. Critics responded that the imagery had been selectively described. They argued that additional geometric paintings had been overlooked and that the famous animal figures belong to different periods, making it difficult to treat them as parts of one unified symbolic programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsodilo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the dating remains contested

The most controversial aspect of the entire debate is not the existence of the cave or its archaeological importance, but the proposed age of the alleged shrine.

The headline figure of roughly 70,000 years refers primarily to Middle Stone Age deposits associated with stone artefacts recovered during excavation. Critics argue that this does not automatically establish that the carved cupules, the python-like appearance of the rock and the proposed ritual activity all belong to the same period. Establishing that different features originated simultaneously is considerably more difficult than dating sediments or stone tools alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsodilo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Questions have also been raised about whether the available dating methods securely link every part of the shrine interpretation. Some archaeologists have argued that the site's radiometric evidence does not conclusively support the specific 70,000-year date for the supposed ritual features, and that some elements may be substantially younger. In archaeology, association is crucial: objects found in the same cave are not necessarily contemporary unless their relationship can be demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsodilo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

As a result, many researchers distinguish between two separate propositions:

* **The cave contains Middle Stone Age archaeological remains.** This is broadly accepted.
* **Those remains demonstrate organised ritual focused on a python shrine around 70,000 years ago.** This remains disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261595980_Ritualized_Behavior_in_the_Middle_Stone_Age_Evidence_from_Rhino_Cave_Tsodilo_Hills_Botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Why the debate still matters

The Tsodilo Python Shrine debate matters because it concerns one of the largest questions in human history: when people began thinking symbolically and performing shared rituals.

If the strongest interpretation is correct, then complex ceremonial behaviour developed in southern Africa far earlier than previously recognised, reinforcing the idea that symbolic culture emerged deep within African prehistory rather than appearing suddenly in later Ice Age Europe. The cave would represent one of humanity's earliest known sacred places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/offerings-to-a-stone-snak/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scientificamerican.com">[Scientific American]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scientificamerican.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientific AmericanOfferings to a Stone Snake Provide the Earliest Evidence of Religion &#124; Scientific AmericanDecember 1, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2006</span></span></span>

If the critics are right, the site remains immensely significant but tells a different story. Rather than revealing the world's oldest shrine, it demonstrates how difficult it is to distinguish ritual from ordinary behaviour in the deep archaeological past. Natural rock shapes, repeated visits over thousands of years and later modifications can easily produce patterns that appear meaningful without necessarily recording a single prehistoric religious tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsodilo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Either way, Tsodilo Hills continues to hold exceptional importance. The landscape has long been regarded as sacred by local communities and preserves one of Africa's richest concentrations of rock art alongside archaeological evidence spanning many millennia. The enduring mystery is therefore not whether Tsodilo was a special place—it clearly was—but whether Rhino Cave truly preserves humanity's oldest identifiable shrine or an intriguing coincidence of geology, archaeology and modern interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://botswanatourism.co.bw/explore/tsodilo-hills" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: botswanatourism.co.bw">[Botswana Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">botswanatourism.co.bw</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Botswana Tourism Tsodilo Hills &#124; Botswana Tourism Organisation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Botswana Tourism Tsodilo Hills &#124; Botswana Tourism Organisation</span></span></span>
