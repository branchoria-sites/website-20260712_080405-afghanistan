## Introduction

These cases differ greatly. Roye’s fate is a genuine archival puzzle. Newport’s celebrated cannon shot is better understood as a national tradition whose historical basis is doubtful. Liberia’s civil-war mysteries are larger and more painful: families still lack complete answers about victims, perpetrators and burial places. Together, they show how unresolved history can shape political legitimacy, communal fear and public memory long after the original events.

## How did President Edward James Roye die?

Edward James Roye became Liberia’s fifth president in January 1870. A wealthy merchant and former chief justice, he led the True Whig Party and sought foreign finance for a country facing severe economic difficulties. His government arranged a large British loan, but its unfavourable terms and disputed handling contributed to a political crisis. Opponents also accused Roye of attempting to extend his presidency or prevent a scheduled election. He was removed from office on 26 October 1871 and imprisoned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_James_Roye" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Edward James Roye</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Edward James Roye</span></span></span>

The broad sequence is clear; Roye’s final hours are not. Later accounts variously say that he died in custody, was killed after escaping, drowned while trying to reach a British ship in Monrovia harbour, or died after a canoe carrying him overturned. Even the date is disputed, usually given as 11 or 12 February 1872. Indiana University’s Svend Holsoe Collection, which contains manuscripts and research devoted to Roye, describes his death simply as mysterious.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.iu.edu/catalog/VAD2737" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.iu.edu">[iu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.iu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archives OnlineThe Svend Holsoe Collection: Edward James Roye Materials...Roye was deposed in 1871 in the first coup d&#x27;état in Africa&#x27;s...</span></span></span>

The drowning story is widely repeated, but repetition is not proof. No securely established contemporary record available in the main published accounts settles whether Roye escaped, whether British protection had been arranged, who accompanied him or whether a body was formally identified. Accounts were produced in a deeply partisan setting: Roye’s supporters regarded his removal as an unlawful coup, while his opponents portrayed it as a defence of constitutional government. That political division makes later narratives difficult to separate from efforts to justify or condemn his overthrow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_James_Roye" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Edward James Roye</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Edward James Roye</span></span></span>

Murder is possible, particularly because Roye had powerful enemies and remained dangerous to the new authorities while alive. Yet there is no firm evidence demonstrating an organised assassination. Accidental drowning is also plausible in the coastal waters off Monrovia, but the precise escape story rests on uncertain testimony. The safest conclusion is therefore limited: Roye was deposed, imprisoned and dead by February 1872, but the surviving evidence does not establish exactly where, how or at whose hands he died.

Roye’s mystery matters because it surrounds Liberia’s first successful removal of a president. Whether he died fleeing justice, escaping political persecution or in the custody of his enemies changes the meaning of the 1871 crisis. The absence of a settled account also illustrates a wider difficulty in Liberian history: many presidential and government records have suffered from poor preservation, political upheaval and civil war. The United States Office of the Historian describes Liberia’s archival system as still recovering from prolonged conflict and notes that important presidential records are dispersed among different institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/countries/archives/liberia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Liberia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianLiberia - Archives - Countries - Office of the HistorianLiberia recovers from years of civil war and political str...</span></span></span>

## Did Matilda Newport really fire the cannon?

For much of the twentieth century, Liberian schoolchildren were taught that Matilda Newport saved the early settlement at Cape Mesurado in 1822. According to the familiar story, indigenous fighters were close to overwhelming the settlers when Newport used a glowing coal from her pipe to fire a cannon. The blast supposedly broke the attack and preserved the colony that later became Monrovia. Liberia commemorated her with a monument, stamps and an annual public holiday established in 1916.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://contestedhistories.org/wp-content/uploads/Liberia_-Matilda-Newport-Monument-Monrovia.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: contestedhistories.org">[Contested Histories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">contestedhistories.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Contested HistoriesLiberia: Matilda Newport Monument, MonroviaThe Matilda Newport Monument in Monrovia, Liberia, commemorates the victory...</span></span></span>

There is good evidence that Newport was a real settler. Records place Matilda and her first husband among the African American emigrants who arrived on the Liberian coast in 1820. There is also no serious doubt that the struggling settlement fought indigenous forces in 1822. The uncertainty concerns Newport’s dramatic role in the battle. Early descriptions of the fighting apparently did not identify a woman firing the decisive cannon; the first known written attribution to Newport appeared decades later, in 1854.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matilda_Newport" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Matilda Newport</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Matilda Newport</span></span></span>

That gap does not prove that the event was invented. Important actions can be omitted from military reports, and oral memories may preserve details absent from official records. Nevertheless, the late appearance of the story, followed by its transformation into a patriotic ceremony, suggests that later Liberians shaped it into a founding legend. Historian Svend Holsoe characterised the tradition as a Liberian invention whose public power increased over time, rather than as a securely documented battlefield episode.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matilda_Newport" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Matilda Newport</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Matilda Newport</span></span></span>

The story also concealed the perspective of the Dei and other indigenous communities resisting the settlement. In older patriotic versions, they appeared merely as attackers defeated by a heroic colonist. Modern interpretations place the battle within a struggle over land, sovereignty and the expansion of a settlement created through the American Colonization Society. The Matilda Newport Monument is consequently treated as a contested memorial: it celebrates settler survival while recalling violence and dispossession for many indigenous Liberians.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://contestedhistories.org/wp-content/uploads/Liberia_-Matilda-Newport-Monument-Monrovia.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: contestedhistories.org">[Contested Histories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">contestedhistories.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Contested HistoriesLiberia: Matilda Newport Monument, MonroviaThe Matilda Newport Monument in Monrovia, Liberia, commemorates the victory...</span></span></span>

President Samuel Doe’s government abolished Matilda Newport Day after the 1980 coup ended more than a century of Americo-Liberian political dominance. The change did not prove or disprove the cannon story; it changed whose historical memory the state officially honoured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matilda_Newport" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Matilda Newport</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Matilda Newport</span></span></span>

The most reasonable assessment separates three claims:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Documented:** Matilda Newport existed, emigrated to the settlement and lived through its precarious early years.
* **Plausible but unverified:** she may have assisted in the defence of Cape Mesurado.
* **Poorly substantiated:** that her single cannon shot decisively saved the settlement exactly as later ceremonies portrayed it.

</div>

The real mystery is therefore not simply whether Newport fired a weapon. It is how an uncertain anecdote became an official national story, and why that story remained politically powerful for so long.

## Were the Harper Seven proven guilty?

The Maryland County killings of the 1970s occupy an uneasy position between solved crime and unresolved historical controversy. Residents around Harper had reported disappearances and mutilated bodies over a period of years. Fear centred on so-called ritual murder: the killing of people and removal of body parts for supposed spiritual or political power. The case that finally forced decisive action was the disappearance of Moses Tweh, a fisherman and popular singer, in June 1977. His mutilated body was found in early July.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

Investigators arrested a group that included senior county officials and politically connected figures. Among them were Maryland County superintendent James Daniel Anderson and legislator Allen Nathaniel Yancy. Several defendants were convicted of involvement in Tweh’s murder, and seven were publicly hanged in Harper on 16 February 1979. Contemporary Liberian news reports and government bulletins recorded the trial, appeals and executions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

The convictions answered the state’s legal question, but they did not eliminate historical doubt. Accounts of the investigation report that suspects were publicly humiliated and allegedly tortured. Some defendants were released and became prosecution witnesses, raising questions about pressure, bargaining and the reliability of testimony. The atmosphere was also highly charged: residents believed that influential people had long enjoyed protection while ordinary victims disappeared without effective investigation. President William Tolbert faced strong public demands to act.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

The involvement of high officials made the prosecution appear, to many residents, like a rare victory against impunity. Yet historians have also interpreted the case in the context of tensions within the ruling True Whig Party. Tolbert was attempting to broaden his political support beyond older elite networks, while some of the accused represented established county power. That does not mean the prosecution was fabricated, but it makes it reasonable to ask whether political calculations affected who was charged, how the investigation proceeded and why the executions became such a public demonstration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

Claims that more than 100 ritual murders occurred in Maryland County between the mid-1960s and 1977 should be treated cautiously. They recur in later summaries, but reliable case-by-case documentation is difficult to locate. “Ritual killing” can also become a catch-all explanation for different disappearances, ordinary homicides and mutilations whose motives were never independently established. The documented core is narrower: Moses Tweh was abducted and murdered; officials and associates were prosecuted; seven people were executed; and serious concerns remain about interrogation methods and the political environment.

Several possible readings therefore coexist:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The verdict was substantially correct.** The defendants participated in Tweh’s killing, and the case exposed a protected network of powerful offenders.
* **The correct suspects were prosecuted through an unreliable process.** Genuine evidence may have existed, but torture, intimidation and public pressure undermined confidence in parts of the case.
* **Political interests shaped the prosecution.** Some accused people may have been guilty, while others were included because their removal served wider struggles within the governing elite.
* **The trial solved only one part of a larger pattern.** Even accepting the verdict, many earlier disappearances and killings were never individually explained.

</div>

The public celebrations after the hangings reflected relief, but they also helped close the case socially before every historical question had been answered. Reports described crowds dancing and expressing confidence that the fear of night-time abduction had ended.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://liberiapastandpresent.org/MarylandRitualMurders10.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: liberiapastandpresent.org">[Liberia Past and Present]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">liberiapastandpresent.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on liberiapastandpresent.org.</span></span></span> The executions became a powerful memory of state punishment, while the quality of the investigation received less sustained public scrutiny.

## Where are Liberia’s wartime missing?

Liberia’s most consequential unresolved cases come from the two civil wars fought between 1989 and 2003. Combatants from government forces and rival armed factions committed massacres, torture, rape, forced recruitment and summary executions. Human Rights Watch documented severe abuses by all sides from the opening stages of the conflict. Later estimates commonly placed the total number of dead at about 250,000, though the destruction of records and movement of displaced populations make exact counting difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/reports/1990/liberia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Human Rights Watch Liberia: A Human Rights Disaster: Violations of the Laws</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights Watch Liberia: A Human Rights Disaster: Violations of the Laws</span></span></span>

Unlike Roye’s single disputed death, the wartime mystery concerns thousands of individual fates. Some people vanished at checkpoints. Others were taken from homes, refugee columns or detention sites. Bodies were left in streets, thrown into rivers or buried in unmarked graves. Families often received conflicting rumours but no identified remains, death certificate or authoritative account.

Liberia’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission gathered more than 20,000 statements from witnesses and survivors in Liberia and the diaspora. Its final report attributed widespread killings and disappearances to successive governments and armed factions, named alleged perpetrators and recommended prosecutions, reparations and further investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hmcwordpress.humanities.mcmaster.ca/Truthcommissions/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Liberia.TRC_.Report-FULL.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hmcwordpress.humanities.mcmaster.ca">[mcmaster.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hmcwordpress.humanities.mcmaster.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Liberia (TRC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Liberia (TRC</span></span></span>

The commission did not, and could not, resolve every disappearance. Testimony collected years after an event may be incomplete or contradictory. Witnesses sometimes knew which armed group controlled an area but not the identity of an individual killer. Victims shared names, fighters used aliases, and records were lost or never created. Recommended follow-up measures included cooperation with specialists capable of tracing missing people and investigating burial sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theadvocatesforhumanrights.org/Res/chapter_14-recommendations_submitted_to_the_truth_and_reconciliation_commission_of_liberia.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theadvocatesforhumanrights.org">[theadvocatesforhumanrights.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theadvocatesforhumanrights.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Chapter Fourteen Recommendations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chapter Fourteen Recommendations</span></span></span>

Mass graves are not merely locations where answers might be found. They are fragile crime scenes. Without controlled excavation, forensic examination and reliable handling of evidence, remains can be mixed, identities lost and legal cases weakened. International guidance stresses that grave sites must be protected both to preserve evidence and to respect the rights of families seeking the truth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icmp.int/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/mass_graves_project_english-4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mass Grave Protection and Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mass Grave Protection and Investigation</span></span></span>

The delay has increased the difficulty. Landscapes change, witnesses die, memories fade and alleged perpetrators acquire political influence. Prince Johnson, whose forces captured and killed President Samuel Doe in 1990, later became a senator and major political figure. He died in November 2024 without appearing before a Liberian war-crimes court. His death removed a potential source of testimony about crimes committed by his faction and about the wider command structures of the war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/2eb30dcea72132f5d0f3ca55ce1f196e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

In May 2024, President Joseph Boakai signed an executive order supporting the creation of a war and economic crimes court. The move followed years of campaigning by victims’ groups and represented an important step towards testing allegations through formal legal proceedings. Yet a court cannot automatically recover missing records, identify every grave or determine every victim’s fate. Its value will depend partly on witness protection, forensic capacity, access to archives and freedom from political interference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/cddf2b6617903e2007ad23d2da534be1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

The central unresolved questions are painfully practical: who was killed, by whom, under whose orders, and where were the victims buried? For relatives, an identified grave or credible account may matter as much as a prominent conviction. Liberia’s wartime disappearances are therefore not mysteries in the entertainment sense. They are unfinished human-rights investigations whose answers could restore names and histories to people reduced to casualty estimates.

## The unresolved legacy of Liberia's historical mysteries
Liberia’s unresolved past is shaped by several recurring problems. Political transitions encouraged rival versions of events; official accounts often served the government or social group in power. Archives were limited even before the civil wars and suffered further disruption during years of violence. Courts sometimes operated under intense pressure, while many wartime crimes never reached a court at all. Oral memory preserved vital information, but it could also combine eyewitness experience with symbolism, rumour and later political interpretation.

These weaknesses do not make every explanation equally credible. The Roye case remains open because decisive contemporary evidence is absent, not because every assassination theory deserves acceptance. The Matilda Newport story is historically important even though its most dramatic detail lacks early confirmation. The Harper convictions cannot simply be dismissed, but allegations of coercion prevent the verdicts from closing all debate. Wartime testimony establishes extensive atrocities beyond serious doubt, although thousands of individual cases still require verification.

The strongest approach is to distinguish levels of certainty. A death, battle or massacre may be well documented while the identity of the perpetrator remains unknown. A person may be historically real while the heroic story attached to them developed later. A court may produce a lawful verdict without persuading historians that its investigation was fair. A truth commission may expose the scale of violence without locating every victim.

That distinction protects Liberia’s history from two opposite errors: treating official narratives as unquestionable fact, or replacing evidence with conspiracy. The enduring value of these mysteries lies in the search for a more complete public record—one capable of recognising victims, testing national legends and explaining how power influenced what Liberians were encouraged to remember or forget.
