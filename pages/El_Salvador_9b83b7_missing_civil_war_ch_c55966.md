## Introduction

Hundreds of children disappeared during [El Salvador]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-el-salvador/' | relative_url }})'s civil war (1980–1992), creating one of the country's most enduring historical mysteries. Some vanished during military operations, others became separated from their families while fleeing violence, and some entered orphanages or adoption systems under new identities. Decades later, many of those children are now adults, raising painful questions that remain unresolved: Were they killed, adopted, raised under different names, or are they still unaware of their biological families? Although investigators have reunited many families through archival research and DNA testing, hundreds of cases remain open, making the search for identity both a humanitarian mission and an unfinished chapter of Salvadoran history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asociacionprobusqueda.org/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asociacionprobusqueda.org">[asociacionprobusqueda.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asociacionprobusqueda.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span></span></span>

## How children disappeared during military operations

The disappearance of children was not the result of a single mechanism. During major military offensives in rural areas, families were often scattered while fleeing bombardments, massacres and ground operations. Infants and young children could become separated from parents who were killed, detained or forced to escape in different directions. In other cases, witnesses reported soldiers taking surviving children after villages had been attacked.

These disappearances occurred under widely varying circumstances:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Children were left behind after attacks because relatives believed they had been killed or could not safely return.
* Some were transferred to children's institutions or orphanages without reliable records of their origins.
* Others entered domestic or international adoption systems with incomplete or altered documentation.
* A number grew up with adoptive families believing they had been abandoned rather than forcibly separated.

</div>

The result was not a single organised programme affecting every child, but hundreds of individual cases shaped by wartime chaos, inconsistent record-keeping and, in some instances, deliberate concealment. Investigators have repeatedly stressed that each disappearance requires reconstruction from fragmentary testimony, military operations, church records, institutional files and surviving family memories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asociacionprobusqueda.org/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asociacionprobusqueda.org">[asociacionprobusqueda.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asociacionprobusqueda.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span></span></span>

## Adoption records, changed identities and DNA searches

For many families, the greatest obstacle has been the loss or alteration of identity itself. Children who were adopted frequently received new names, new birth records or incomplete paperwork, making later searches extremely difficult. Some were adopted abroad and grew up unaware that they had surviving relatives in El Salvador.

This explains why the mystery extends beyond locating a missing person. Even when an individual is found, investigators must establish whether documentary records accurately reflect their origins.

The organisation Pro-Búsqueda, founded in 1994 by relatives of the disappeared together with Jesuit priest Jon Cortina, became the leading institution investigating these cases. Its work combines:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* interviews with surviving relatives and witnesses;
* examination of civil, church and adoption records;
* cooperation with foreign authorities where international adoptions occurred;
* DNA testing to confirm biological relationships;
* psychological support before and after reunification.

</div>

DNA has transformed many investigations, especially where paperwork proved unreliable or had been falsified. Modern genetic comparison has enabled adults living both inside and outside El Salvador to discover biological relatives decades after their disappearance as children. Yet DNA alone cannot answer every question. Many parents have died before providing samples, some records no longer exist, and some adoptees remain unaware that their family histories may be incomplete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asociacionprobusqueda.org/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asociacionprobusqueda.org">[asociacionprobusqueda.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asociacionprobusqueda.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span></span></span>

The search has therefore become as much about recovering identity as locating a person. Adults reunited with biological families often discover different names, different birthdays and entirely different childhood histories from those they had believed throughout their lives. Emotional reunions are frequently accompanied by difficult questions about belonging, language, nationality and relationships with both biological and adoptive families.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asociacionprobusqueda.org/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asociacionprobusqueda.org">[Pro-Búsqueda]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asociacionprobusqueda.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span></span></span>

## The Serrano Cruz case and the barriers to answers

Among the best-known disappearances are the sisters Ernestina and Erlinda Serrano Cruz, aged seven and three, who vanished during a military operation in Chalatenango in June 1982. Their family maintained that soldiers took the girls after relatives became separated while fleeing the operation.

The case eventually reached the Inter-American Court of Human Rights. In 2005, the Court concluded that El Salvador had failed to conduct an effective investigation into the sisters' disappearance and ordered measures intended to improve searches for missing children, including stronger investigative mechanisms and reparations for the family. However, the judgment did not establish what ultimately happened to the sisters themselves. That absence of a final answer illustrates the broader challenge facing many wartime disappearances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2008/03/missing-children-el-salvador-20080328/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International The missing children of El Salvador</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalThe missing children of El Salvador - Amnesty International...</span></span></span>

The Serrano Cruz case also exposed wider structural barriers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* military archives have often been incomplete, inaccessible or disputed;
* wartime records from institutions and orphanages were frequently inadequate;
* adoption documentation could be inconsistent or altered;
* decades of delay meant witnesses died or memories faded before investigations began.

</div>

Even where courts recognised state responsibility for inadequate investigations, reconstructing an individual child's journey decades later has often proved impossible because crucial evidence no longer exists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2008/03/missing-children-el-salvador-20080328/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International The missing children of El Salvador</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalThe missing children of El Salvador - Amnesty International...</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery remains unresolved

Although hundreds of children have now been located, many cases remain open. The uncertainty persists because several different outcomes are plausible, and each requires different evidence to verify.

Some children almost certainly died during military operations without being identified. Others survived and were adopted, either within El Salvador or abroad. Still others may still be living under different identities, unaware that relatives continue searching for them.

The destruction of investigative records has also complicated the search. In 2013, armed attackers targeted Pro-Búsqueda's headquarters, destroying a large proportion of its archives and damaging recently collected DNA material. Human rights bodies condemned the attack because the organisation's records represented decades of work tracing disappeared children and documenting potential evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oas.org/en/iachr/media_center/PReleases/2013/090.asp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oas.org">[Organization of American States]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">anization of American StatesIACHR Condemns Attack on Pro-Búsqueda and Destruction of Records in El SalvadorNovember 22, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 22, 2013</span></span></span>

The mystery therefore survives not because historians doubt that children disappeared, but because the personal histories of many individual children remain incomplete. Every unresolved case reflects missing records, interrupted family histories and unanswered questions about identity that continue to affect survivors and relatives decades after the war ended.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asociacionprobusqueda.org/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asociacionprobusqueda.org">[asociacionprobusqueda.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asociacionprobusqueda.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pro-Búsqueda Home &#124; Pro-Búsqueda</span></span></span>
