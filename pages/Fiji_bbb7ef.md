## Introduction

These mysteries matter because they expose a wider problem in Fiji’s history: much of the surviving written record was produced by colonial officials, missionaries and foreign newspapers, while archaeological evidence is incomplete and oral traditions can change as they pass between generations. Some questions may eventually be resolved by new excavations or scientific testing. Others are likely to remain open because the decisive witnesses, documents or physical traces have disappeared.

## Why the *Joyita* was found empty

The *Joyita* is the clearest unsolved disappearance associated with Fiji. On 3 October 1955, the 21-metre trading vessel left Apia in Samoa for the Tokelau Islands. It carried 16 crew members, nine passengers and a cargo that included food, timber and medical supplies. The voyage should have taken about two days. The ship never arrived, and an aerial search found nothing.

On 10 November, more than five weeks after its departure, another vessel encountered the *Joyita* drifting north of Vanua Levu. It was badly listing, partly flooded and hundreds of kilometres from its intended route. Every person aboard had vanished. Its dinghy and three emergency rafts were missing, as were navigational instruments, the ship’s log and some of the captain’s possessions. The formal commission investigating the loss was appointed by the New Zealand administration of Western Samoa. It concluded that the ship had been in poor condition but that the fate of those aboard was “inexplicable on the evidence” presented. That carefully worded finding is still the centre of the mystery: investigators could reconstruct a serious emergency, but not the final decision to abandon the vessel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazette.howison.co.nz/nzg/1956/issue/1/page/7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazette.howison.co.nz">[New Zealand Gazette]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazette.howison.co.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">New Zealand Gazette Page 7, No</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1, NZ Gazette 1956 - New Zealand Gazette IndexCommission of Inquiry, Motor Vessel Joyita, Maritime casualty, Investigation Names. ### What probably went wrong

Examination of the vessel found evidence of a mechanical failure rather than an attack. A corroded pipe in an engine-cooling system had allowed seawater into the hull. Debris probably obstructed the pumps, while defective wiring severely limited the radio’s range. The ship had also left port with one engine already out of action. Electrical switches and clocks suggested that the crisis developed at night, when finding the source of the flooding would have been difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Joyita" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MV Joyita</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MV Joyita</span></span></span>

The *Joyita* nevertheless possessed an unusual degree of buoyancy because its holds were insulated with cork. Even when flooded and listing, it remained afloat. Captain Thomas “Dusty” Miller is generally presumed to have known that abandoning it would be dangerous. This is why the missing boats are so important: someone apparently ordered or accepted an evacuation from a vessel that was damaged but not sinking.

One plausible reconstruction is that those aboard did not understand the ship’s buoyancy. With water rising, engines failing and the radio apparently unable to summon help, passengers and less experienced crew members may have believed that the vessel was about to founder. The captain may have been injured or overruled. Bloodstained bandages found aboard have encouraged this theory, although they do not establish who was hurt or how seriously. The evacuees would then have entered overloaded small craft and been lost in rough seas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Joyita" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MV Joyita</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MV Joyita</span></span></span>

Piracy, mutiny, Japanese fishing vessels and even a Soviet submarine were suggested at different times. None is supported by persuasive physical evidence. The mechanical-emergency explanation requires no secret attackers, but it still cannot answer who made the decision to leave, exactly where the boats went, or why not one trace of 25 people was recovered. The *Joyita* mystery is therefore best understood as a probably explicable maritime disaster whose final human sequence is unknowable.

Fiji became central to the story because the ship was discovered near its waters, towed to Suva and later operated between Fijian ports. Its subsequent groundings helped give it the reputation of an unlucky vessel, adding folklore to what had begun as a documented loss at sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Joyita" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MV Joyita</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MV Joyita</span></span></span>

## Who were Fiji’s first settlers?

The deeper archaeological mystery concerns the first people to settle the Fijian islands. Archaeologists associate the earliest known communities with the Lapita cultural tradition, recognised particularly by pottery decorated with finely stamped geometric designs. Lapita sites extend across a vast area of the western Pacific, showing that skilled seafarers moved eastwards through island groups long before European navigation reached the region.

At Bourewa, on the south-western coast of Viti Levu, excavations uncovered what researchers regard as one of Fiji’s earliest known settlements. Radiocarbon evidence suggests occupation beginning roughly three millennia ago, when the site was probably on or beside a small offshore island with easy access to reefs and marine resources. Nearby settlements appear to have developed later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.usc.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Early-Lapita-settlement-site-at-Bourewa/99450434502621" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.usc.edu.au">[edu.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.usc.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Portal Early Lapita settlement site at Bourewa, southwest Viti Levu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research PortalEarly Lapita settlement site at Bourewa, southwest Viti Levu...July 10, 2004 — by P Nunn · 2004 · Cited by 61 — A newly-d...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 2004</span></span></span>

The broad outline is well established: settlers reached Fiji by sea, established coastal communities and brought pottery-making, agriculture and a network of exchange. The unanswered questions concern their precise routes, identities and relationships with later populations.

Radiocarbon dating does not provide a simple founding date. Samples may come from old wood, disturbed layers or material deposited after a settlement began. At Bourewa, researchers assessed dozens of dates but rejected some because they could not be securely connected with the Lapita occupation. The site’s shoreline has also changed, eroding or burying parts of the original settlement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nunn Petchey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nunn Petchey</span></span></span>

The distribution of early sites raises further questions. Were the islands settled in a rapid wave, with voyagers moving quickly through the archipelago? Did one founding community establish smaller settlements elsewhere? Or did several groups arrive independently, following different routes through Melanesia and western Polynesia? Archaeological models have produced different answers because the surviving sites are unevenly distributed and many early coastal locations may now lie underwater or beneath later deposits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-319-51726" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Nature Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nature Link Lapita Archaeology in the Southwest Pacific</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nature Link Lapita Archaeology in the Southwest Pacific</span></span></span>

The disappearance of elaborately decorated Lapita pottery presents another puzzle. It does not mean that the Lapita settlers vanished. A more likely explanation is that their descendants changed how pottery was made and used as communities became established and developed distinct island identities. Yet the speed and pattern of that cultural change remain debated.

This is a mystery with realistic prospects of progress. More coastal surveys, underwater archaeology, improved dating and carefully conducted ancient-DNA research could clarify how many migration events occurred. The central uncertainty is not whether Fiji was settled by capable ocean-going communities, but how those communities formed the population and cultures that followed.

## What Fiji’s human remains really show

No subject in Fiji’s historical memory is more distorted by outsiders than cannibalism. Nineteenth-century sailors, missionaries and colonial writers repeatedly presented the islands as a place of indiscriminate man-eating. The image helped missionaries portray conversion as a rescue from savagery and allowed colonial intervention to be described as a civilising necessity.

There is credible evidence that human flesh was consumed in some Fijian contexts. The difficult question is how often it occurred, where it occurred, and what it meant. Archaeology does not support treating every scattered or damaged human bone as proof of a feast.

At the Navatu site on Viti Levu, an osteological study compared human remains from ordinary burials with bones found in other deposits. The burial remains were mostly unmodified, while some other material showed patterns consistent with processing. Such evidence can include cut marks, burning, deliberate breakage and the selective presence or absence of particular bones. No single mark is decisive because burial, warfare, animal activity and later disturbance can produce superficially similar damage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10502244/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Research in the Lau Islands likewise found modified human remains at several archaeological sites, but interpretation required comparison with burial practice, animal butchery, diet and the physical history of each deposit. Researchers have warned that the dividing line between cannibalism and complex mortuary treatment is not always clear. A body might be dismembered, heated or moved as part of burial ritual without its flesh necessarily being eaten.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholarscommons.fgcu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Kana-Tamata-or-Feasts-of-Men/99383341558706570" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholarscommons.fgcu.edu">[fgcu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholarscommons.fgcu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fgcu.edu.</span></span></span>

An adolescent’s remains found in an earth-oven context on Waya Island illustrate the problem. The bones bore modifications that could support a cannibalistic interpretation, including breakage and possible exposure to heat. Yet researchers also considered whether the treatment formed part of a mortuary ceremony rather than an ordinary act of food consumption.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230867401_Culturally_modified_human_remains_recovered_from_an_earth-oven_interment_on_Waya_Island_Fiji" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

A recently investigated mass burial on Viti Levu has revived similar questions. Possible explanations include violence, ritual treatment and epidemic mortality. Fiji suffered catastrophic disease outbreaks during the nineteenth century, including the 1875 measles epidemic, so a group burial cannot safely be labelled evidence of warfare or cannibalism without dating, skeletal analysis and historical context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.andrewlawler.com/mass-grave-found-in-fiji-sparks-a-mystery-cannibalism-or-contagion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: andrewlawler.com">[Andrew Lawler]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">andrewlawler.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Andrew Lawler Mass grave found in Fiji sparks a mystery: cannibalism or</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Andrew Lawler Mass grave found in Fiji sparks a mystery: cannibalism or</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusion lies between denial and colonial caricature. Cannibalism appears to have occurred in Fiji, particularly in some ritual, political or wartime settings. The evidence does not justify the old image of a society organised around routine consumption of human flesh. Archaeology is gradually replacing that stereotype with a more complicated picture in which treatment of the dead could express hostility, victory, spiritual power, mourning or respect.

## What happened to Thomas Baker?

On 21 July 1867, Wesleyan missionary Thomas Baker and several Fijian Christian companions were killed in the highlands of Viti Levu. Baker’s death is not mysterious in the basic sense: contemporary and later accounts agree on the location, the broad circumstances and the responsibility of local people. What remains uncertain is the exact trigger, the number of victims and how much of the familiar story was shaped by missionary storytelling.

The best-known version says that Baker offended a chief by touching his head while retrieving a comb. Because the head carried special status, the gesture supposedly led directly to his killing and consumption. Another version places the incident within a larger political struggle over missionary expansion, local authority and the pressure on highland communities to accept Christianity. A personal insult may have contributed, but reducing the episode to one accidental breach of etiquette removes the conflict surrounding Baker’s mission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Baker_%28missionary%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas Baker (missionary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomas Baker (missionary</span></span></span>

Published accounts also differ over how many Fijian members of the party died. Some report seven, others eight. The Fijian victims are often treated as background figures even though they were participants in a dangerous period of religious and political transformation. The commonly repeated claim that Baker was Fiji’s “last victim of cannibalism” is also difficult to prove. It is better understood as a symbolic ending used in missionary and tourism narratives than as a securely documented national last occurrence.

Objects associated with the event—including pieces said to be the soles of Baker’s footwear—have helped turn the killing into a museum story. Their survival does not verify every detail attached to them. Relics can be authentic while the explanations accumulated around them remain simplified or embellished.

In 2003, people from the village associated with the killing held a formal reconciliation with Baker’s descendants. Participants linked the ceremony to a belief that the community had suffered misfortune because of an inherited curse. News reports often presented the event as an apology by “cannibals”, repeating the sensational language through which Fiji had long been marketed abroad. For the community, however, the ceremony also concerned responsibility, memory and the repair of a relationship across generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2003-11-13/fijians-apologise-to-missionarys-descendants/1508580" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News Fijians apologise to missionary&#x27;s descendants</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC News Fijians apologise to missionary&#x27;s descendants</span></span></span>

The historical question is therefore not whether Baker died. It is how an episode involving local resistance, religious conversion and several Fijian deaths became condensed into a memorable tale about a missionary, a comb and a curse.

## Was the “Fiji mermaid” ever from Fiji?

One famous object bearing Fiji’s name has almost no reliable connection to the country. The so-called Fiji, or “Feejee”, mermaid was a grotesque figure made by attaching part of a monkey to the tail of a fish. In 1842, the American showman P. T. Barnum promoted such a specimen as the remains of a real creature supposedly captured near Fiji.

The object was probably produced in Asia, where fabricated mermaid specimens had been made for display and sale. Barnum’s campaign involved invented testimony, misleading newspaper letters and a man posing as a naturalist. The exhibit’s supposed Fijian origin supplied exotic distance: few members of its American audience possessed enough knowledge of the Pacific to test the claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiji_mermaid" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fiji mermaid</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fiji mermaid</span></span></span>

The whereabouts of Barnum’s original specimen are uncertain. It may have been destroyed in a museum fire, while several later objects have been presented as the authentic mermaid. Researchers who have examined these claimants generally regard them as additional fabrications rather than the missing original. The unresolved part is therefore the history of the object, not its biological identity.

For Fiji, the case is significant because it demonstrates how the country’s name functioned within nineteenth-century entertainment. “Fiji” could be used as shorthand for a remote place where European and American audiences imagined that ordinary rules did not apply. The mermaid belongs to the history of Western hoaxing and museum spectacle, but its false geographical label reflects the same exoticising culture that popularised stories of curses and indiscriminate cannibalism.

## Why some Fijian mysteries resist solution

These cases remain uncertain for different reasons. The *Joyita* lost all its human witnesses at sea. Early settlement sites have been damaged by erosion and changing coastlines. Human remains can preserve signs of physical treatment without preserving the beliefs or intentions behind it. Colonial accounts of Baker’s death mixed observation with missionary purpose, while the Fiji mermaid began as deliberate deception.

They nevertheless share several warning signs that help separate a serious historical mystery from modern mythmaking:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A missing answer is not evidence for an extraordinary answer.** The absence of the *Joyita*’s passengers does not support submarines, sea monsters or paranormal intervention.
* **Physical evidence needs context.** Cut or heated bone may indicate cannibalism, but it can also result from mortuary practice, violence or later disturbance.
* **A repeated story is not necessarily an early story.** The comb explanation for Baker’s death became famous partly because it was simple and memorable.
* **Colonial labels carried political weight.** Descriptions of Fiji as inherently savage helped justify religious and imperial expansion.
* **Folklore can be historically important without being literally factual.** The belief in a curse connected with Baker’s death shaped reconciliation and public memory, regardless of whether misfortune can be traced to supernatural punishment.

</div>

Fiji’s enduring enigmas are most revealing when treated neither as tourist legends nor as puzzles with a single dramatic solution. They show how accidents become mysteries, how incomplete remains invite competing interpretations, and how foreign accounts can overshadow local experience. The unanswered questions are part of the story, but so is the struggle over who has the authority to explain them.
