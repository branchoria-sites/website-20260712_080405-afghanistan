## Introduction

Did the Spanish warship **San Telmo** become the first vessel whose crew reached Antarctica? The honest answer is that nobody knows. What is well established is that the 74-gun ship disappeared in September 1819 after being crippled by storms while rounding Cape Horn, carrying more than 600 sailors, soldiers and passengers. Within months, British sealers reported finding wreckage on Livingston Island in the South Shetland Islands. If that wreckage did indeed come from *San Telmo*, and if any of those aboard survived long enough to reach land, they would have set foot in Antarctica months before the first generally accepted recorded landings. The mystery persists because the available evidence strongly suggests a shipwreck in Antarctic waters, yet no definitive archaeological proof has ever demonstrated that survivors reached shore.

## The Warship's Final Voyage

The *San Telmo* was one of [Spain]({{ 'spain/' | relative_url }})'s large ships of the line, launched in 1788 and armed with 74 guns. In 1819 it sailed from Cádiz as part of a squadron sent to reinforce Spanish forces in Peru during the South American wars of independence. The expedition had already suffered setbacks before reaching the dangerous waters around Cape Horn.

On 2 September 1819, the convoy encountered severe storms in the Drake Passage. According to reports from the accompanying vessels, *San Telmo* suffered catastrophic damage. Its rudder and parts of its stern structure were reportedly lost, leaving the ship barely controllable before it disappeared into heavy seas. The remaining ships continued separately, but *San Telmo* was never seen again. In 1822 the Spanish Navy formally declared the vessel and all 644 people aboard lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_ship_San_Telmo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spanish ship San Telmo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spanish ship San Telmo</span></span></span>

The accepted facts therefore end with a disappearance, not a confirmed wreck. Everything afterwards depends upon interpreting fragmentary evidence found hundreds of kilometres farther south.

## Why the Antarctic Theory Emerged

The puzzle began because of discoveries made shortly after *San Telmo* vanished.

In February 1819, British merchant captain William Smith had accidentally sighted what became known as the South Shetland Islands. During subsequent voyages in late 1819 and 1820, British sealers explored the islands more thoroughly, making the first documented landings recognised in traditional exploration histories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishantarcticterritory.org.uk/two-hundred-years-since-the-discovery-of-the-south-shetland-islands/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishantarcticterritory.org.uk">[British Antarctic Territory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishantarcticterritory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Antarctic TerritoryTwo hundred years since the discovery of the South Shetland Islands - British Antarctic TerritoryFebruary 19...</span></span></span>

However, later British accounts described finding signs that another ship had already come to grief there. Captain Robert Fildes recorded remains of a recently wrecked vessel on the northern coast of Livingston Island, including timbers, spars and other debris that appeared relatively fresh. Some later historians concluded that the most plausible candidate was *San Telmo*, since no other known large European vessel had disappeared in the region at that time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_ship_San_Telmo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spanish ship San Telmo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spanish ship San Telmo</span></span></span>

This raises the central question: if the wreck reached Livingston Island, could some of its crew have reached shore before dying?

## Wreckage Reports and Competing Explanations

The surviving evidence supports several interpretations rather than one decisive conclusion.

The strongest points in favour of the Antarctic landing theory include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The timing fits. *San Telmo* disappeared only weeks before British sealing expeditions reached the South Shetland Islands.
* Contemporary British reports describe wreckage consistent with a substantial wooden warship rather than a small sealing vessel.
* The western end of Livingston Island still includes **San Telmo Island**, a name preserving the long-standing association between the Spanish ship and the area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_ship_San_Telmo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spanish ship San Telmo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spanish ship San Telmo</span></span></span>

</div>

Spanish researchers have also pointed to archaeological surveys carried out during the 1990s around Cape Shirreff and nearby sites. These investigations reported nineteenth-century materials that some researchers considered compatible with a Spanish naval vessel, although none has provided universally accepted identification of *San Telmo* itself. More importantly, none has demonstrated the existence of a surviving camp established by shipwrecked sailors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://as.com/actualidad/sociedad/la-historia-del-san-telmo-el-navio-espanol-desaparecido-en-1819-que-pudo-ser-el-primero-en-llegar-a-la-antartida-n/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: as.com">[Diario AS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">as.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on as.com.</span></span></span>

The alternative explanation is simpler. A ship that had lost both steering and structural integrity in one of the world's most violent stretches of ocean could easily have broken apart before reaching land. Floating wreckage might then have washed ashore without any survivors.

Another possibility is that the debris belonged to another vessel. Early nineteenth-century sealing expanded rapidly after the South Shetlands became known, and wooden ships were frequently damaged in the region. Although *San Telmo* remains the leading candidate, absolute identification has never been achieved.

## Would Survivors Have Been Able to Live?

Even if *San Telmo* reached Livingston Island, survival would have been extraordinarily difficult.

The South Shetland Islands lie in one of the harshest environments on Earth. Temperatures remain close to freezing even in summer, storms are frequent, and food would have been extremely limited for men suddenly stranded without shelter or adequate clothing.

Some historians suggest that a handful of sailors might have survived for days or perhaps weeks by using wreckage as shelter and hunting seals or seabirds. Others argue that the crew, already exhausted after the storm and lacking specialised polar equipment, would probably have succumbed quickly to exposure.

No confirmed graves, camps, tools or inscriptions attributable to *San Telmo*'s crew have ever been found. Without such evidence, any reconstruction of their final days remains speculative.

## What Would Count as Proof?

The mystery survives because historians distinguish between a highly plausible scenario and demonstrated fact.

Evidence that would substantially strengthen the case would include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A securely identified section of *San Telmo*'s hull found underwater.
* Artefacts carrying unmistakable Spanish naval markings in their original archaeological context.
* Human remains conclusively linked to members of the crew through forensic or genetic analysis.
* Contemporary documents describing survivors before later British arrivals.

</div>

At present, none of these standards has been met.

The absence of proof is particularly important because exploration history traditionally distinguishes between **sighting**, **landing**, and **surviving on land**. Even if *San Telmo* drifted into Antarctic waters, historians still require convincing evidence that people actually reached shore before rewriting the chronology of Antarctic exploration.

## Why the Mystery Still Matters

The story of *San Telmo* occupies an unusual place in Spain's maritime history because it combines documented tragedy with a genuine historical uncertainty.

Unlike many legends of forgotten discoveries, the disappearance itself is not disputed. The uncertainty concerns what happened after contact with the outside world ended. If even a few members of the crew reached Livingston Island alive, Spain's connection with Antarctica would begin not with a planned expedition but with an accidental shipwreck.

For most historians, the accepted first documented landings remain those made by British sealers after William Smith's discovery of the South Shetland Islands, because they are supported by clear contemporary records.
