## Introduction

Was an ancient city hidden beneath Tongatapu? The short answer is both yes and no. Modern airborne laser scanning, or LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging), has revealed that [Tonga]({{ 'what-secrets-still-survive-in-tongas/' | relative_url }})’s main island preserves an immense network of ancient earthworks, roads and settlement features that were largely invisible from ground level. The discoveries have transformed archaeologists’ understanding of how people lived on Tongatapu, showing a densely occupied, carefully organised landscape rather than scattered villages. However, describing the island as a “lost city” can be misleading. The evidence points instead to an extensive form of low-density urbanisation spread across the landscape rather than a compact city of stone buildings. This distinction matters because it changes how historians understand the development of political power, population and social organisation in the Pacific.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a Pacific Society &#124; Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory &#124; Springe...</span></span></span>

## What aerial mapping found beneath the modern landscape

For generations, archaeologists knew Tongatapu contained royal tombs, ceremonial monuments and earthworks, but many smaller features had become difficult to detect because of vegetation, farming and centuries of landscape change. LiDAR transformed this picture by producing a detailed digital model of the ground surface that could reveal subtle archaeological remains hidden beneath trees and modern land use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440316300206" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

The surveys identified an extraordinary archaeological landscape, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Around 10,000 earthen mounds of varying sizes, many interpreted as former residential house platforms.
* Networks of ancient roads linking settlements across the island.
* Ceremonial and civic spaces associated with mound complexes.
* Defensive earthworks and fortifications from different periods.
* Monumental chiefly structures already known from traditional archaeology, now understood as parts of a much larger built environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440316300206" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

</div>

One important finding is that many of the mounds are surprisingly small and shallow. Traditional ground surveys often missed them because they rise only a few tens of centimetres above the surrounding land. Automated analysis of the LiDAR data allowed researchers to map these subtle features systematically, revealing settlement patterns that had previously been impossible to reconstruct.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440316300206" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

The mapping also showed that the distribution of mounds was not random. Their arrangement reflects long-term patterns of land division, transport routes and social organisation, suggesting that the landscape itself embodied political authority and community structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://agris.fao.org/search/en/providers/122535/records/65de27aa4c5aef494fda7a53" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: agris.fao.org">[Agris]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">agris.fao.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Automated feature extraction for prospection and analysis of monumental earthworks from aerial LiDAR in the Kingdom of TongaJanuary...</span></span></span>

## Why the "lost city" label can mislead

Media coverage of the research sometimes described the discovery as an "ancient city" hidden beneath Tongatapu. While that phrase captures public imagination, archaeologists generally use more careful language.

The new evidence does not reveal buried streets lined with stone buildings comparable to Rome or Pompeii. Instead, it supports the idea that Tongatapu developed a form of **low-density urbanisation**. In this model, settlements spread across a broad landscape while remaining socially, economically and politically integrated through roads, public spaces and institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a Pacific Society &#124; Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory &#124; Springe...</span></span></span>

This matters because many traditional definitions of cities were based on densely packed buildings enclosed within walls. Research over the past two decades has shown that some ancient societies—including the Maya and parts of Southeast Asia—developed extensive urban landscapes instead. The Tongatapu evidence suggests that the Pacific should now be included in this broader understanding of how cities could develop.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a Pacific Society &#124; Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory &#124; Springe...</span></span></span>

Researchers therefore argue that the real discovery is not a forgotten metropolis suddenly uncovered beneath jungle. Rather, it is recognition that an apparently rural modern landscape preserves the remains of an interconnected settlement system extending across much of the island.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a Pacific Society &#124; Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory &#124; Springe...</span></span></span>

## What the earthworks reveal about early Tongan society

Perhaps the most important mystery is not whether the earthworks exist—they clearly do—but what they reveal about the rise of Tongan society.

Radiocarbon dating and archaeological investigation indicate that some major earthwork complexes may have originated centuries before the fully developed Tuʻi Tonga state emerged. Recent research argues that organised settlement growth and increasingly complex public infrastructure began well before the political institutions traditionally associated with Tonga's regional power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a Pacific Society &#124; Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory &#124; Springe...</span></span></span>

The LiDAR evidence also helps explain how such a large society functioned:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Residential mounds appear to have been occupied and rebuilt over long periods.
* Broad trunk roads connected communities across the island, with narrower roads branching into neighbourhoods.
* Open communal spaces, wells and mound complexes repeatedly occur together, suggesting planned local centres rather than isolated households.
* Fortifications reveal that warfare also became an important influence on parts of the landscape, particularly during later periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[springer.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a Pacific Society &#124; Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory &#124; Springe...</span></span></span>

</div>

Because the surveys cover the whole island, archaeologists can examine ordinary residential areas alongside elite monuments. This provides a much fuller picture of society than earlier research, which naturally focused on spectacular structures such as royal tombs and ceremonial sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440316300206" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

## What remains uncertain

Despite the remarkable mapping results, important questions remain unresolved.

Many mounds have not been excavated, so their exact functions cannot always be identified from shape alone. Some served as house platforms, others as burial mounds, sporting structures or chiefly features, and distinguishing between them often requires field investigation rather than remote sensing alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440316300206" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

Dating also remains incomplete. Although recent excavations suggest that organised earthwork construction began much earlier than once believed, not every mound has been dated, making it difficult to reconstruct the precise sequence of settlement expansion across the island.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a Pacific Society &#124; Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory &#124; Springe...</span></span></span>

Finally, archaeologists continue to debate exactly when Tongatapu crossed the threshold from large interconnected settlements to genuine urbanisation. The latest research argues that urban processes began before state formation, but this interpretation is still being discussed within wider debates about how cities should be defined across different cultures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a Pacific Society &#124; Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory &#124; Springe...</span></span></span>

## Why the hidden landscape matters

Tongatapu's hidden settlement landscape is significant because it overturns older assumptions that complex urban development in the Pacific was limited or arrived late. Instead, the island preserves one of the world's best examples of a low-density settlement system whose organisation only became fully visible through modern aerial mapping.

Rather than uncovering a mythical lost city, LiDAR has revealed something arguably more important: an entire inhabited landscape whose roads, house platforms, ceremonial spaces and earthworks demonstrate that early Tongans organised society on a scale far larger and more sophisticated than had previously been recognised.
