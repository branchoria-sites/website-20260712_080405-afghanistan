## Introduction

The mystery of the *San José* is no longer about whether the ship existed or where it sank. Historians agree that the Spanish warship exploded and went down near Cartagena in 1708 during the War of the Spanish Succession, carrying a vast cargo of gold, silver, emeralds and commercial goods, while around 600 people died aboard. What remains fiercely disputed is who has the legal, moral and archaeological right to the wreck and everything it contains. More than three centuries later, the *San José* has become one of the world's most controversial underwater heritage sites: part war grave, part archaeological time capsule and part symbol of colonial exploitation. The arguments involve [Colombia]({{ 'what-really-lies-behind-colombias/' | relative_url }}), Spain, a US salvage company, Indigenous representatives and archaeologists, each advancing different ideas about ownership, preservation and historical justice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history/2026/april/recovery-holy-grail-shipwrecks-begins-amid-decades-long-legal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteRecovery of the ‘Holy Grail of Shipwrecks’ Begins amid Decades-Long Legal Battle &#124; Naval History - April 2026, Volume...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 2026</span></span></span>

## The battle and discovery of the wreck

The *San José* was a heavily armed Spanish galleon sailing from South America towards Europe when it encountered a British squadron commanded by Commodore Charles Wager on 8 June 1708. Britain hoped to capture the treasure fleet and deprive Spain of badly needed funds during the War of the Spanish Succession. Instead, the *San José* exploded—probably after its powder magazine detonated—and sank off the coast near Cartagena. Only a handful of those aboard survived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history/2026/april/recovery-holy-grail-shipwrecks-begins-amid-decades-long-legal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteRecovery of the ‘Holy Grail of Shipwrecks’ Begins amid Decades-Long Legal Battle &#124; Naval History - April 2026, Volume...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 2026</span></span></span>

Because the ship disappeared with enormous quantities of precious cargo, it quickly entered maritime legend. Estimates of the treasure's modern value vary widely, often reaching many billions of pounds or dollars, but no reliable inventory survives. Cargo manifests were incomplete, private merchants carried undeclared valuables, and many estimates are based on later speculation rather than contemporary accounting. The exact financial value therefore remains uncertain even though the ship unquestionably carried exceptional wealth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history/2026/april/recovery-holy-grail-shipwrecks-begins-amid-decades-long-legal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteRecovery of the ‘Holy Grail of Shipwrecks’ Begins amid Decades-Long Legal Battle &#124; Naval History - April 2026, Volume...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 2026</span></span></span>

For decades, treasure hunters searched for the wreck. A predecessor of the American company Sea Search Armada claimed to have located it in the early 1980s and argued that agreements with Colombia entitled it to a substantial share of any recovery. Colombia rejected that interpretation and maintained that later surveys identified the *San José* at different, confidential coordinates. In December 2015, President Juan Manuel Santos announced that a Colombian-led expedition had identified the wreck using modern underwater technology. Images of distinctive bronze cannon decorated with dolphins helped archaeologists identify the vessel. The precise location has never been publicly released in order to protect the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history/2026/april/recovery-holy-grail-shipwrecks-begins-amid-decades-long-legal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[usni.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteRecovery of the ‘Holy Grail of Shipwrecks’ Begins amid Decades-Long Legal Battle &#124; Naval History - April 2026, Volume...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 2026</span></span></span>

## Why ownership remains so fiercely contested

Unlike many famous shipwrecks, the *San José* sits at the intersection of several competing legal traditions rather than under a single clear rule.

**Colombia's position** is that the wreck lies within Colombian waters and forms part of the nation's submerged cultural heritage. The government has increasingly emphasised archaeological research rather than commercial salvage and declared the wreck and its cultural contents protected national heritage, arguing that historically important objects cannot simply be treated as commodities for sale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://investmentpolicy.unctad.org/investment-dispute-settlement/cases/1342/sea-search-armada-v-colombia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: investmentpolicy.unctad.org">[unctad.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">investmentpolicy.unctad.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCTAD Investment Policy HubSea Search-Armada v. Colombia &#124; Investment Dispute Settlement Navigator &#124; UNCTAD Investment Policy Hub...</span></span></span>

**Spain's position** rests on the argument that the *San José* was a sovereign Spanish naval vessel. Under principles of sovereign immunity recognised in international law, Spain argues that warships remain the property of the flag state even after sinking. Spanish authorities also describe the wreck as an underwater military grave that deserves respectful preservation rather than commercial exploitation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://exteriores.gob.es/RepresentacionesPermanentes/unesco/es/Comunicacion/Noticias/Paginas/Articulos/Gale%C3%B3n-San-Jos%C3%A9-.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: exteriores.gob.es">[Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">exteriores.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs Galeón San José</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs Galeón San José</span></span></span>

**Sea Search Armada's claim** centres on discovery rather than sovereignty. The company argues that its predecessor identified the wreck decades before Colombia's announcement and that earlier agreements entitled it to a significant proportion of recovered treasure. Colombia disputes both the location claimed by the company and the legal consequences of those earlier arrangements. The disagreement has developed into international arbitration before the Permanent Court of Arbitration, where the central questions concern contractual and investment rights rather than simply ownership of the ship itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://investmentpolicy.unctad.org/investment-dispute-settlement/cases/1342/sea-search-armada-v-colombia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: investmentpolicy.unctad.org">[unctad.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">investmentpolicy.unctad.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCTAD Investment Policy HubSea Search-Armada v. Colombia &#124; Investment Dispute Settlement Navigator &#124; UNCTAD Investment Policy Hub...</span></span></span>

The result is an unusually complex dispute in which several legal systems overlap: Colombian constitutional law, international maritime law, investment arbitration and principles governing underwater cultural heritage.

## Colonial wealth, human remains and ethical ownership

The dispute extends far beyond legal ownership because the cargo itself represents the history of European colonial rule in the Americas.

Much of the silver and gold transported aboard Spanish treasure fleets originated in colonial mining systems that relied heavily on forced Indigenous labour and other coercive practices. Representatives of the Qhara Qhara Indigenous nation in present-day Bolivia have argued that wealth extracted from territories around Potosí should not simply be viewed as Spanish or Colombian property. Instead, they see the cargo as a reminder of colonial exploitation whose legacy deserves recognition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history/2026/april/recovery-holy-grail-shipwrecks-begins-amid-decades-long-legal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteRecovery of the ‘Holy Grail of Shipwrecks’ Begins amid Decades-Long Legal Battle &#124; Naval History - April 2026, Volume...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 2026</span></span></span>

The wreck is also a burial site. Hundreds of sailors, soldiers and passengers died when the ship exploded, and human remains may still lie within or around the wreck. This has strengthened arguments that the site should be treated with the same dignity afforded to military graves on land rather than excavated primarily for valuable artefacts. Spain has repeatedly described the *San José* as an underwater grave, while many maritime archaeologists likewise argue that scientific investigation should take precedence over treasure recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://exteriores.gob.es/RepresentacionesPermanentes/unesco/es/Comunicacion/Noticias/Paginas/Articulos/Gale%C3%B3n-San-Jos%C3%A9-.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: exteriores.gob.es">[Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">exteriores.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs Galeón San José</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs Galeón San José</span></span></span>

## Can archaeology and treasure recovery coexist?

Colombia has attempted to present its recent expeditions as scientific research rather than commercial salvage. Officials have stated that initial operations are intended to document the site and recover selected objects for conservation and study, not to sell cargo on the international market. This approach reflects growing international acceptance that historic shipwrecks are archaeological sites rather than sources of marketable treasure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e2ab7f0ff58b0540e801bdc304117058" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The San Jose, known as the &quot;holy grail of shipwrecks,&quot; sank over 300 years ago carrying a bounty from Spanish-controlled colonies. ## Why the *San José* still matters

The *San José* has become one of Colombia's defining historical controversies because it combines several different mysteries into a single site. The ship's identity is well established, yet the true scale of its cargo remains uncertain. Its location has been confirmed, yet kept secret. The law offers competing principles rather than one definitive answer. Meanwhile, the wreck embodies both the wealth of the Spanish Empire and the violence through which much of that wealth was acquired.

For historians, archaeologists and the public, the most important question is no longer how much treasure lies beneath the Caribbean. It is whether one of the world's greatest shipwrecks should be understood primarily as lost wealth, national heritage, a military grave, evidence of colonial exploitation, or an archaeological archive that belongs to humanity as much as to any modern state. Until those competing ideas can be reconciled, the *San José* will remain one of Colombia's most enduring historical disputes.
