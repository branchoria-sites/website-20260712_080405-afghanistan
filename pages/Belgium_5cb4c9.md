## Introduction

These cases matter because they demonstrate four different ways a mystery can survive: the decisive object is missing, the investigation failed, folklore outlived the evidence, or the evidence itself was misleading. Understanding those differences is essential if Belgium’s unresolved history is to be separated from speculation and modern mythmaking.

## Where is the missing Just Judges panel?

The theft of *The Just Judges* from St Bavo’s Cathedral in Ghent is Belgium’s most famous lost-treasure mystery. During the night of 10 April 1934, thieves removed two painted surfaces from the lower left wing of the Ghent Altarpiece, the fifteenth-century masterpiece associated with Hubert and Jan van Eyck. One surface depicted John the Baptist. The other showed the group of mounted figures known as *The Just Judges*. Cathedral staff discovered the loss the following morning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visit.gent.be/en/see-do/ghent-altarpiece" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visit.gent.be">[Visit Gent]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visit.gent.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ghent Altarpiece &#124; Visit GentTwo panels of the Ghent Altarpiece, &#x27;The Just Judges&#x27; and &#x27;John the Baptist&#x27; had been stolen f...</span></span></span>

## Who were the Brabant killers?

Between 1982 and 1985, unidentified attackers carried out a series of armed robberies and shootings in Belgium, particularly in Brabant and neighbouring areas. Twenty-eight people were killed and more than twenty wounded. The gang struck shops, restaurants, a firearms dealer and several Delhaize supermarkets. Its final known attack, at Aalst on 9 November 1985, left eight people dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/article/2024/jun/28/belgiums-crazy-killers-mystery-goes-unsolved-after-police-close-file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">belgiums crazy killers mystery goes unsolved after police close file</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This article is more than 2 years old. Investigation that lasted...Read more...</span></span></span>

Witness accounts led the public and investigators to imagine a core group of three men, commonly described by nicknames referring to a tall participant, a gunman and an older driver. These labels were useful shorthand, not confirmed identities. The wider network may have included additional accomplices, stolen-vehicle suppliers, arms contacts or participants who changed from one raid to another.

The most troubling feature was the apparent mismatch between the violence and the money stolen. The attackers repeatedly endangered themselves by firing on customers, staff and bystanders, sometimes for relatively modest proceeds. Their aggression appeared excessive even by the standards of armed robbery. That imbalance created the enduring question: were these crimes committed for profit, or was robbery partly a disguise for some other purpose?

Several explanations have competed for attention.

**A highly violent criminal gang** remains the simplest possibility. The perpetrators may have used extreme brutality to control witnesses, create panic or satisfy the impulses of one or more members. Criminal behaviour does not always follow a rational balance between risk and financial reward.

**Police or security-service connections** have long been suspected because of the attackers’ apparent confidence with weapons, vehicles and escape routes. Leads involving former officers and people linked to the gendarmerie have been investigated, but no institutional conspiracy has been proved.

**Far-right terrorism or political destabilisation** is the most consequential theory. In this interpretation, the attacks were intended to spread fear and encourage support for stronger state security during the Cold War. The existence of overlapping far-right, criminal and security-service circles in Belgium makes the theory historically plausible enough to investigate. It does not, however, establish that the killings were ordered as a coordinated covert campaign.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.leidensecurityandglobalaffairs.nl/articles/ghosts-from-a-terrorist-past-the-brabant-killers-in-belgium" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leidensecurityandglobalaffairs.nl">[Leiden Security and Global Affairs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leidensecurityandglobalaffairs.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ghosts from a terrorist past the brabant killers in belgium</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ghosts from a terrorist past the brabant killers in belgium</span></span></span>

**Targeted killings hidden within apparently random attacks** form another hypothesis. Particular victims may have been the real objectives while other deaths created confusion. No consistent list of targets or documentary evidence has demonstrated such a plan.

The investigation’s failures became almost as important as the competing theories. Evidence was divided between jurisdictions, police bodies did not always cooperate effectively, and promising leads were delayed, lost or reassessed years later. These problems created fertile ground for suspicion. When an investigation appears disorganised, genuine errors can easily be reinterpreted as deliberate obstruction.

Belgian prosecutors announced in June 2024 that the main investigation would be closed after decades of work failed to identify the perpetrators. The decision did not end every legal avenue. In January 2025, an appeals court in Mons ordered that two additional witnesses be heard, and later reporting described renewed attention to a theory involving two deceased French criminal brothers. As of 2025, however, that lead had not produced a judicially established solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/article/2024/jun/28/belgiums-crazy-killers-mystery-goes-unsolved-after-police-close-file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">belgiums crazy killers mystery goes unsolved after police close file</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This article is more than 2 years old. This distinction matters. The Brabant killings remain unsolved, but the existence of an unsolved case does not make every theory equally credible. It is reasonable to suspect that the group drew on experienced criminal networks and may have included people with police or military knowledge. It is not reasonable to present a state-directed campaign as established fact.

The victims’ families have also resisted treating the case as an abstract national riddle. For them, arguments over hidden political structures sit beside a more immediate failure: twenty-eight people were killed, yet no one was convicted for the campaign. The mystery endures not only because the attackers disappeared, but because the Belgian justice system never produced an explanation capable of restoring confidence.

## Was King Albert I murdered?

King [Albert I]({{ 'albert-i/' | relative_url }}) died on 17 February 1934 after going climbing alone at Marche-les-Dames, near Namur. His body was found beneath the rocks after he failed to return. The accepted explanation was that he had fallen during a solitary climb. No one witnessed the accident, and the absence of a complete account encouraged rumours almost immediately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nieuws.kuleuven.be/en/content/2016/blood-of-king-albert-i-identified-after-80-years" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nieuws.kuleuven.be">[Nieuws]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nieuws.kuleuven.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">blood of king albert i identified after 80 years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">blood of king albert i identified after 80 years</span></span></span>

Albert’s public standing made a mundane accident difficult for some people to accept. He was closely associated with Belgian resistance during the First World War and had acquired an image of courage, duty and personal discipline. His death therefore attracted stories involving assassination, suicide, political secrets or a body moved from another location.

The physical scene also suffered from poor preservation by modern forensic standards. People collected bloodstained leaves and other items as souvenirs, disturbing material that might otherwise have helped reconstruct the fall. This failure left later generations with gaps that conspiracy theories could fill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newhistorian.com/2016/07/24/dna-tests-quash-king-albert-death-conspiracy-theory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newhistorian.com">[New Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newhistorian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">New Historian DNA Tests Quash King Albert I Death Conspiracy Theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New Historian DNA Tests Quash King Albert I Death Conspiracy Theory</span></span></span>

Modern DNA analysis has nevertheless resolved one of the most important disputes. Forensic geneticist Maarten Larmuseau and colleagues examined blood on leaves said to have been collected at Marche-les-Dames. They compared the genetic material with that of two distant relatives of Albert. The study strongly supported the conclusion that the blood belonged to the king.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nieuws.kuleuven.be/en/content/2016/blood-of-king-albert-i-identified-after-80-years" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nieuws.kuleuven.be">[Nieuws]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nieuws.kuleuven.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">blood of king albert i identified after 80 years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">blood of king albert i identified after 80 years</span></span></span>

That finding does not reconstruct the exact mechanics of the fall. It cannot show where Albert placed his hands, whether a rock gave way or what caused him to lose his balance. It does, however, make theories that he died elsewhere and was secretly transported to the site much less credible. The genetic evidence is consistent with the official accident account and directly contradicts many staged-scene narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27470949/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The case is therefore best described as an unwitnessed accident with a partly unrecoverable sequence of events, not an evenly balanced murder mystery. A small uncertainty remains because no observer saw the fall. The broader cause of death, however, is supported far better by historical and forensic evidence than the alternatives.

Albert’s death shows how an unanswered detail can be mistaken for a completely open question. Not knowing precisely how a person fell does not mean that assassination, suicide and accident have equal evidential weight. In this case, the mystery survives mainly through royal folklore and the emotional appeal of a hidden plot.

## How museum glue distorted Neanderthal history

Spy Cave, near Namur, holds a central place in the history of human-origins research. In 1886, excavators Max Lohest, Marcel Depuydt and Julien Fraipont uncovered Neanderthal remains alongside prehistoric tools and animal bones. The work was unusually systematic for its period and became the first Neanderthal discovery produced through planned scientific research and formally reported as such.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturalsciences.be/en/museum/exhibitions-activities/exhibitions/gallery-of-humankind-our-evolution/the-spy-man" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalsciences.be">[Institute of Natural Sciences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalsciences.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalsciences.be.</span></span></span>

More than a century later, radiocarbon tests created a puzzle. Some Belgian Neanderthal bones appeared to be surprisingly recent, suggesting that small populations might have survived in north-western Europe thousands of years later than researchers expected. One Spy specimen had previously produced an age of roughly 28,000 years.

If accurate, those dates would have altered the history of contact between Neanderthals and modern humans. They raised the possibility that Belgium had preserved one of Europe’s final Neanderthal refuges. Yet the results did not fit comfortably with evidence from other regions.

A 2021 study applied a more selective dating method to Neanderthal remains from Spy and other Belgian sites. Researchers isolated hydroxyproline, an amino acid found in bone collagen, before measuring the samples. This reduced the risk that later substances would distort the result. The revised dates indicated that the Belgian Neanderthals disappeared considerably earlier, broadly around 44,000 to 40,000 years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7999949/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The most memorable finding concerned contamination. A Spy shoulder blade that had previously produced a very late date contained substantial modern cattle DNA. The likely explanation was that museum workers had once treated the bone with an animal-based glue made from cattle material. The newer carbon in the glue had made the ancient specimen appear younger than it really was.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.arch.ox.ac.uk/neanderthals-disappeared-north-west-europe-earlier-thought" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arch.ox.ac.uk">[Oxford Architecture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arch.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ox.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The mystery was therefore created after excavation. The Neanderthal had not survived unusually late; the specimen’s conservation history had altered its chemical clock. What once seemed to be a dramatic prehistoric exception became a lesson in how museums themselves can change the evidence they preserve.

The case is not a reason to dismiss all debate about the timing of Neanderthal disappearance. Radiocarbon dating near the limits of the method remains difficult, and different European regions need not have followed exactly the same chronology. A 2024 study of possible Neanderthal and modern-human overlap continued to emphasise the importance of both sample selection and preparation methods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047248423001501" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

What has changed is the specific claim that the Spy remains prove an exceptionally late Belgian survival. The strongest current evidence no longer supports that interpretation. This is a largely solved mystery whose importance lies in the correction: researchers discovered that understanding an ancient bone requires knowing not only where it was excavated, but also what happened to it during more than a century in a collection.

## Which Belgian mysteries are truly unresolved?

These cases occupy different positions on the scale between open mystery and persistent legend.

**The fate of *The Just Judges* is genuinely unknown.** The theft, ransom correspondence and Goedertier connection are well documented, but the panel’s location or destruction has never been established. A future discovery could still resolve the case decisively.

**The Brabant killings remain unresolved at their core.** The identities and motives of the attackers have not been proved. Investigative mistakes and institutional distrust also mean that some potentially valuable evidence may never be recovered or interpreted with confidence.

**Albert I’s death retains only limited uncertainty.** No one can narrate the fall moment by moment, but the accident explanation fits the available evidence far better than murder or staged-body theories.

**The Spy Cave dating anomaly has been substantially explained.** Improved laboratory methods identified contamination and revised the chronology. The broader scientific debate continues, but the supposed Belgian late-survival exception has lost much of its evidential basis.

The distinction is more than a technical matter. A missing answer, an unproved criminal theory, an unwitnessed event and a corrected scientific error should not all be described in the same way. Doing so makes weak speculation appear equivalent to documented uncertainty.

Belgium’s mystery history is most revealing when the limits of knowledge are stated precisely. The missing panel demonstrates how one absent object can sustain a century of searching. The Brabant killings show how investigative failure can deepen rather than reduce uncertainty. Albert I’s death illustrates the durability of conspiracy after stronger evidence emerges. Spy Cave shows that science can solve a puzzle by questioning the history of the evidence itself.

## Why these cases remain part of Belgian memory

Each mystery is attached to an institution or symbol of unusual national importance. The Ghent Altarpiece is one of Belgium’s greatest artistic treasures. The Brabant killings challenged confidence in policing, justice and the state. Albert I occupies a powerful place in the country’s memory of the First World War. The Spy fossils helped establish Belgium’s importance in the early scientific study of Neanderthals.

They are also tied to physical places that keep the stories visible. Visitors can see the replacement panel in Ghent, visit the rocks at Marche-les-Dames or encounter the Spy Neanderthals in Belgium’s natural-history collections. The Brabant attacks remain associated with ordinary commercial sites where families and employees were killed. These locations prevent the cases from becoming purely archival.

Public memory, however, tends to flatten the difference between uncertainty and conspiracy. A missing painting invites endlessly specific hiding places. An unsolved gang encourages theories involving entire institutions. An unwitnessed royal death becomes assassination. A misleading scientific date becomes evidence of a lost prehistoric population.

The most responsible reading is less dramatic but more interesting. Belgium’s enduring mysteries show that the past can remain uncertain for very different reasons: an object may vanish, investigators may fail, legends may resist forensic correction, or old conservation methods may corrupt a scientific result. The challenge is not merely to choose the most exciting explanation, but to identify what the evidence can still support—and what it cannot.
