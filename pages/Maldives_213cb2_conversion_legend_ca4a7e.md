## Introduction

The [Maldives]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-the-maldives/' | relative_url }}) really did convert from Buddhism to Islam during the twelfth century. What remains debated is not whether the conversion happened, but how it happened and who deserves the credit. Over the centuries, a genuine political and religious transformation became intertwined with one of the archipelago's best-known legends: the story of a terrifying sea demon that demanded the monthly sacrifice of a young woman until a visiting Muslim holy man defeated it through recitation of the Qur'an. At the same time, historians have long disagreed over the identity of that missionary, with different traditions naming different men from different parts of the Islamic world. These overlapping stories make the conversion one of the Maldives' most enduring historical mysteries, not because the event itself is doubtful, but because its remembered origins blend history, folklore and competing claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/2/201" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Religion, Power, and National Identity: The Dual Role of Islam in the History and Modernization of the MaldivesFebruary 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 8, 2025</span></span></span>

## What is known about the royal conversion

The broad outline of events is well established. During the twelfth century, the Buddhist kingdom of the Maldives adopted Islam under King Dhovemi, who took the Muslim title Sultan Muhammad al-Adil. This marked the beginning of the Islamic sultanate that would shape Maldivian political and religious life for centuries. The exact year is less certain: many modern accounts use 1153, while some later documentary traditions point to 1193, illustrating how even basic chronology remains open to debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/2/201" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Religion, Power, and National Identity: The Dual Role of Islam in the History and Modernization of the MaldivesFebruary 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 8, 2025</span></span></span>

The conversion was almost certainly more complex than a single dramatic moment. The Maldives already sat on busy Indian Ocean trade routes linking South Asia, Arabia, East Africa and the Persian Gulf. Muslim merchants had been visiting the islands for generations, and some islanders may already have practised Islam before the royal court officially adopted the new faith. Rather than an isolated miracle, many historians see the conversion as the culmination of long commercial, diplomatic and cultural contact with the wider Islamic world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/2/201" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Religion, Power, and National Identity: The Dual Role of Islam in the History and Modernization of the MaldivesFebruary 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 8, 2025</span></span></span>

The mystery begins because the earliest detailed narrative survives only long after the event itself. The famous traveller Ibn Battuta visited the Maldives in the fourteenth century—roughly two centuries after the conversion—and recorded the story as local people told it to him rather than as an eyewitness account. His version became enormously influential but cannot be treated as a contemporary record of twelfth-century events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Maldives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of the Maldives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of the Maldives</span></span></span>

## The competing identities of the missionary

One of the most persistent historical disputes concerns the identity of the man who converted the king.

Ibn Battuta identified him as **Abu al-Barakat Yusuf al-Barbari**, a Muslim scholar from the Maghreb in North Africa. According to his account, the missionary defeated the supernatural threat facing the islands, persuaded the ruler to embrace Islam, and became permanently honoured in Maldivian memory. The continued importance of his shrine in Malé helped reinforce this version of events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Maldives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of the Maldives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of the Maldives</span></span></span>

Later Maldivian traditions, however, often identify the missionary differently. Rather than a North African, they describe him as **Yusuf Shams al-Din al-Tabrizi**, linking him to Tabriz in present-day Iran. This version appears in several local historical traditions and has led many scholars to question whether Ibn Battuta misunderstood an inscription, relied on an already altered oral tradition, or simply preserved one version among several circulating in the medieval Indian Ocean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Battuta" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ibn Battuta</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ibn Battuta</span></span></span>

Some modern researchers have proposed further possibilities. Because "al-Barbari" could also refer to the East African port of Berbera rather than a Berber from Morocco, another theory places the missionary in the Horn of Africa instead. Given the close trading links between the Maldives and East Africa, this interpretation is geographically plausible, although it remains unproven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://edition.mv/international_space_station_/29635" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: edition.mv">[The Edition]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">edition.mv</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Edition Maldives – And the journey towards an Islamic nation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Edition Maldives – And the journey towards an Islamic nation</span></span></span>

None of these theories has achieved universal acceptance because the surviving evidence is limited:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* **The North African tradition** relies mainly on Ibn Battuta's influential account.
* **The Tabrizi tradition** draws strength from later Maldivian historical writings and local religious memory.
* **The East African interpretation** attempts to reconcile linguistic and trading connections but lacks decisive documentary proof.

</div>

The available sources are simply too late and too contradictory to identify the missionary with complete confidence. That uncertainty is the real historical puzzle.

## How the sea-demon story entered public memory

The legendary element centres on **Rannamaari**, a fearsome being said to emerge from the sea once each month.

According to the best-known version, the people believed the creature demanded the sacrifice of a virgin. Lots were drawn among families, and the chosen girl was left overnight in a seaside shrine. When the visiting Muslim scholar volunteered to take her place, he spent the night reciting the Qur'an. The demon failed to appear, convincing the king that Islam possessed greater spiritual power than the old beliefs. The ruler converted, the idols were destroyed, and the population followed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Maldives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of the Maldives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of the Maldives</span></span></span>

Modern historians generally interpret this account symbolically rather than literally. The sea demon may represent the defeat of pre-Islamic religious practices, while the monthly sacrifice dramatises the moral contrast between the old religion and the new faith. Such miracle stories are common in medieval conversion narratives across many cultures, where they emphasise divine authority rather than provide straightforward historical reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/2/201" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Religion, Power, and National Identity: The Dual Role of Islam in the History and Modernization of the MaldivesFebruary 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 8, 2025</span></span></span>

The legend nevertheless became deeply embedded in Maldivian cultural memory because it gave the conversion a memorable narrative structure. Instead of remembering a gradual political and religious transition influenced by trade and diplomacy, later generations inherited a story in which supernatural danger, personal courage and miraculous intervention explained one of the country's defining historical moments.

## Questions left unresolved by Conversion Legend
The conversion legend remains important because it sits at the intersection of history, religion and national identity.

For historians, the central questions are practical ones: which medieval sources deserve the most confidence, how oral traditions changed over time, and what archaeological or documentary evidence can actually establish. The disagreement over the missionary's identity illustrates the difficulties of reconstructing early Maldivian history, where relatively few contemporary local records survive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/2/201" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Religion, Power, and National Identity: The Dual Role of Islam in the History and Modernization of the MaldivesFebruary 8, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 8, 2025</span></span></span>

For many Maldivians, however, the legend carries cultural significance beyond its literal accuracy. Whether the missionary came from Morocco, Tabriz or elsewhere, the story explains the beginning of the Islamic era that has shaped the country's institutions and identity for more than eight centuries. The sea-demon narrative survives not because historians consider it factual, but because it expresses the transformation in dramatic and memorable form.

The enduring mystery, therefore, is not whether the Maldives became Islamic—it unquestionably did—but how a real historical conversion became fused with competing traditions, miracle stories and regional claims that continue to be debated today.
