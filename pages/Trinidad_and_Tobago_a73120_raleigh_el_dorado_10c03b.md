## Introduction

In 1595, Trinidad became the launching point for one of the most influential failed quests in early modern history. Sir Walter Raleigh seized the Spanish settlement of San José de Oruña before travelling towards the Orinoco River in search of the legendary golden city of El Dorado. Although he never found the immense wealth he promised, his expedition transformed scattered rumours, Indigenous accounts, Spanish reports and imperial ambitions into one of history's most enduring myths. The mystery is not whether Raleigh searched for El Dorado—he certainly did—but how uncertain information became accepted by many Europeans as persuasive evidence for a vast hidden empire. Trinidad's role was crucial because it served as both the military gateway to Guiana and the place where Raleigh gathered intelligence that shaped the legend for generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/walter-raleighs-quest-for-el-dorado.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Live Science Walter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceWalter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live ScienceMay 11, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 11, 2020</span></span></span>

## Why did Raleigh attack Trinidad?

By the mid-1590s, Spain had claimed much of northern South America, yet the interior remained largely unknown to Europeans. Spanish governor Antonio de Berrío had spent years searching unsuccessfully for El Dorado, convinced that immense riches lay beyond the Orinoco River. Raleigh believed that capturing Berrío and accessing Spanish intelligence would give England an opportunity to challenge Spanish dominance while discovering a source of wealth that could rival Peru.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/walter-raleighs-quest-for-el-dorado.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Live Science Walter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceWalter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live ScienceMay 11, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 11, 2020</span></span></span>

### Raleigh's attack on San José de Oruña

Raleigh arrived off Trinidad in March 1595. After initially presenting himself peacefully, he launched a surprise assault on the island's capital, San José de Oruña (today's St Joseph), on 7 April. The settlement was captured and much of it burned. Governor Berrío was taken prisoner and questioned extensively about Guiana and the supposed kingdom of gold beyond the Orinoco.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/walter-raleighs-quest-for-el-dorado.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Live Science Walter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceWalter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live ScienceMay 11, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 11, 2020</span></span></span>

This attack was not simply a raid. It had three connected objectives:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Remove the nearest Spanish military threat before entering the Orinoco.
* Obtain maps, reports and local knowledge from Berrío.
* Position England as a competitor for any future conquest of Guiana.

</div>

For Trinidad, the event marked one of the earliest major English military actions on the island, linking its history directly with European imperial rivalry rather than merely Caribbean exploration.

## How reports of gold changed into the city of Manoa

The greatest mystery surrounding Raleigh's campaign is not the journey itself but the evolution of its central claim. Earlier Spanish explorers had already circulated stories about wealthy Indigenous rulers somewhere inland, but these stories varied enormously in location and detail. Some referred to ritual gold offerings by chiefs; others described prosperous settlements; still others mentioned a great inland lake.

Raleigh combined these fragments into a far more coherent and persuasive narrative. In his 1596 book *The Discoverie of the Large, Rich and Bewtiful Empire of Guiana*, he described an immense empire centred on the golden city of Manoa, supposedly situated beside the vast Lake Parime. Although presented with considerable confidence, much of this description rested on second-hand testimony rather than direct observation. Raleigh never reached the area where he claimed Manoa existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eada.lib.umd.edu/text-entries/discovery-of-the-large-rich-and-beautiful-empire-of-guiana-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eada.lib.umd.edu">[Early Americas Digital Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eada.lib.umd.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umd.edu.</span></span></span>

Rather than inventing everything outright, Raleigh blended several elements:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Spanish reports collected from Berrío and other settlers.
* Accounts from Indigenous intermediaries whose statements may have referred to different places or political centres.
* Existing European expectations that another wealthy civilisation, comparable to the Aztec or Inca empires, remained undiscovered.
* His own political need to persuade Queen Elizabeth I and English investors that further expeditions deserved funding.

</div>

The resulting narrative became far more influential than the uncertain evidence on which it rested.

## Why Lake Parime became one of history's great cartographic mysteries

One striking feature of Raleigh's account was the supposed Lake Parime, beside which Manoa allegedly stood. For centuries, European mapmakers included this enormous lake on maps of northern South America despite the absence of reliable confirmation.

Modern historians generally conclude that no such lake existed in the form shown on these maps. Some researchers suggest the legend may have grown from seasonal flooding across the Rupununi savannahs or misunderstandings of interconnected river systems that temporarily created vast expanses of water during the rainy season. Others argue that repeated copying by European cartographers gradually transformed speculation into apparent geographical fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Parime" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lake Parime</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lake Parime</span></span></span>

This illustrates an important historical puzzle: maps often appear authoritative, yet in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries they frequently reproduced respected guesses alongside genuine observation. Lake Parime became less an observed place than a geographical tradition.

## Why imperial ambition kept the legend alive

The persistence of El Dorado owed as much to politics as to geography. Raleigh's account appeared during the Anglo-Spanish War, when England sought opportunities to weaken Spanish power overseas. A rich inland empire offered an attractive justification for further military and colonial ventures.

Raleigh's published narrative therefore served several purposes simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* It defended the cost and risks of his expedition.
* It promoted England's claim to Guiana.
* It encouraged future investment.
* It portrayed Spain as having failed to exploit extraordinary wealth already within reach.

</div>

Even after repeated expeditions failed to locate Manoa, belief proved remarkably resilient. Successive explorers often concluded not that the city did not exist, but that they had searched the wrong river or season.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eada.lib.umd.edu/text-entries/discovery-of-the-large-rich-and-beautiful-empire-of-guiana-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eada.lib.umd.edu">[Early Americas Digital Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eada.lib.umd.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umd.edu.</span></span></span>

## What is firmly established, and what remains uncertain?

Modern scholarship draws a much clearer distinction between documented events and legendary claims.

Well established facts include:

* Raleigh reached Trinidad in March 1595.
* He captured San José de Oruña and imprisoned Governor Antonio de Berrío.
* He travelled up the Orinoco River seeking evidence of El Dorado.
* He published an influential account promoting the existence of Manoa and its immense wealth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/walter-raleighs-quest-for-el-dorado.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Live Science Walter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceWalter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live ScienceMay 11, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 11, 2020</span></span></span>

The unresolved questions concern interpretation rather than basic chronology.

Historians continue to debate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* How much Raleigh genuinely believed his own descriptions.
* Whether Indigenous reports were misunderstood, selectively quoted or deliberately exaggerated.
* How much information came directly from Berrío versus other informants.
* To what extent Raleigh consciously shaped his narrative as political propaganda for the English Crown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Discovery_of_Guiana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Discovery of Guiana</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Discovery of Guiana</span></span></span>

</div>

Few modern historians accept the existence of Manoa as Raleigh described it. Instead, the enduring mystery lies in how fragments of real geography, genuine Indigenous knowledge and imperial ambition combined to create one of Europe's most powerful geographical myths.

## Why Trinidad's role still matters

Within [Trinidad and Tobago]({{ 'which-trinidad-and-tobago-mysteries/' | relative_url }})'s history, Raleigh's campaign represents far more than a brief military episode. The island became the place where violence, intelligence gathering and storytelling converged to reshape European understanding of northern South America. Without the seizure of San José de Oruña and Raleigh's conversations with Berrío and other informants, the English version of El Dorado might never have gained such prominence.

The lasting significance is therefore not the search for a city of gold itself, but the demonstration of how exploration narratives could influence maps, diplomacy, colonial strategy and public imagination for centuries. Trinidad served as the gateway through which an uncertain collection of rumours entered European history as a legend that outlived the expedition that inspired it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/walter-raleighs-quest-for-el-dorado.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[livescience.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Live Science Walter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceWalter Raleigh&#x27;s bloody quest for El Dorado &#124; Live ScienceMay 11, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 11, 2020</span></span></span>
