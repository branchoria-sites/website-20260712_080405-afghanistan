## Introduction

On 2 July 1937, Amelia Earhart and her navigator, Fred Noonan, departed Lae in what was then the Australian-administered Territory of New Guinea for Howland Island, a tiny coral island more than 4,000 kilometres away. It was the longest and most demanding overwater stage of Earhart's attempt to fly around the world. They never reached their destination. Lae therefore marks the final point at which their journey is fully documented through witnesses, maintenance records, weather reports and flight preparations. Everything after take-off is reconstructed from intermittent radio transmissions, navigation estimates and unsuccessful searches. That clear dividing line is why [Papua New Guinea]({{ 'what-remains-unsolved-in-papua-new/' | relative_url }}) occupies such an important place in one of aviation's greatest unsolved mysteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/amelia-earhart/nasm_A19500108000" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span></span></span>

## The documented departure from Lae

Earhart and Noonan arrived in Lae after successfully completing thousands of kilometres of flying across Asia and the Dutch East Indies. Their Lockheed Electra had already survived an earlier accident in Hawaii during a previous attempt at the world flight, and by the time they reached New Guinea they had travelled roughly 35,000 kilometres around the globe. The remaining Pacific crossings were considered the greatest challenge of the expedition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/amelia-earhart/nasm_A19500108000" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span></span></span>

The stop at Lae was well recorded. Local officials, mechanics, journalists and residents observed the aircraft being serviced and fuelled. Weather information was gathered, the aircraft's load was reviewed, and Earhart prepared for an estimated journey of about 20 hours over open ocean. Contemporary accounts describe the Electra departing Lae's airfield shortly after 10 a.m. local time on 2 July 1937, climbing eastwards before disappearing from view. It remains the last confirmed sighting of both the aircraft and its occupants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://text-message.blogs.archives.gov/2017/07/12/the-japanese-governments-offer-of-assistance-to-help-find-amelia-earhart-july-1937/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: text-message.blogs.archives.gov">[The Text Message]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">text-message.blogs.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Text MessageThe Japanese Government’s Offer of Assistance to Help Find Amelia Earhart, July 1937 – The Text MessageJuly 12, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: july 1937</span></span></span>

Because the preparations at Lae are so well documented, historians have been able to reconstruct the final known condition of the aircraft with unusual confidence. There is no reliable evidence that the Electra suffered a catastrophic mechanical failure before departure, although historians continue to debate whether its heavy fuel load, communications equipment and operational limitations contributed to the difficulties that followed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/amelia-earhart/nasm_A19500108000" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span></span></span>

## What happened after the aircraft left Lae?

After leaving New Guinea, Earhart and Noonan headed across one of the most isolated stretches of the Pacific. Their destination, Howland Island, measured only a few kilometres in length and rose only a few metres above sea level, making it exceptionally difficult to locate even with accurate navigation.

Noonan was among the world's most experienced aerial navigators, relying on celestial observations, dead reckoning and estimated wind conditions. However, cloud cover, uncertain winds and the inability to make visual fixes could steadily increase navigational error over such a long flight. Even a small mistake early in the journey could result in missing Howland Island by many kilometres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/will-search-for-amelia-earhart-ever-end-180953646/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Will the Search for Amelia Earhart Ever End?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Will the Search for Amelia Earhart Ever End?</span></span></span>

The flight also depended heavily on successful radio communication with the US Coast Guard cutter *Itasca*, stationed near Howland Island to provide navigation assistance. That plan proved far less effective than expected.

## Radio messages and the search for Howland

The surviving radio log from *Itasca* provides the most important evidence for reconstructing the final hours of the flight. Earhart transmitted several position reports as she approached the island, but communication was largely one-way. The crew aboard *Itasca* could hear Earhart clearly on several occasions, while there is strong evidence that she could not hear many of their replies because of incompatible radio procedures, equipment limitations and frequency misunderstandings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/amelia-earhart-missing-80-years-not-forgotten" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

Among the best-known transmissions were:

* Earhart reported that she believed the aircraft was close to Howland but could not see either the island or *Itasca*.
* She warned that fuel was running low.
* She requested radio bearings that never reached her effectively.
* Her final acknowledged transmission stated that the aircraft was flying along the "line 157–337" (often written as 157–337 or 156–336 in different documents), indicating she and Noonan were searching along a navigational line of position running northwest to southeast.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://text-message.blogs.archives.gov/2017/07/12/the-japanese-governments-offer-of-assistance-to-help-find-amelia-earhart-july-1937/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: text-message.blogs.archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">text-message.blogs.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Text MessageThe Japanese Government’s Offer of Assistance to Help Find Amelia Earhart, July 1937 – The Text MessageJuly 12, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: july 1937</span></span></span>

Exactly what Earhart intended by the final message remains debated. Historians generally agree that it referred to a navigational line rather than a precise location, but because she did not indicate which direction along that line she intended to fly, the message left rescuers with considerable uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/air-space-magazine/an-american-obsession-18677828/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Amelia Earhart, American Idol</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Amelia Earhart, American Idol</span></span></span>

When no further transmissions were received, *Itasca* immediately began searching. The United States Navy and Coast Guard mounted one of the largest air-and-sea searches of its time, covering vast areas around Howland Island. Despite extensive efforts lasting more than two weeks, no confirmed wreckage, oil slick or survivors were found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://text-message.blogs.archives.gov/2017/07/12/the-japanese-governments-offer-of-assistance-to-help-find-amelia-earhart-july-1937/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: text-message.blogs.archives.gov">[The Text Message]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">text-message.blogs.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Text MessageThe Japanese Government’s Offer of Assistance to Help Find Amelia Earhart, July 1937 – The Text MessageJuly 12, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: july 1937</span></span></span>

## Crash theory, alternative claims and missing evidence

The explanation supported by most aviation historians and by the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum is also the simplest: Earhart and Noonan failed to locate Howland Island, exhausted their fuel supply and ditched in the Pacific Ocean, where the aircraft later sank in extremely deep water. This interpretation fits the known radio messages, the increasing signal strength received by *Itasca* as the aircraft approached the island and the absence of confirmed evidence that the Electra reached another destination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/amelia-earhart/nasm_A19500108000" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span></span></span>

Alternative theories continue to attract public interest, but each faces significant evidential problems.

**Landing on Nikumaroro.** Some researchers argue the aircraft reached Nikumaroro, roughly 650 kilometres southeast of Howland, where Earhart and Noonan may have survived for a time. Expeditions have recovered artefacts and analysed possible radio reports after the disappearance, but no object has been conclusively identified as coming from Earhart's Electra, and no human remains have settled the question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://people.com/new-declassified-records-of-amelia-earhart-disappearance-have-been-released-11851059" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: people.com">[People.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">people.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Additional archival material includes accounts suggesting she may have sent distress signals post-crash. **Japanese capture.** Another long-standing claim suggests the pair landed in territory controlled by Japan and were captured. Although the idea has appeared repeatedly in books, documentaries and popular culture, historians have found no contemporary military records or verified physical evidence demonstrating that such an event occurred. ## Why Lae remains central to the mystery

For Papua New Guinea, the significance of Lae lies not in speculation but in documentation. The events before departure are unusually well recorded: the aircraft's preparations, the witnesses, the route planning and the moment of take-off are all supported by contemporary evidence. Once the Electra disappeared beyond the horizon, the historical record became progressively thinner, relying almost entirely on radio logs and later interpretation.

That sharp transition explains why Lae continues to feature in investigations nearly ninety years later. It represents the last point where historians can say with confidence what happened. Every competing explanation for Earhart's disappearance begins with the same uncontested fact: the Lockheed Electra left Lae on the morning of 2 July 1937 and was never seen again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/amelia-earhart/nasm_A19500108000" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum Amelia Earhart &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span></span></span>
