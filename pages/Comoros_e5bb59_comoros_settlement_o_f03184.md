## Introduction

The mystery of who first settled the [Comoros]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-comoross/' | relative_url }}) Islands is not solved by a single dramatic discovery but by the gradual replacement of an older foundation legend with a far more complex picture. For centuries, many elite families traced their origins to noble settlers from Persia, especially the city of Shiraz, presenting the islands as the creation of prestigious foreign founders. Modern archaeology, linguistics and genetics tell a different story. Rather than pointing to one founding migration, they reveal that the Comoros emerged as a meeting place where African, Austronesian, Middle Eastern and later Persian influences blended over several centuries. The real historical puzzle is not whether Persian influence existed—it clearly did—but why stories of Persian founders became so influential when the islands' earliest settlement appears to have been far more diverse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/36647/chapter-abstract/321648607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicFrom the Origins: Archaeology and Traditions &#124; Islands in a Cosmopolitan Sea: A History of the Comoros &#124; Oxford AcademicSepte...</span></span></span>

## What archaeology suggests about early settlement

Archaeological research increasingly places the Comoros within the wider story of the western Indian Ocean rather than treating the islands as an isolated civilisation. Excavations show that permanent settlement was underway by the eighth century AD, with sites on Mayotte and the other islands demonstrating established communities engaged in farming, fishing and long-distance trade. Researchers describe this as the **Dembeni phase**, named after an important archaeological site on Mayotte that became one of the wealthiest trading centres in the region.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoros" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The finds from Dembeni are particularly important because they challenge simple founder stories. Archaeologists have uncovered:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Chinese ceramics.
* Persian and Gulf pottery.
* Glassware from across the Islamic world.
* Evidence of trade in Madagascan rock crystal.

</div>

These discoveries show that by the ninth to twelfth centuries the islands were already deeply integrated into Indian Ocean commerce. Imported goods demonstrate active connections with Persia and the wider Islamic world, but they do not prove that Persians established the first settlements. Instead, they indicate that thriving island communities were participating in extensive maritime exchange.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholars.aku.edu/en/publications/islamic-archaeology-in-the-comoros-the-swahili-and-the-rock-cryst-3/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholars.aku.edu">[The Aga Khan University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholars.aku.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aku.edu.</span></span></span>

The archaeological record also remains incomplete. Large areas of the archipelago have never been systematically excavated, meaning there are still unanswered questions about exactly when each island was first occupied and whether earlier settlements remain undiscovered beneath later towns. That genuine uncertainty helps explain why debates over origins continue.

## African, Asian and Middle Eastern ancestry

One of the strongest challenges to the traditional Persian foundation narrative comes from combining archaeology with genetics and historical linguistics.

Current evidence suggests the population developed through several overlapping migrations rather than one founding event. The main contributors include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **East African settlers**, speaking Bantu languages that eventually developed into today's Comorian languages.
* **Austronesian voyagers**, whose wider expansion across the Indian Ocean also shaped Madagascar.
* **People from the Arabian Peninsula**, who helped spread Islam and expanded commercial links.
* **Merchants and migrants from the Persian Gulf**, who became part of established coastal trading societies rather than replacing them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/36647/chapter-abstract/321648607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicFrom the Origins: Archaeology and Traditions &#124; Islands in a Cosmopolitan Sea: A History of the Comoros &#124; Oxford AcademicSepte...</span></span></span>

</div>

Genetic studies reinforce this mixed picture. They identify African, Austronesian and Middle Eastern ancestry within modern Comorian populations, matching what archaeology already suggested. The islands appear to have served as a bridge between mainland Africa and Madagascar while also participating in trade routes extending to Arabia, Persia and South Asia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/36647/chapter-abstract/321648607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicFrom the Origins: Archaeology and Traditions &#124; Islands in a Cosmopolitan Sea: A History of the Comoros &#124; Oxford AcademicSepte...</span></span></span>

Language tells a similar story. Modern Comorian languages belong to the Bantu branch of the Niger–Congo family and are closely related to Swahili, showing that African linguistic foundations remained dominant even as Arabic vocabulary and Islamic culture spread through trade and religion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoros" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Why Persian foundation legends became so influential

The persistence of Persian foundation stories is one of the most intriguing historical questions surrounding the Comoros.

Across the Swahili coast, many ruling families claimed descent from immigrants from Shiraz in Persia. These **Shirazi traditions** appeared not only in the Comoros but also in places such as Kilwa and Zanzibar. Modern historians generally see these traditions as containing some historical memory of Persian migrants while also serving political purposes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/36647/chapter-abstract/321648607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicFrom the Origins: Archaeology and Traditions &#124; Islands in a Cosmopolitan Sea: A History of the Comoros &#124; Oxford AcademicSepte...</span></span></span>

Claiming Persian ancestry offered several advantages:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* It connected local rulers to the prestigious Islamic world.
* It strengthened political legitimacy.
* It distinguished elite families from rival groups.
* It linked island societies to respected commercial networks across the Indian Ocean.

</div>

Rather than inventing Persian connections from nothing, these traditions probably magnified the importance of genuine later migrations and trading relationships. Persian merchants certainly visited and settled parts of the western Indian Ocean, but modern scholarship finds little evidence that they founded previously uninhabited islands or displaced an earlier population wholesale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/36647/chapter-abstract/321648607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicFrom the Origins: Archaeology and Traditions &#124; Islands in a Cosmopolitan Sea: A History of the Comoros &#124; Oxford AcademicSepte...</span></span></span>

This pattern resembles developments elsewhere on the Swahili coast, where archaeology often demonstrates strong local African continuity beneath elite genealogies emphasising foreign origins.

## Why archaeology and oral tradition sometimes disagree

The apparent contradiction between scientific evidence and oral tradition is less dramatic than it first appears because they answer different historical questions.

Archaeology investigates who first occupied the islands and how communities developed over time. Oral traditions often explain who established recognised dynasties, introduced Islam or created legitimate political authority.

A dynasty that genuinely descended from Persian migrants could become remembered as the "founders" of a kingdom even though earlier farming and fishing communities had lived on the islands for centuries. This distinction helps reconcile the archaeological evidence with long-standing local traditions instead of treating one as entirely false.

Historians therefore increasingly interpret the Persian foundation stories as memories of an important political and cultural transformation rather than literal accounts of the islands' first human settlement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/36647/chapter-abstract/321648607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicFrom the Origins: Archaeology and Traditions &#124; Islands in a Cosmopolitan Sea: A History of the Comoros &#124; Oxford AcademicSepte...</span></span></span>

## What remains uncertain

Although the broad outline has become much clearer, several important questions remain open.

The precise timing of the earliest settlement is still debated because archaeological evidence is uneven across the islands. Some researchers suggest earlier occupation than the currently accepted eighth-century evidence, but definitive proof remains limited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoros" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Scholars also continue to investigate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Whether the first settlers reached the islands directly from East Africa, Madagascar or Southeast Asia.
* How quickly different migration streams mixed.
* The exact scale of migration from Persia compared with Arabia.
* How accurately later oral traditions preserve memories of genuine medieval events.

</div>

As new excavations, radiocarbon dating and ancient DNA techniques become available, these questions may become easier to answer.

## Why the debate still matters

The settlement history of the Comoros is significant because it reshapes how the islands fit into Indian Ocean history. Instead of being portrayed as a remote outpost founded by outsiders, the archipelago now appears as an active crossroads where African communities, Austronesian seafarers, Arab merchants and Persian traders all contributed to a distinctive island society.

The enduring mystery lies not in whether one civilisation founded the Comoros, but in understanding how centuries of migration, trade and political storytelling became condensed into elegant foundation legends. Modern evidence suggests that the Persian stories preserve part of the islands' history, but only one chapter in a much longer and more multicultural story of settlement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/36647/chapter-abstract/321648607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[oup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicFrom the Origins: Archaeology and Traditions &#124; Islands in a Cosmopolitan Sea: A History of the Comoros &#124; Oxford AcademicSepte...</span></span></span>
