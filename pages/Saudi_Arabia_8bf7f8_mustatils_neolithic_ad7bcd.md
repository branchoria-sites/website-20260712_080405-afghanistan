## Introduction

Thousands of long, low stone rectangles scattered across north-western [Saudi Arabia]({{ 'saudi-arabia/' | relative_url }}) are among the most remarkable prehistoric monuments ever discovered on the Arabian Peninsula. Known as mustatils, these structures were built around 7,000 years ago, making them older than both Stonehenge and the Egyptian pyramids. Archaeologists now broadly agree that they were ceremonial monuments rather than homes, animal pens or defensive works. Yet the central mystery remains: why did Neolithic herders invest enormous effort in building more than 1,600 monuments across such a vast landscape, and what beliefs inspired the rituals performed within them? Recent excavations have transformed understanding of their purpose, but they have also revealed how much remains unknown about the religious ideas, social organisation and seasonal gatherings of Arabia's early pastoral communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-western Arabia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Co...</span></span></span>

## What excavations reveal about their purpose

For decades, mustatils puzzled archaeologists. Their walls are usually too low to confine livestock, their interiors too narrow for habitation, and they lack the features expected in settlements or forts. Early aerial surveys could identify their unusual rectangular plans but offered little clue to their function.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-western Arabia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Co...</span></span></span>

The breakthrough came through excavation of an exceptionally well-preserved mustatil east of AlUla. Inside the monument's "head" chamber, archaeologists uncovered carefully arranged deposits of animal horns and skull fragments placed around upright standing stones. The assemblage was dominated by cattle but also included sheep, goats and gazelles. Radiocarbon dating placed the monument in the late sixth millennium BC, firmly within the Arabian Late Neolithic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0281904" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cult, herding, and ‘pilgrimage’ in the Late Neolithic of north-west Arabia: Excavations at a mustatil east of AlUla &#124; PLOS One...</span></span></span>

The pattern of the finds was crucial. The animal remains were deliberately deposited rather than discarded as ordinary food waste, and there was no evidence that people lived inside the monument. Instead, researchers concluded that these were ritual offerings associated with repeated ceremonial visits over time. The excavated site also contained traces of burning and several phases of deposition, suggesting that the monument remained important across multiple generations rather than serving as a single-use structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0281904" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cult, herding, and ‘pilgrimage’ in the Late Neolithic of north-west Arabia: Excavations at a mustatil east of AlUla &#124; PLOS One...</span></span></span>

This evidence has largely resolved one longstanding question: mustatils were ceremonial monuments. The unresolved question is what those ceremonies actually meant.

## Animal offerings, standing stones and communal gatherings

The discoveries point towards rituals centred on valuable animals rather than everyday economic activity. Cattle were particularly significant because they represented wealth, survival and social status in Neolithic pastoral societies. Sacrificing or dedicating cattle would therefore have been an expensive and symbolically powerful act rather than an ordinary meal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-western Arabia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Co...</span></span></span>

Equally intriguing are the upright stones found within some excavated monuments. Researchers interpret these as sacred standing stones that may have acted as focal points for ritual activity. Animal remains were concentrated around them, indicating that offerings were directed towards these stones rather than scattered randomly throughout the enclosure. Although later Arabian religions also used sacred stones, archaeologists emphasise that there is no evidence for direct continuity across thousands of years. Similar forms do not necessarily represent the same beliefs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0281904" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cult, herding, and ‘pilgrimage’ in the Late Neolithic of north-west Arabia: Excavations at a mustatil east of AlUla &#124; PLOS One...</span></span></span>

Researchers have cautiously suggested that some mustatils may have attracted groups travelling from different grazing areas. The idea has sometimes been described as a form of "pilgrimage", not in the sense of organised historical religions, but as repeated journeys by dispersed pastoral communities to shared ceremonial places. The monuments may have provided opportunities to renew social ties, exchange information, arrange marriages, settle disputes or reinforce collective identities alongside religious ceremonies. The archaeological evidence supports repeated communal gatherings, but not the detailed motivations behind them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0281904" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cult, herding, and ‘pilgrimage’ in the Late Neolithic of north-west Arabia: Excavations at a mustatil east of AlUla &#124; PLOS One...</span></span></span>

## What their scale suggests about early Arabian society

One of the greatest mysteries lies not in individual monuments but in the landscape they create. More than 1,600 mustatils have been identified across north-western Saudi Arabia, stretching across an immense area. Their broad architectural similarities imply that different communities shared common ideas over considerable distances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-western Arabia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Co...</span></span></span>

Constructing these monuments required coordinated labour. Even though the walls are relatively low, many mustatils extend for well over 100 metres, with some approaching 600 metres in length. Building them demanded planning, access to stone, cooperation and enough social organisation to mobilise work without evidence of cities, kings or permanent bureaucracies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-western Arabia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Co...</span></span></span>

This challenges older assumptions that monumental architecture only emerged after large agricultural states developed. Instead, Arabia's Neolithic herders appear capable of organising ambitious communal construction projects while remaining largely mobile pastoralists. Their monuments suggest that complex social cooperation did not always depend upon urban civilisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-western Arabia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Co...</span></span></span>

The monuments also belong to a period when north-west Arabia experienced more favourable climatic conditions than today. Seasonal grasslands and lakes supported larger herds, making wider social networks both possible and valuable. As the climate later became drier, many aspects of this ceremonial landscape disappeared, leaving only the stone monuments behind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-western Arabia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Co...</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery remains unsolved

Although archaeologists now understand far more than they did a decade ago, the central mystery is still unresolved because the builders left no written records. Every interpretation must be reconstructed from architecture, animal bones, dating evidence and landscape archaeology rather than contemporary explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-western Arabia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Co...</span></span></span>

Several key questions remain open:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **What deity or belief system was involved?** No inscriptions, symbols or surviving traditions explain whom the offerings honoured.
* **Why were cattle especially important?** They may have symbolised fertility, rainfall, prosperity, ancestry or social prestige, but the archaeological evidence cannot distinguish confidently between these possibilities.
* **Did every mustatil serve the same purpose?** Similar architecture suggests shared traditions, yet individual monuments may have developed different local roles over centuries.
* **Who organised construction?** The monuments imply leadership and cooperation, but there is no evidence for permanent political authorities comparable with later states.
* **How often were ceremonies held?** Excavated sites indicate repeated use, but the seasonal rhythm and duration of gatherings remain uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0281904" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cult, herding, and ‘pilgrimage’ in the Late Neolithic of north-west Arabia: Excavations at a mustatil east of AlUla &#124; PLOS One...</span></span></span>

</div>

These uncertainties are genuine archaeological problems rather than invitations to speculation. Claims that mustatils were astronomical observatories, alien constructions or products of a lost super-civilisation are unsupported by excavation evidence.

## Why mustatils matter

The importance of mustatils extends well beyond their architectural form. They demonstrate that prehistoric Arabia contained organised ceremonial landscapes on a scale once thought unlikely for mobile pastoral societies. Their builders invested enormous effort in monuments whose primary purpose appears to have been symbolic rather than practical.

Recent excavations have therefore solved one of Saudi Arabia's major archaeological puzzles by showing that mustatils were places of ritual centred on animal offerings and communal gatherings. Yet the deeper mystery remains remarkably resilient. Without written records, archaeologists can identify what people did far more confidently than they can explain what those actions meant. The monuments stand as enduring evidence that complex religious life, regional cooperation and monumental construction flourished in Arabia long before the emergence of cities, kingdoms or recorded history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-western Arabia &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Co...</span></span></span>
