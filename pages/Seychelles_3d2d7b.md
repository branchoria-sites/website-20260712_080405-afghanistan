Together, these cases show how incomplete records, political secrecy and later storytelling can turn real events into enduring national mysteries.

The mystery begins with what supposedly happened afterwards. According to the familiar version, Levasseur concealed his share of the loot, perhaps in Seychelles. When he was executed on Réunion in 1730, he allegedly threw a coded paper into the crowd and challenged anyone capable of understanding it to find his treasure. The cipher later became associated with Bel Ombre, a rocky coastal district in north-western Mahé.

Bel Ombre has consequently endured more than a century of treasure hunting. Members of the Savy family reportedly excavated there during the early twentieth century. Reginald Cruise-Wilkins began a much larger search in 1949, interpreting holes, carvings, rock formations and documents as an elaborate system of clues. He continued until his death in 1977, and his son John later resumed the work with drilling, pumping and heavy machinery. No authenticated pirate hoard has been recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nation.sc/article.html?id=85714" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nation.sc">[nation.sc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nation.sc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was Mr Cruise Wilkin senior, Reginald Herbert Cruise...Read more...</span></span></span>

### The cryptogram may be the weakest link

The popular story often treats the cipher as an eighteenth-century object whose meaning remains unsolved. Recent historical and cryptological research points to a more fundamental problem: no securely documented original has been traced to Levasseur’s lifetime.

The familiar 17-line cryptogram first became widely known through Charles de La Roncière’s 1934 book *Le Flibustier Mystérieux*. La Roncière was a respected maritime historian, but this particular work blended historical material with literary storytelling. Researchers have found no contemporary account of Levasseur throwing a coded message at his execution, and no independently authenticated chain of custody connecting the published cipher to the pirate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogram_of_Olivier_Levasseur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span></span></span>

Carola Dahlke’s study of the legend compared the treasure story with contemporary records and concluded that only a small historical core survives beneath extensive later invention. The real elements include Levasseur, the capture of the Portuguese ship and his eventual execution. The cryptogram, the theatrical final challenge and the celebrated “Fiery Cross of Goa” appear to belong largely to later storytelling rather than reliable eighteenth-century documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/385137034_Demystifying_La_Buse%27s_Cryptogram_and_the_Fiery_Cross_of_Goa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">But when secret ciphers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Demystifying La Buse&#x27;s Cryptogram and the Fiery Cross of...May 8, 2024 — PDF &#124; The field of cryptology alone offers a multit...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>

There is another common misunderstanding. The cipher is not an impenetrable sequence that modern cryptographers cannot read. Its signs can be rendered into a rough French text, although the resulting wording is obscure and does not supply a clear location. The real puzzle is provenance: who composed it, when, and for what purpose? A readable message is not proof that Levasseur wrote it or that it describes treasure in Seychelles.

### What could still be true?

Several possibilities remain, but they do not carry equal weight.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A genuine hoard may have been buried somewhere in the western Indian Ocean.** Pirates sometimes concealed valuables temporarily, and Levasseur possessed wealth after the 1721 capture. That does not establish that anything was buried at Bel Ombre.
* **The cipher may preserve an older document whose history has been lost.** This cannot be ruled out completely, but no original manuscript or convincing archival trail has surfaced.
* **The Bel Ombre clues may be ordinary features given meaning retrospectively.** Rock holes, carvings and natural formations are especially vulnerable to selective interpretation once searchers already expect a designed puzzle.
* **The entire coded-treasure narrative may be twentieth-century mythmaking attached to a real pirate.** On the surviving evidence, this is the strongest explanation.

</div>

The responsible conclusion is therefore not that there is certainly no treasure. It is that the evidence linking Levasseur’s loot, the cryptogram and Bel Ombre is much weaker than the legend suggests. The absence of a find after extensive excavation matters, but the absence of an authenticated eighteenth-century cipher matters more.

## Who made the graves on Silhouette Island?

At Anse Lascars on Silhouette Island, old graves have long been described as the burials of Arab sailors or traders who reached Seychelles before European ships. The idea is plausible in a broad geographical sense. Arab and Swahili mariners crossed the western Indian Ocean for centuries, and cartographic evidence suggests that sailors probably knew of Seychelles before Portuguese navigators recorded the islands in the early sixteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archimer.ifremer.fr/doc/00799/91111/96767.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archimer.ifremer.fr">[archimer.ifremer.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archimer.ifremer.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ifremer.fr.</span></span></span>

The graves have therefore become part of a larger question: did pre-European visitors merely see the islands, stop briefly for water and food, or establish some form of temporary settlement?

Seychelles was permanently colonised only in 1770. The earliest well-documented European landing occurred in 1609, when the English East India Company ship *Ascension* reached an apparently uninhabited island and found abundant water, fish, birds, coconuts and tortoises. Nothing in that account indicates an established local population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter/553412868?searchresult=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[oup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Academic History of the Seychelles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academic History of the Seychelles</span></span></span>

The Anse Lascars graves seem at first to challenge this picture. They are recognised as a heritage feature, and even UNESCO’s description of Silhouette’s proposed World Heritage significance refers to ancient graves traditionally associated with Arab traders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5799/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span> Yet heritage descriptions often preserve established local interpretations rather than settle archaeological disputes.

Accounts of examinations of the remains report that bones recovered from the graves were dated to around 1800, after colonisation had begun. If correct, that would make the burials early colonial rather than medieval or pre-European.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silhouette_Island" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Silhouette Island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Silhouette Island</span></span></span> The difficulty is that publicly accessible information about the excavation, samples and dating is limited. Without a complete archaeological report explaining which grave was sampled, how securely the remains were associated with it and what dating method was used, the conclusion cannot be treated as final.

The most balanced assessment is that early Arab or Swahili knowledge of Seychelles is probable, but the Anse Lascars burials do not presently provide secure proof of a pre-European community. Occasional landings remain entirely possible. Permanent settlement before 1770 remains unsupported.

This distinction matters. Saying that sailors probably knew of the islands is not the same as claiming that an unknown civilisation lived there. The surviving evidence supports contact and navigation far better than colonisation.

## What remains unclear about the 1981 mercenary invasion?

On 25 November 1981, a group of mercenaries led by Mike Hoare arrived at Seychelles International Airport disguised as members of a drinking club travelling for a holiday. Their aim was to overthrow President France-Albert René’s government. A concealed weapon was discovered during customs inspection, gunfire broke out and the operation collapsed before the attackers could move beyond the airport.

During the fighting, Seychellois officer David Antat and mercenary Johan Fritz were killed. Most of the attackers escaped by hijacking an Air India aircraft that had landed during the crisis. Others were captured and later tried in Seychelles. The broad sequence of events is firmly documented and was discussed by the United Nations Security Council.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_Seychelles_coup_attempt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1981 Seychelles coup attempt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1981 Seychelles coup attempt</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery concerns responsibility beyond the men at the airport. Who authorised, encouraged and financed the operation?

South Africa initially denied official involvement. Later findings of South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission described the force as a mixture of mercenaries and members of the South African Defence Force, placing the operation within the apartheid state’s wider regional struggle against governments it regarded as hostile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sabctrc.saha.org.za/reports/volume2/chapter2/subsection44.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sabctrc.saha.org.za">[Truth Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sabctrc.saha.org.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Truth Commission TRC Final Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truth Commission TRC Final Report</span></span></span> Contemporary reporting also noted immediate doubts about Pretoria’s denials and questions about the unusually favourable treatment of mercenaries who returned to South Africa aboard the hijacked aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csmonitor.com/1981/1207/120758.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csmonitor.com">[Christian Science Monitor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csmonitor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on csmonitor.com.</span></span></span>

There were additional allegations involving Seychellois exiles and foreign intelligence interests. Some opposition figures wanted to remove René, whose 1977 coup had displaced the country’s first president, James Mancham. But the precise division of responsibility among exiles, mercenary organisers, South African military or intelligence personnel and other possible supporters remains imperfectly documented.

The strongest evidence supports some level of South African state involvement, not a wholly private adventure by freelance soldiers. What remains uncertain is how far authorisation extended, who shaped the operational plan and which outside actors had advance knowledge. The failed invasion is therefore not mysterious because nobody knows what happened. It is mysterious because the surviving record does not fully reveal the political command structure behind it.

## Who killed Gérard Hoarau?

Gérard Hoarau was a leading Seychellois opponent of President René’s one-party government. Living in exile in Britain, he headed the Seychelles National Movement and advocated political change in the islands. On 29 November 1985, he was shot repeatedly outside his home in Edgware, north London, with a Sterling sub-machine gun. No one has been convicted of his murder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.murdermap.co.uk/historical-murders/gerard-hoarau-assassination-murder-unsolved/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: murdermap.co.uk">[murdermap.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">murdermap.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">murdermap The assassination of Gerard Hoarau</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">murdermap The assassination of Gerard Hoarau</span></span></span>

The circumstances immediately encouraged suspicions of a political assassination. Hoarau was a prominent exile, the weapon and manner of attack suggested planning, and his telephone had reportedly been interfered with. Three people were convicted in 1986 of offences connected with obstructing or interfering with the investigation, but those proceedings did not identify the gunman or establish who ordered the killing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2018/aug/02/man-arrested-in-antrim-over-1985-killing-of-exiled-politician" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">man arrested in antrim over 1985 killing of exiled politician</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">man arrested in antrim over 1985 killing of exiled politician</span></span></span>

For decades the case appeared dormant. The Metropolitan Police reviewed it in 2016, generating new lines of inquiry. A man was arrested in Northern Ireland in 2018, but no murder charge followed. Police renewed their appeal for information in 2023, confirming that the investigation remained active nearly four decades after Hoarau’s death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.seychellesnewsagency.com/public/articles/18510/appeal-for-info-londons-met-police-investigating-1985-murder-of-gerard-hoarau" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: seychellesnewsagency.com">[Seychelles News Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">seychellesnewsagency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">appeal for info londons met police investigating 1985 murder of gerard hoarau</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">appeal for info londons met police investigating 1985 murder of gerard hoarau</span></span></span>

The central theories are straightforward but unproved:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Hoarau was killed on behalf of political interests threatened by his opposition activities.
* The murder arose from divisions within exile or mercenary networks connected with attempts to change the Seychellois government.
* Individuals with political motives acted independently rather than under direct state orders.

</div>

Public accusations have often gone much further than the available criminal evidence. Suspicion of political involvement is reasonable because of Hoarau’s position, the professional appearance of the attack and the historical climate. It is not the same as proof against a particular government official or organisation.

The mystery endures partly because the murder occurred across several overlapping worlds: Seychellois exile politics, Cold War intelligence, apartheid-era regional operations and private mercenary networks. Witnesses, records and participants were spread across different jurisdictions. Passage of time has made prosecution harder, although continuing police appeals indicate that investigators do not regard the case as formally closed.

## Why these mysteries became part of Seychellois memory

Seychelles was permanently settled comparatively late, and much of its written history was produced by colonial administrators, visiting sailors and institutions outside the islands. That leaves the earliest period especially vulnerable to gaps. A grave, a carved rock or an old family document can acquire unusual importance when few contemporary records survive.

The country’s geography also encourages treasure stories. Remote beaches, granite formations and sheltered coves resemble the landscape imagined in pirate fiction. Real piracy did affect western Indian Ocean shipping, so legends could grow around genuine names and events rather than being invented from nothing. Bel Ombre’s physical excavation site then gave the story a visible presence: each tunnel and drilled rock became evidence not necessarily of treasure, but of the strength of belief in it.

Modern political mysteries developed for different reasons. One-party rule, exile, covert regional conflict and intelligence activity created genuine secrecy. In the cases of the 1981 invasion and Hoarau’s murder, uncertainty is not simply folklore filling an empty past. It reflects incomplete official disclosure, disputed testimony and the difficulty of investigating operations crossing national borders.

The four cases therefore belong in different evidential categories. The 1721 pirate raid is real, but the Seychelles cryptogram legend is poorly authenticated. Pre-European sailors probably knew of the islands, but the Silhouette graves do not securely prove early settlement. The 1981 coup attempt is documented, while its full sponsorship remains partly concealed. Hoarau’s killing is an established unsolved murder whose political authorship is suspected but unproved.

## What the evidence supports

Seychelles’ historical mysteries become clearer when the confirmed event is separated from the later claim attached to it.

The strongest conclusion about La Buse is that a real pirate and a real fortune were transformed into a twentieth-century coded-treasure legend. Bel Ombre may still conceal objects from the maritime past, but no reliable evidence demonstrates that Levasseur buried his 1721 loot there.

The strongest conclusion about the Silhouette graves is that Seychelles probably received occasional visitors before Europeans formally recorded and settled the archipelago. The graves themselves, however, remain an uncertain and possibly colonial-era clue rather than proof of a lost population.

The political cases contain less folklore and more documented secrecy. South African involvement in the 1981 mercenary attack is substantially supported, although the complete chain of command remains obscure. Gérard Hoarau’s assassination remains the country’s clearest unresolved historical crime: the victim, method and political setting are known, but the killer and those behind him have never been established in court.

These are not mysteries because every explanation is equally possible. In each case, some interpretations fit the surviving evidence far better than others. Their lasting importance lies in the boundary between what Seychelles can document, what it can reasonably infer and what generations of retelling have added.
