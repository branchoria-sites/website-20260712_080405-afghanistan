## Introduction

Around 3.66 million years ago, a fall of volcanic ash at Laetoli in northern [Tanzania]({{ 'what-still-mystifies-us-about-tanzanias/' | relative_url }}) captured a moment that lasted only minutes but has fascinated scientists for decades. The best-known footprints, known as the **Site G** trail, provide the oldest widely accepted evidence that an early human relative walked habitually upright. Yet the deeper mystery is not whether these ancient walkers were bipedal, but exactly **who** they were. For many years the answer seemed straightforward: the tracks belonged to *Australopithecus afarensis*, the same species as the famous fossil "Lucy". More recent research, however, has reopened the debate by showing that another nearby trail, **Site A**, was probably made by a different kind of hominin altogether. Rather than revealing a single species crossing an ash field, Laetoli may preserve a landscape shared by multiple branches of the human family.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Footprint evidence of early hominin locomotor diversity at Laetoli, Tanzania &#124; NatureDecember 1, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2021</span></span></span>

## What the Site G footprints establish

The Site G footprints were discovered in 1978 during excavations led by the palaeoanthropologist Mary Leakey. Preserved beneath later layers of volcanic ash, the trackway records at least three individuals walking across damp ground shortly after an eruption.

Several features make these footprints exceptional:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The walkers moved fully upright rather than combining walking with knuckle-walking or climbing.
* The footprints show a forward-pointing big toe rather than the grasping toe seen in modern chimpanzees.
* Weight shifted from heel to toe in a recognisably human pattern.
* The trackways indicate a stable, efficient form of bipedal walking rather than an occasional upright stance.

</div>

These characteristics have convinced most researchers that the footprints represent habitual bipedalism rather than an experimental way of moving. Although the feet were not identical to those of modern humans, they functioned in ways remarkably similar to our own.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/srep21916" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Laetoli’s lost tracks: 3D generated mean shape and missing footprints &#124; Scientific ReportsFebruary 23, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2016</span></span></span>

The leading candidate for the Site G trackmaker remains *Australopithecus afarensis*. Fossils of this species are known from East Africa at approximately the right age, and their leg, pelvis and foot anatomy fits the evidence preserved in the tracks. While absolute certainty is impossible because footprints cannot be matched to a skeleton, this attribution remains the strongest interpretation supported by current evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Footprint evidence of early hominin locomotor diversity at Laetoli, Tanzania &#124; NatureDecember 1, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2021</span></span></span>

## Why the Site A trail changed the debate

The mystery became more complicated because Laetoli contains another, much shorter footprint trail discovered two years before Site G.

Initially, Site A received little attention. Its footprints looked unusual, with a crossing gait and a different foot shape from the famous Site G prints. Some researchers even suggested that they had been made by a bear walking briefly on its hind legs. Since bears do not naturally occur in modern sub-Saharan Africa, this explanation was always unusual, but the strange appearance of the prints kept the idea alive for decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Footprint evidence of early hominin locomotor diversity at Laetoli, Tanzania &#124; NatureDecember 1, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2021</span></span></span>

In 2021 researchers relocated the forgotten Site A trail and re-examined it using modern techniques including three-dimensional scanning, digital photogrammetry and comparisons with the walking patterns of humans, chimpanzees and bears. Their conclusion overturned the long-standing bear hypothesis.

The evidence suggested that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the footprint proportions do not match those of bears walking upright;
* the step width is far narrower than expected for a bipedal bear;
* the gait is consistent with a hominin rather than another mammal;
* the foot differs significantly from the Site G trackmaker.

</div>

Instead of proving the tracks belonged to *Australopithecus afarensis*, the new analysis argued that they probably came from another bipedal hominin living in the same region at roughly the same time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Footprint evidence of early hominin locomotor diversity at Laetoli, Tanzania &#124; NatureDecember 1, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2021</span></span></span>

This interpretation fits a wider trend in palaeoanthropology. Fossil discoveries across eastern Africa increasingly suggest that several early hominin species overlapped during the Pliocene rather than evolving in a simple, single line toward modern humans. Laetoli may therefore provide behavioural evidence of this diversity rather than merely skeletal evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Footprint evidence of early hominin locomotor diversity at Laetoli, Tanzania &#124; NatureDecember 1, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2021</span></span></span>

## What footprints cannot reveal

The Laetoli trails are extraordinarily informative, but they also have clear limits.

Footprints can show:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* how an individual walked;
* approximate body size;
* whether the walker was bipedal;
* aspects of foot anatomy;
* whether multiple individuals moved together or separately.

</div>

They cannot directly reveal:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the precise species responsible;
* age or sex with certainty;
* social relationships between the walkers;
* language, behaviour or culture;
* whether different trails were made hours, days or longer apart.

</div>

This distinction explains why debate continues despite the remarkable preservation. A footprint records movement rather than identity. Even with modern digital analysis, scientists must compare the impressions with fossil skeletons whose own relationships remain under discussion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Footprint evidence of early hominin locomotor diversity at Laetoli, Tanzania &#124; NatureDecember 1, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2021</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery still matters

The enduring mystery is no longer whether the Laetoli footprints represent upright walking. That question is largely settled. Instead, the uncertainty concerns how many different kinds of early hominins shared East Africa nearly 3.7 million years ago.

If only Site G existed, Laetoli would reinforce the long-held view that *Australopithecus afarensis* dominated the landscape. Site A instead hints that another lineage—with a differently shaped foot and a distinct walking style—may have lived alongside it. Although researchers remain cautious about assigning the prints to a specific species, the possibility of multiple contemporary hominins has become far more plausible than it once appeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Footprint evidence of early hominin locomotor diversity at Laetoli, Tanzania &#124; NatureDecember 1, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2021</span></span></span>

For Tanzania, Laetoli remains one of the country's most significant archaeological sites because it preserves direct evidence of behaviour rather than bones alone. Every new analytical method extracts additional information from footprints made millions of years ago, yet the identity of every walker cannot be read from the ash. That combination of extraordinary evidence and persistent uncertainty is precisely what makes the Laetoli footprint trails one of Tanzania's most enduring historical and scientific mysteries.
