## Introduction

[Switzerland]({{ 'switzerland/' | relative_url }})'s retreating glaciers are doing something that would once have seemed impossible: they are solving mountain disappearances that remained unexplained for decades. As Alpine ice melts and shifts, it reveals the bodies, clothing and personal belongings of climbers, farmers and travellers who vanished long ago. These discoveries rarely uncover new mysteries. More often, they resolve old ones by explaining where people died, confirming identities through DNA and allowing families to hold funerals after generations of uncertainty. At the same time, the glaciers continue to conceal many victims, reminding investigators that the mountains still hold unanswered questions beneath the ice. The story is therefore not simply about climate or archaeology, but about how glaciers preserve evidence, distort it over time and occasionally return it.

## How glaciers preserve and move human remains

A glacier is not a frozen vault where everything stays in the same place. Instead, it behaves like an extremely slow-moving river of ice. Snow accumulates at higher elevations, compresses into ice and gradually flows downhill under its own weight.

When someone falls into a crevasse or is rapidly buried by snow, cold temperatures can greatly slow decomposition. Clothing, leather, paper, metal objects and even soft tissues may survive remarkably well for decades. However, the glacier itself never stops moving. As the ice deforms and melts, bodies and belongings can be carried hundreds of metres from the place where an accident originally occurred before emerging near the glacier's edge many years later. This means that where remains are discovered is often not where the fatal accident happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/planet-earth/climate-change/body-of-climber-missing-for-nearly-40-years-discovered-in-melting-swiss-glacier" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceBody of climber missing for nearly 40 years discovered in melting Swiss glacier &#124; Live ScienceJuly 28, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2023</span></span></span>

For investigators, every recovered object becomes valuable evidence. Boots, climbing equipment, watches, identity papers and backpacks help reconstruct events, while modern DNA testing can confirm identities even when physical recognition is impossible. These techniques have transformed discoveries that once would have remained anonymous into documented historical cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/planet-earth/climate-change/body-of-climber-missing-for-nearly-40-years-discovered-in-melting-swiss-glacier" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceBody of climber missing for nearly 40 years discovered in melting Swiss glacier &#124; Live ScienceJuly 28, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2023</span></span></span>

## Disappearances resolved after decades in the ice

The best-known Swiss example is the discovery of Marcelin and Francine Dumoulin.

The farming couple disappeared on 15 August 1942 while crossing the mountains from Savièse to feed cattle on a high alpine pasture. Despite extensive searches, they were never found, leaving seven children to grow up without knowing what had happened to their parents. In July 2017, a worker maintaining equipment near the Tsanfleuron Glacier spotted human remains emerging from the ice. DNA testing quickly confirmed that the bodies belonged to the Dumoulins, still accompanied by personal possessions including backpacks and everyday items from the 1940s. Their discovery finally allowed surviving relatives to hold the funeral they had hoped for over seventy-five years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/swiss-couple-found-bodies-ice-glacier-spd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[nationalgeographic.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalgeographic.com.</span></span></span>

The Dumoulin case is not unique. Swiss police have repeatedly identified long-missing mountaineers after glaciers exposed remains decades after their disappearance. In 2023, climbers discovered human remains on the Theodul Glacier near Zermatt. DNA analysis identified the victim as a German climber who had vanished in 1986. Equipment emerging alongside the body helped investigators understand both the identity of the victim and the likely circumstances of the accident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/planet-earth/climate-change/body-of-climber-missing-for-nearly-40-years-discovered-in-melting-swiss-glacier" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceBody of climber missing for nearly 40 years discovered in melting Swiss glacier &#124; Live ScienceJuly 28, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2023</span></span></span>

Other recoveries have involved climbers missing since the 1970s, as well as older disappearances whose identities became possible only through advances in forensic genetics. Although each case is individual, together they demonstrate a consistent pattern: glaciers can preserve evidence long enough for modern science to answer questions that earlier rescue efforts could not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/planet-earth/climate-change/body-of-climber-missing-for-nearly-40-years-discovered-in-melting-swiss-glacier" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceBody of climber missing for nearly 40 years discovered in melting Swiss glacier &#124; Live ScienceJuly 28, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why identification is still difficult

Finding remains is only the beginning of an investigation.

Glacier movement may separate bodies from personal possessions, scatter equipment over wide areas or damage skeletal remains. Seasonal melting can expose only part of a body before fresh snowfall buries it again. Search teams therefore treat every discovery as part of a larger forensic scene rather than an isolated find.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/planet-earth/climate-change/body-of-climber-missing-for-nearly-40-years-discovered-in-melting-swiss-glacier" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceBody of climber missing for nearly 40 years discovered in melting Swiss glacier &#124; Live ScienceJuly 28, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2023</span></span></span>

Identification also depends on historical records. Older disappearances may have incomplete police files, missing dental records or no surviving close relatives for DNA comparison. In some cases, investigators must combine several forms of evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* DNA comparisons with living relatives.
* Historical missing-person records.
* Clothing and equipment dating.
* Personal possessions recovered with the remains.
* The estimated path of glacier movement over several decades.

</div>

Only when these different strands agree can authorities confidently identify an individual.

## Why some disappearances remain unsolved

Despite recent discoveries, many Alpine disappearances have not been resolved.

Some victims may lie beneath glaciers that have not yet retreated far enough to expose them. Others may have fallen onto rock faces, into deep crevasses or beneath avalanches where remains were never incorporated into moving ice. In certain cases, erosion may have destroyed crucial evidence before it reached the glacier's edge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/swiss-couple-found-bodies-ice-glacier-spd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalgeographic.com.</span></span></span>

Swiss authorities have long recorded hundreds of unresolved mountain disappearances across the Alps. Although glacier retreat has led to more discoveries in recent years, it should not be assumed that every missing person will eventually be found. Many accidents occurred in terrain where recovery may never be possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/swiss-couple-found-bodies-ice-glacier-spd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalgeographic.com.</span></span></span>

## What these discoveries mean for Switzerland's historical mysteries

Glacier discoveries occupy an unusual place among Switzerland's historical mysteries because they often move from uncertainty to resolution rather than remaining permanently unsolved.

Unlike a disputed murder or a vanished treasure, many mountain disappearances eventually receive an evidence-based answer. The mystery lies in the long period between disappearance and discovery, when families have no explanation and rescuers no trace. When glaciers finally reveal their evidence, the result is less a sensational revelation than the completion of an unfinished historical record.

These recoveries also illustrate the growing importance of forensic science in historical investigation. DNA analysis, careful archaeological recovery and knowledge of glacier movement now allow investigators to identify victims who would once have remained anonymous. Yet the Alps continue to conceal unknown stories beneath the ice, ensuring that Switzerland's glaciers remain both archives of the past and sources of future discoveries.
