## Introduction

The best-known case is the theory that Amelia Earhart and Fred Noonan reached [Nikumaroro]({{ 'nikumaroro/' | relative_url }}) after disappearing in 1937. It remains plausible but unproved: documents, artefacts and old bone measurements support a castaway scenario, yet no object has been conclusively linked to either traveller or their aircraft. On Tarawa, by contrast, the mystery of missing American war dead is being steadily resolved through archaeology and DNA. Other Kiribati enigmas show how mysteries can be created—or corrected—by faulty maps, fragmentary archaeology and oral history. Together, they reveal a country whose historical record is unusually vulnerable to distance, weather, colonial disruption and the movement of shorelines.

## Did Amelia Earhart reach Nikumaroro?

On 2 July 1937, American aviator Amelia Earhart and navigator Fred Noonan disappeared while flying from Lae, New Guinea, towards Howland Island. The generally accepted explanation is that their Lockheed Electra ran out of fuel and fell into the central Pacific. A competing theory proposes that they continued south-east along a navigational line and reached Gardner Island, now Nikumaroro, in the Phoenix Islands of Kiribati.

Supporters of the Nikumaroro theory argue that Earhart could have landed on the atoll’s broad reef flat while the tide was low. The aircraft might then have remained usable briefly before being damaged or swept into deeper water. This scenario has been linked to reports of intermittent radio signals received after the disappearance, although the authenticity and location of those signals remain disputed. The Smithsonian’s National Air and Space Museum describes the Nikumaroro interpretation as a fact-based line of investigation but emphasises that rival searches and theories have also failed to produce decisive proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/air-and-space-quarterly/issue-12/aviation-archaeology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Air and Space Museum Treasure Hunting for Airplanes: The Great Debate</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space MuseumTreasure Hunting for Airplanes: The Great DebateSeptember 23, 2024 — 23 Sept 2024 — Later this year, in fact...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 23, 2024</span></span></span>

### The castaway evidence

The strongest historical clue emerged three years after Earhart vanished. In 1940, colonial officer Gerald Gallagher reported the discovery of a partial human skeleton on Nikumaroro. Nearby objects included part of a shoe sole, a bottle, the remains of a fire and a box once used to hold a sextant. Gallagher considered the possibility that the remains belonged to Earhart and arranged for them to be examined in Fiji.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tighar.org/Projects/Earhart/Archives/Research/Bulletins/01_Castaway.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tighar.org">[TIGHAR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tighar.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">01 Castaway</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Castaway of Gardner IslandA partial human skeleton, badly damaged by coconut crabs, was found on the island in 1940 lying under...</span></span></span>

Dr David Hoodless, who studied the bones in 1941, concluded that they most probably belonged to a short, stocky man of mixed or European ancestry. The bones subsequently disappeared, leaving researchers with Hoodless’s measurements rather than the remains themselves. This loss is central to the mystery: modern DNA testing could potentially have settled the question, but it cannot be performed on measurements recorded in a file.

In 2018, forensic anthropologist Richard Jantz reassessed those measurements using modern statistical methods and estimates of Earhart’s body proportions. He concluded that Earhart resembled the measured individual more closely than 99 per cent of people in his comparison sample. Jantz therefore argued that the remains were more likely than not to have been hers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.upress.ufl.edu/fa/article/view/525" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.upress.ufl.edu">[University of Florida Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.upress.ufl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Florida PressAmelia Earhart and the Nikumaroro Bonesby RL Jantz · 2018 · Cited by 4 — This analysis reveals that Earhart is...</span></span></span>

That conclusion is not universally accepted. Earlier critics Peter Cross and Richard Wright argued that retrospective identification was too uncertain and that the original assessment could not simply be dismissed. Only a limited selection of bones had been recovered, some measurements were ambiguous, and later researchers had to estimate Earhart’s skeletal dimensions from photographs and clothing. The statistical resemblance is intriguing, but it is not equivalent to a DNA match or a unique anatomical identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X15300109" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Nikumaroro bones identification controversy: First...by PJ Cross · 2015 · Cited by 5 — The primary supporting evidence...</span></span></span>

Other objects found during repeated expeditions have included fragments of glass, pieces of aluminium, American-made consumer items and remains suggesting that someone camped and ate birds, fish or shellfish in the island’s south-east. None has been proved to have belonged to Earhart or Noonan. Nikumaroro was visited by ships, labourers, colonial settlers and American Coast Guard personnel, all of whom could have left imported objects behind.

### The missing aircraft

The decisive evidence would be a component bearing a serial number or other feature unique to Earhart’s Electra. Despite numerous land and underwater searches, no such item has been authenticated. Reef conditions make the absence difficult to interpret: storms, currents, coral growth and a steep underwater slope could have dispersed or buried wreckage, but those same possibilities allow the theory to survive without confirming it.

A new investigation has focused on the “Taraia Object”, an anomaly visible in aerial and satellite images of Nikumaroro’s lagoon. Purdue Research Foundation and the Archaeological Legacy Institute describe their planned 2026 expedition as an attempt to determine whether the object could be part of Earhart’s aircraft. The proposed work includes sonar, magnetometer surveying and controlled underwater excavation. At present, however, the object is only an unidentified feature in imagery, not established aircraft wreckage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://amelia.prf.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amelia.prf.org">[prf.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amelia.prf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archaeological Legacy Institute: Home</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archaeological Legacy Institute: Home</span></span></span>

The fairest verdict is that Nikumaroro offers a coherent alternative to the crash-at-sea explanation. The castaway documents are genuine, and the bone measurements deserve attention. Yet every important link remains circumstantial. It has not been demonstrated that Earhart landed on the reef, that the post-disappearance transmissions came from her aircraft, that the skeleton was hers, or that any known object on the island came from the Electra.

## Why did so many Tarawa graves disappear?

The missing graves of the Battle of Tarawa form a different kind of mystery. They were not created by an unexplained disappearance but by the confusion of battle, hurried burial work and serious failures in post-war record-keeping.

United States forces assaulted Betio, a small island in Tarawa Atoll, on 20 November 1943. Roughly 1,000 American Marines and sailors were killed during several days of fighting, while more than 2,000 were wounded. The Japanese garrison was almost entirely destroyed. Bodies were buried quickly in battlefield cemeteries, isolated graves and trenches marked on plans whose accuracy varied considerably.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-025/h-025-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on navy.mil.</span></span></span>

In 1946 and 1947, the US Army’s graves registration service attempted to gather the American dead into a central burial ground known as Lone Palm Cemetery. Investigators discovered that some wartime cemetery markers had been moved, rearranged or used as memorial markers rather than placed directly over graves. Construction and occupation had also changed the landscape. By the end of the process, almost half of the known American casualties had not been located, while more than 90 sets of unidentified Tarawa remains were eventually buried in Honolulu.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dpaa.mil/News-Stories/ID-Announcements/Article/2295671/marine-accounted-for-from-world-war-ii-athon-f/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa.mil">[dpaa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marine Accounted For From World War II (Athon, F.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marine Accounted For From World War II (Athon, F.</span></span></span>

For decades, official records described many of these men as “non-recoverable”. That designation gave the impression that their bodies had been destroyed or lost at sea. Archaeological discoveries on Betio have shown that some were still in their original graves.

In 2009, the non-profit organisation History Flight located a burial area associated with Cemetery 33. Subsequent excavations uncovered trenches and rows that did not correspond neatly with the post-war reconstruction of the site. Remains were transferred to the US Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency, where specialists used dental records, skeletal analysis, chest radiographs, personal equipment and mitochondrial DNA to restore individual identities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dpaa.mil/News-Stories/ID-Announcements/Article/1999165/marine-accounted-for-from-world-war-ii-whitaker-c/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa.mil">[dpaa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marine Accounted for from World War II (Whitaker, C.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marine Accounted for from World War II (Whitaker, C.</span></span></span>

The identification of Marine Franklin Athon illustrates how the mystery developed. Records stated that Athon had been buried in Row D of a cemetery on Betio, but post-war investigators found no remains they could associate with him. In 2019, archaeologists uncovered a previously unknown burial area believed to be the missing Row D. Scientific and documentary evidence then allowed Athon to be identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dpaa.mil/News-Stories/ID-Announcements/Article/2295671/marine-accounted-for-from-world-war-ii-athon-f/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa.mil">[dpaa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marine Accounted For From World War II (Athon, F.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marine Accounted For From World War II (Athon, F.</span></span></span>

Other cases have been solved by combining newly excavated remains with bones long held as unidentified in Honolulu. In the case of Roy Waltz, material recovered on Betio was found to belong with remains previously catalogued as “Tarawa Unknown X-228”. DNA and anthropological analysis helped reunite the separated evidence and establish his identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dpaa.mil/News-Stories/ID-Announcements/Article/2074089/marine-accounted-for-from-world-war-ii-waltz-r/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa.mil">[dpaa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marine Accounted For From World War II (Waltz, R.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marine Accounted For From World War II (Waltz, R.</span></span></span>

The Tarawa mystery therefore has no single conspiratorial explanation. The graves were obscured by inaccurate maps, temporary markers, mistaken assumptions, rushed recovery work and later development on an extremely crowded island. The continuing discoveries matter both to the families of the missing and to Kiribati, where homes, roads and public buildings occupy ground that was once a battlefield. Recovery work depends on cooperation with the Kiribati authorities and local communities, not merely on the reconstruction of American military records.

## How did Sarah Ann Island vanish from the map?

Sarah Ann Island was once shown on charts of the central Pacific, north of the equator. American commercial interests claimed it during the nineteenth-century guano rush, when the United States’ Guano Islands Act encouraged citizens to claim unoccupied islands containing valuable seabird deposits. Yet later expeditions could not find Sarah Ann at its recorded position.

The puzzle became especially important before the total solar eclipse of 8 June 1937. Astronomers hoped that a small island close to the eclipse track might provide a valuable observation station. Searches failed to locate Sarah Ann, and observations were instead carried out from islands including Kanton, now part of Kiribati.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adsabs.harvard.edu/full/1933PASP...45...33S" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adsabs.harvard.edu">[Astrophysics Data System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adsabs.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

The romantic explanation was that Sarah Ann had sunk beneath the sea. Pacific charts contained many reports of low islands that were seen once and then apparently vanished, and coral atolls were widely imagined as fragile features capable of disappearing suddenly.

The documentary evidence favours a more ordinary solution: copying and coordinate errors. Historical map research indicates that “Sarah Island” or “Sarah Ann” may have been another name associated with Malden Island, which lies south rather than north of the equator. A misplaced north–south designation could therefore have produced a duplicate island hundreds of kilometres from the real one. Later chartmakers repeated the position because it appeared in earlier navigational references.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mapmyths.com/blog/sarah-ann/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mapmyths.com">[Map Myths]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mapmyths.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sarah ann</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sarah ann</span></span></span>

Sarah Ann was thus probably not a physical island that vanished. It was a paper island: a real sighting, name or claim detached from its correct location and transformed through repetition into a separate geographical feature. Its story shows why historic maps must be treated as records of reported knowledge rather than exact pictures of what existed.

The case also reflects the political history of the region. A mark on a chart could support a guano claim, a scientific expedition or a claim of sovereignty. Errors were therefore capable of acquiring legal and strategic importance long after the original navigator had left the area.

## What do Kiribati’s ruins and oral histories really tell us?

Internet accounts sometimes describe the stone structures of Malden Island as evidence of an unknown civilisation, complete with roads disappearing into the sea and monuments of supposedly inexplicable origin. Archaeology offers a less sensational but more valuable interpretation.

Malden contains house remains, graves and raised ceremonial platforms consistent with wider Polynesian building traditions. Similar architecture occurs elsewhere in the central and eastern Pacific. The real unanswered questions concern when settlers arrived, where they came from, whether occupation was permanent or intermittent, and why the island was empty when regularly documented by outsiders. Archaeological work across Kiribati remains comparatively limited, especially when measured against research on larger Pacific islands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-030-30018" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[springer.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archaeology and Cultural Heritage in Kiribati</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archaeology and Cultural Heritage in Kiribati</span></span></span>

The ruins do not require Phoenicians, a lost global civilisation or unknown stone-building technology. They demonstrate that Polynesian voyagers reached and used islands that later appeared remote and uninhabitable to Europeans. Changes in rainfall, freshwater supply, food resources, population and voyaging networks may all have contributed to abandonment. The surviving evidence is too incomplete to reduce the process to one dramatic event.

A more productive example comes from Makin, in northern Kiribati. Three unusually large coral boulders lie well inland, where ordinary waves could not readily have placed them. Local oral history described enormous waves sweeping across the island, but the account had no corresponding written record.

Geological investigation found that the boulders had been torn from the reef and transported by an extreme marine event, most probably a tsunami towards the end of the sixteenth century. The researchers combined physical analysis with information preserved by Makin storytellers, providing the first dated evidence for a high-magnitude prehistoric tsunami in that part of the central and western Pacific.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/iar.12417" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiley.com.</span></span></span>

Important details remain uncertain, including the exact source and path of the tsunami. But the broad mystery—how immense reef boulders reached their present position—has moved towards an evidence-based solution. The case also challenges the assumption that oral traditions are merely decorative folklore. They can retain information about rare disasters for centuries, particularly in places where colonial archives began late and written records remain sparse.

Kiribati traditions also include accounts of islands or lands said to have disappeared. Some may preserve memories of shoreline erosion, storm damage, temporary sandbanks or confusion between similarly named places. Others cannot be matched securely to a physical location. Historical ecology research treats such stories as evidence requiring comparison with geology, archaeology and navigational knowledge, rather than accepting every vanished island literally or dismissing the traditions altogether.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232694981_Historical_Ecology_in_Kiribati_Linking_Past_with_Present_1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">232694981 Historical Ecology in Kiribati Linking Past with Present 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">232694981 Historical Ecology in Kiribati Linking Past with Present 1</span></span></span>

## Why Kiribati’s mysteries are unusually difficult to solve

Kiribati covers an immense ocean area while possessing very little land. Its island groups are widely separated, and many individual atolls are uninhabited or difficult to reach. An object lost just beyond a reef may descend into deep water; a grave may be obscured by later construction; and a colonial document may be held thousands of kilometres away in Fiji, Britain, Australia or the United States.

The physical landscape is itself unstable. Atoll shorelines shift as sand and coral debris are moved by waves, currents and storms. Freshwater conditions affect whether settlements can survive, while vegetation can conceal structures and graves. These processes do not erase every trace, but they make it hazardous to interpret the absence of visible evidence as proof that nothing happened.

The archival record is equally uneven. European documents often concentrate on ships, missionaries, phosphate, guano claims, war and colonial administration. They may preserve a sextant-box description or a cemetery diagram while saying little about local knowledge, land use or how island communities understood the same events. Archaeology and oral history are therefore essential correctives, not optional additions.

Kiribati’s historical mysteries are most revealing when the categories remain clear. The Nikumaroro theory is a serious but unconfirmed hypothesis. The missing graves of Tarawa are documented consequences of wartime and administrative failure that modern recovery work is gradually correcting. Sarah Ann Island is probably a cartographic duplication rather than a drowned landmass. Malden’s ruins are genuine Polynesian heritage, not evidence of an exotic lost civilisation. Makin’s boulders show that a traditional account and geological evidence can converge without every detail of the past becoming certain.

What links these cases is not a single hidden history, but the fragile path by which evidence survives. In Kiribati, the boundary between land and sea is narrow, and the boundary between record, memory and speculation can be just as difficult to trace.
