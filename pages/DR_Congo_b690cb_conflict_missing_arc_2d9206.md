## Introduction

The greatest mystery surrounding the Democratic Republic of the Congo's modern conflicts is not a single unsolved crime but the sheer scale of what can no longer be known with certainty. Since independence in 1960, successive rebellions, regional wars and local conflicts have destroyed archives, displaced officials, scattered records and left countless families without definitive answers about missing relatives. Many deaths were never registered, many disappearances were never formally investigated, and entire collections of administrative documents vanished alongside government offices and military installations.

As a result, historians, investigators and relatives of the missing must reconstruct events from fragments: surviving government files, United Nations investigations, humanitarian records, church archives, forensic evidence and oral testimony. While many atrocities are well documented, the full record of who disappeared, who was killed and where victims were buried may never be completely recovered.

## How conflict erased Congo's documentary record

The destruction of archives in [DR Congo]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-dr-congos/' | relative_url }}) has occurred through several overlapping processes rather than a single catastrophic event.

First, repeated warfare physically destroyed government buildings, local administrative offices, police stations and court records. Fighting during the Congo Crisis of the 1960s, the First Congo War (1996–1997), the Second Congo War (1998–2003), and numerous later conflicts damaged or abandoned many state institutions. In insecure regions, records were often left behind during evacuations or deliberately burned to prevent opponents from obtaining information.

Second, weak administrative systems meant that many records never existed in complete form. Large rural areas had limited civil registration even before conflict intensified. Births, deaths and population movements frequently went unrecorded, making later casualty reconstruction exceptionally difficult.

Third, conflict displaced the people responsible for preserving institutional memory. Civil servants fled, local officials were killed or replaced, and archives deteriorated through neglect in tropical conditions where humidity, insects and mould rapidly damage paper records.

The result is not merely missing paperwork but missing evidence. Without reliable administrative records, investigators often cannot establish precise timelines, identify victims or verify conflicting accounts decades later.

## Why casualty figures remain uncertain

One of the most misunderstood aspects of the Congolese wars is the uncertainty surrounding death tolls.

Large estimates for conflict-related mortality are widely cited, but they often measure different things. Some figures count direct violence alone, while others include deaths caused indirectly by disease, hunger and displacement resulting from war. Because many deaths occurred in remote communities beyond effective government control, exact numbers cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

The same uncertainty affects disappearances. Families frequently reported relatives leaving villages during attacks, being detained by armed groups or government forces, or fleeing into forests without ever returning. In many cases there was no functioning authority capable of recording arrests, deaths or burial locations.

This does not mean the violence is in doubt. Rather, the uncertainty reflects the collapse of the systems that would normally document it.

## Disappearances without records

Conflict-related disappearances in DR Congo occurred in many different ways.

Some people disappeared after being detained by military forces or armed groups. Others vanished while escaping attacks or during mass population movements. In areas repeatedly changing hands between competing armed organisations, witnesses themselves were often displaced or killed, making later investigation extremely difficult.

Several factors continue to obstruct efforts to identify the missing:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* destroyed detention registers;
* missing military records;
* undocumented burials;
* inaccessible or unmarked mass grave sites;
* changing control over conflict zones;
* fear among surviving witnesses;
* population displacement across national borders.

</div>

International humanitarian law requires parties to armed conflict to record detainees, document deaths where possible and help families learn the fate of missing relatives. In practice, these obligations were frequently ignored or impossible to implement during many phases of the Congolese conflicts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icrc.org/en/law-and-policy/protected-persons-missing-persons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icrc.org">[ICRC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icrc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">protected persons missing persons</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Protected persons: Missing persons &#124; ICRCJuly 28, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2014</span></span></span>

## The UN Mapping Report and the challenge of reconstruction

One of the most important attempts to reconstruct lost evidence is the United Nations Mapping Report, published after years of investigation into serious violations committed between 1993 and 2003.

Rather than claiming to provide a complete historical record, investigators assembled thousands of surviving pieces of evidence from multiple sources, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* eyewitness testimony;
* humanitarian organisations;
* church documentation;
* medical records;
* judicial files;
* NGO investigations;
* surviving administrative documents;
* United Nations field reports.

</div>

The report documented hundreds of incidents involving killings, disappearances, torture and other grave violations. Its authors repeatedly acknowledged that many additional incidents almost certainly occurred but could not be verified because records had been destroyed, witnesses had died or insecurity prevented further investigation. The project itself was prompted in part by the discovery of mass graves in North Kivu, highlighting how much evidence remained hidden or undocumented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mapping-report.org/en/introduction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mapping-report.org">[mapping-report.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mapping-report.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mapping ReportJune 10, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2018</span></span></span>

## Oral testimony fills some gaps—but not all

Where archives have disappeared, oral history has become indispensable.

Researchers frequently rely on interviews with survivors, religious leaders, local officials and former combatants to reconstruct events village by village. These testimonies can identify attack dates, describe missing individuals and preserve memories that would otherwise disappear entirely.

Oral evidence has clear strengths:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* it preserves local knowledge absent from official files;
* it records experiences ignored by state institutions;
* it sometimes identifies forgotten burial locations;
* multiple independent accounts can corroborate one another.

</div>

Yet oral testimony also has unavoidable limitations. Memories fade over decades, traumatic events may be recalled differently by different witnesses, and continuing insecurity can discourage people from speaking openly. Historians therefore compare oral accounts with every surviving documentary source available rather than treating either as complete on its own.

## The role of churches, humanitarian organisations and surviving archives

Although many official archives were damaged or lost, alternative collections have survived.

Churches maintained parish registers, correspondence and mission reports in many regions throughout periods when government institutions collapsed. Humanitarian organisations recorded displacement, family separation and emergency relief operations. International agencies also preserved operational reports that now provide valuable chronological evidence.

The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), whose mandate includes helping families clarify the fate of missing persons, has developed extensive tracing systems and archives documenting people affected by armed conflict. Such records can sometimes help establish detention, separation or humanitarian contact even when state records no longer exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icrc.org/en/icrc-archives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icrc.org">[ICRC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icrc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ICRC Archives &#124; ICRCDecember 17, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 17, 2023</span></span></span>

These collections rarely provide complete answers, but together they preserve fragments of history that might otherwise have vanished.

## Can Congo's lost conflict records be recovered?

Complete recovery is unlikely.

Paper archives destroyed during fighting cannot usually be recreated, and many eyewitnesses have died. Some burial sites have been disturbed, while environmental conditions have erased physical traces of older events.

Nevertheless, reconstruction remains possible in important ways.

Modern investigations increasingly combine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* digitisation of surviving archives;
* forensic archaeology at suspected burial sites;
* satellite imagery;
* DNA identification where feasible;
* cross-checking humanitarian and church records;
* systematic collection of survivor testimony.

</div>

International experience shows that even decades after conflict, careful documentation can identify previously unknown victims and establish responsibility for specific crimes. However, it also demonstrates that no investigation can fully replace records that were never created or have been permanently lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://missingpersons.icrc.org/library/report-best-practices-matter-missing-persons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: missingpersons.icrc.org">[Missing Persons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">missingpersons.icrc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icrc.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the missing archives still matter

The disappearance of records affects far more than academic history.

Families seeking death certificates, inheritance rights or confirmation of a relative's fate often encounter the same documentary gaps that confront historians. Courts face difficulties prosecuting older crimes when evidence has vanished, while truth-seeking efforts must work around missing official documentation.

For many Congolese communities, the central mystery is therefore not whether violence occurred—its occurrence is extensively documented—but whether individual stories can ever be fully recovered. Every surviving register, missionary diary, humanitarian report or witness testimony becomes part of rebuilding a historical record that conflict attempted to erase.

The enduring puzzle is not simply how many people disappeared, but how much of their history can still be reconstructed before the remaining evidence is lost forever.
