## Introduction

“Congo” can refer to two modern states: the Democratic Republic of the Congo, formerly the Belgian Congo and Zaire, and the neighbouring Republic of the Congo, formerly French Congo. Most documentary mysteries discussed here belong to the much larger Democratic Republic, while some traditions—especially stories of unknown animals in the Congo Basin—cross modern borders.

The central difficulty is rarely a total absence of evidence. More often, there is abundant evidence that is incomplete, politically controlled, scattered between countries or compromised by the people who first investigated the event. That distinction matters: uncertainty does not make every theory equally credible.

## Lumumba’s murder: known crime, unfinished reckoning

Patrice Lumumba became the first prime minister of the independent Republic of the Congo in June 1960. Within months, the new state was fractured by army mutiny, foreign intervention, Cold War rivalry and the secession of mineral-rich Katanga. Lumumba was removed from power, arrested and transferred to Katanga, where he and two political allies, Maurice Mpolo and Joseph Okito, were executed on 17 January 1961.

The basic sequence is now well established. Lumumba was beaten, transported under guard and killed by a firing squad in the presence of Katangese and Belgian personnel. His body was later dug up, cut apart and dissolved in acid, an attempt to prevent the creation of a grave that might become a national shrine. What remains contested is the full chain of responsibility. Congolese political rivals and Katangese authorities played direct roles, but Belgian officials assisted the transfer that placed Lumumba in the hands of his enemies. A Belgian parliamentary investigation later accepted that Belgium bore “moral responsibility”, while stopping short of concluding that the Belgian government had issued a direct order to kill him. The United States Central Intelligence Agency had separately considered plans to remove Lumumba, but the existence of such schemes does not prove that an American assassination plan caused the execution that occurred in Katanga.

The unresolved issue is therefore narrower, but still important: who knew precisely what was going to happen, who authorised each stage, and whether surviving documents can establish criminal responsibility rather than broader political complicity. In March 2026, a Belgian court ordered former diplomat Étienne Davignon to stand trial over alleged involvement in Lumumba’s unlawful transfer and mistreatment. Davignon denies wrongdoing, and any trial must determine individual liability rather than simply repeat the established history of Belgian interference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ecchr.eu/en/case/crimes-during-liberation-wars-the-lumumba-murder/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecchr.eu">[ECCHR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecchr.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">crimes during liberation wars the lumumba murder</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crimes during liberation wars: The Lumumba murder50 years after Lumumba&#x27;s murder, his son filed a criminal complaint on 23 June 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2011</span></span></span>

Lumumba’s missing body deepened the mystery’s place in public memory. For decades, Congo possessed no grave and almost no physical remains of one of its founding leaders. The returned tooth consequently carried extraordinary symbolic weight. When Lumumba’s mausoleum in Kinshasa was vandalised in 2024, uncertainty over whether the tooth had been taken briefly recreated the original fear that even the last trace of him could disappear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/383a9c9d7099a1f6d83e727667fd6c9e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Four suspects were arrested, but it is unclear if the tooth was stolen. The credible conclusion is not that Lumumba’s death remains an entirely unsolved murder. The killers, location and broad political circumstances are known. The enduring mystery lies in the still-incomplete record of foreign decision-making, responsibility within Belgian institutions and the destruction of evidence intended to erase the victim.

## The Hammarskjöld crash: accident or attack?

Shortly after midnight on 18 September 1961, an aircraft carrying United Nations Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld crashed near Ndola, then in Northern Rhodesia and now in Zambia. Hammarskjöld was travelling to negotiate with Moïse Tshombe, leader of secessionist Katanga, during the Congo Crisis. All sixteen people aboard died.

An early Rhodesian inquiry favoured pilot error. A later United Nations investigation did not rule out accident but left open the possibility of sabotage or attack. Doubts grew because witness testimony, intelligence information and reports of another aircraft near Hammarskjöld’s plane were either discounted, unavailable or poorly examined at the time.

The case has since produced claims involving mercenary pilots, Katangese forces, Western intelligence services and mining interests that feared the United Nations would end Katanga’s secession. Some accounts allege that Hammarskjöld’s aircraft was fired upon; others suggest an attempted interception that caused the pilots to lose control. These theories remain unproven, but they are not based solely on folklore. Later reviews identified evidence judged sufficient to justify continued investigation, including reports of aerial activity and possible communications intercepts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.un.org/en/delegate/63-years-later-mystery-still-surrounds-death-dag-hammarskj%C3%B6ld" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: un.org">[United Nations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">63 years later mystery still surrounds death dag hammarskjöld</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations63 years later, mystery still surrounds death of Dag...29 Oct 2024 — The 1961 plane crash that killed Secretary-General Da...</span></span></span>

The main obstacle is state secrecy. The United Nations has repeatedly asked governments to search intelligence, military and diplomatic archives for relevant records. Some states have cooperated more fully than others, and investigators have warned that potentially significant documents may still exist in classified collections. In 2024, the United Nations described the cause of the crash as an enduring mystery and continued to treat an external attack or threat as a plausible possibility requiring further evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.un.org/en/delegate/63-years-later-mystery-still-surrounds-death-dag-hammarskj%C3%B6ld" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: un.org">[United Nations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">63 years later mystery still surrounds death dag hammarskjöld</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations63 years later, mystery still surrounds death of Dag...29 Oct 2024 — The 1961 plane crash that killed Secretary-General Da...</span></span></span>

No surviving piece of evidence conclusively proves that the aircraft was shot down. Equally, the original pilot-error explanation cannot safely be treated as final. The first investigation took place in a colonial political environment closely connected to the Katanga conflict, and its limitations have become part of the case.

Although the crash occurred outside Congo, it belongs centrally to Congolese history. Hammarskjöld died while attempting to resolve a struggle over Congolese sovereignty, foreign-backed secession and control of mineral wealth. The mystery also shows how Congo’s crises extended through regional airspace, European capitals, intelligence networks and international institutions.

## Who really organised Kabila’s assassination?

President Laurent-Désiré [Kabila]({{ 'kabila/' | relative_url }}) was shot inside the presidential palace in Kinshasa on 16 January 2001. The alleged gunman, a young bodyguard named Rashidi Kasereka, was killed almost immediately. Kabila died soon afterwards, and his son Joseph Kabila assumed the presidency.

The official case presented the killing as part of a conspiracy involving soldiers and officials close to the president. Dozens of defendants were tried before a military court, and many received death sentences. Yet major doubts surrounded the proceedings. Amnesty International reported serious concerns about fairness, including the defendants’ access to lawyers, the use of military justice and the reliability of evidence in a trial with potentially fatal consequences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/afr62/023/2002/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">trial and the possibly imminent passing of death</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalDemocratic Republic of Congo: From assassination to state...12 Dec 2002 — Almost two years on from the assassinatio...</span></span></span>

Because the alleged assassin was killed at the scene, investigators lost the person best placed to explain motive, planning and possible accomplices. Rival explanations subsequently blamed disaffected child soldiers, senior members of Kabila’s security establishment, foreign governments and actors connected to the regional war then raging across Congo. None has been demonstrated conclusively.

The political setting made certainty especially difficult. Kabila had taken power in 1997 with support from Rwanda and Uganda, then broken with his former allies. By 2001, Congo’s war involved several neighbouring states, Congolese rebel organisations and competing military and commercial networks. A palace murder in such circumstances could plausibly have arisen from a personal grievance, an internal power struggle, a military conspiracy or external sponsorship.

The convictions did not settle these questions. Human rights organisations and the Catholic Church criticised the process, while some prisoners consistently maintained that they had been used as scapegoats. In 2021, President Félix Tshisekedi pardoned at least 26 people convicted in connection with the assassination, including Colonel Eddy Kapend, who had been portrayed as a central organiser. The pardons did not declare them innocent, but they reinforced the public sense that the trial had failed to establish a persuasive account of the crime.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/congo-president-pardons-soldiers-jailed-over-kabila-assassination-2021-01-02/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Congo president pardons soldiers jailed over Kabila assassination</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congo president pardons soldiers jailed over Kabila assassination</span></span></span>

The safest assessment is that Kasereka probably fired the fatal shots, but the evidence identifying those who directed or enabled him remains weak and politically compromised. The central mystery is not the identity of the man in the room; it is whether he acted alone and why he was immediately silenced.

## What was the Ishango bone used for?

The Ishango bone is a small engraved artefact discovered in 1950 near the Semliki River, in what is now eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo. It bears carefully grouped notches arranged in columns and has often been described as one of the earliest known objects displaying organised numerical thought.

Its date is not perfectly settled because volcanic activity complicated the dating of the archaeological layers, but it is generally placed many thousands of years before the earliest written number systems. The patterning is deliberate enough that most serious interpretations treat the marks as more than accidental scratches. What cannot be recovered is the system understood by the person who made them.

Proposed explanations include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a tally or counting device;
* a reference table involving doubling or multiplication;
* a number system organised around twelve, three and four;
* a record connected to lunar cycles;
* a ceremonial or narrative object whose pattern had symbolic rather than purely mathematical meaning.

</div>

Supporters of the base-twelve interpretation point to repeated numerical groupings and apparent relationships between columns. Other researchers argue that such readings risk selecting patterns that look meaningful to modern mathematicians while ignoring alternative arrangements. Even authors who favour a sophisticated mathematical function acknowledge that the evidence is insufficient to prove knowledge of prime numbers or any single computational method.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.1019" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

A 2025 analysis proposed that irregularities in the groupings could form part of a deliberate internal structure, possibly used as a reference system with movable markers. It offered a fresh reason to regard the design as highly organised, but presented its conclusions as a working hypothesis rather than a decipherment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06412" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Decoding the Ishango Bone: Unveiling Prehistoric Mathematical Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Decoding the Ishango Bone: Unveiling Prehistoric Mathematical Art</span></span></span>

The Ishango bone is therefore not a “code” awaiting one obvious solution. Without accompanying writing, comparable objects or an uninterrupted cultural tradition explaining its use, no interpretation can be confirmed. Its importance survives that uncertainty. It demonstrates purposeful notation in prehistoric Central Africa and challenges older histories that treated mathematical thinking as if it had begun only in a small number of literate ancient societies.

The mystery also has a colonial dimension. The original artefact is held in Belgium, far from the community and landscape in which it was found. Questions about its meaning are consequently linked to wider debates about ownership, access and who has authority to interpret Congolese heritage.

## The missing record of the Congo Free State

Between 1885 and 1908, most of what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo was controlled as the Congo Free State, the personal domain of Belgium’s King Leopold II. Forced labour, hostage-taking, mutilation, village destruction, hunger and epidemic disease caused catastrophic population loss.

There is no credible doubt about the violence. Missionary testimony, official inquiries, photographs, correspondence and administrative records document a system built around coercive extraction, particularly of rubber. The unresolved question is numerical: how many people died, and how much of the demographic collapse resulted directly from killing, as opposed to starvation, displacement, falling birth rates and disease intensified by colonial rule?

The frequently repeated figure of ten million deaths is an estimate, not a count drawn from a reliable census. No comprehensive population census existed before Leopold’s rule, and later figures were themselves incomplete. Historians therefore reconstruct the scale of loss from local studies, administrative reports, demographic assumptions and retrospective comparisons. The destruction, concealment and dispersal of records make precision impossible.

This uncertainty is sometimes misused in two opposite ways. One is to present a single number as definitively proved. The other is to suggest that disagreement over the number casts doubt on the underlying atrocities. It does not. The archive supports a vast and systematic humanitarian disaster even though it cannot supply a final death toll.

Researchers continue to find underused Congo Free State material in Belgian public, missionary and institutional collections. The AfricaMuseum has acknowledged that little-known or unpublished archives can still change understanding of how the regime worked and how Africans resisted it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.africamuseum.be/en/research/news/The_Congo_Free_State" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africamuseum.be">[Royal Museum for Central Africa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africamuseum.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on africamuseum.be.</span></span></span>

The archival puzzle remains highly current. Belgium holds extensive administrative, photographic, cartographic and geological records concerning Congo. The Belgian State Archives describes itself as the country’s main repository for colonial government records, while other enormous collections remain at the AfricaMuseum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.arch.be/index.php?l=en&amp;m=online-resources&amp;r=archives-related-to-the-colonial-era&amp;sr=the-archives-about-the-history-of-colonisation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arch.be">[Arch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arch.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arch.be.</span></span></span>

In 2026, a dispute intensified over millions of colonial-era geological documents sought by the Congolese government and a private mineral-exploration company. Belgium argued that no foreign company should receive privileged access and proposed institutional digitisation instead; Congolese officials pressed for faster access to information about their own territory. The dispute turned old paper maps and rock samples into a modern question of sovereignty, mineral wealth and control of historical knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ft.com/content/18e96774-a526-45d0-8b0b-3f005b5b0831" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ft.com">[ft.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Financial Times Belgian museum caught in row over millions of DR Congo records</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The archives, spanning 500 meters of files, include valuable geoscientific data on copper, cobalt, and lithium – critical minerals for gl...</span></span></span>

This is a different kind of lost treasure story. The “treasure” is information—records of land, minerals, labour and colonial decisions—held partly beyond Congo’s borders. What remains unknown may affect not only the interpretation of the past but the exploitation of resources in the present.

## The Congo dinosaur: folklore reshaped as modern myth

Perhaps the best-known popular “mystery” of the Congo Basin is the supposed creature commonly portrayed as a surviving long-necked dinosaur inhabiting remote rivers and swamps. Western accounts developed during the early twentieth century, when reports of large unknown animals were combined with Europe and America’s growing fascination with dinosaurs.

Expeditions have collected stories and eyewitness claims but no bones, carcasses, clear photographs, environmental DNA or other reproducible evidence of a large unknown reptile. Suggested identifications have included elephants, hippopotamuses, crocodiles, rhinoceroses remembered from older traditions, unfamiliar fish and spiritual beings never intended to be understood as zoological species.

The largest distortion came when outsiders translated varied local traditions into a single creature resembling a sauropod dinosaur. This process often stripped stories of their religious, metaphorical or social meaning. Later cryptozoological expeditions sometimes asked witnesses leading questions or showed illustrations of prehistoric animals, making apparently consistent testimony less reliable.

The legend also became attached to young-Earth creationism. Some expeditions openly sought a living dinosaur because they believed it would disprove evolutionary science, although the survival of an ancient lineage would not itself overturn evolution. Creationist organisations have promoted this motive directly, showing that the investigation began with an ideological objective rather than neutral zoological evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icr.org/article/306/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icr.org">[Institute for Creation Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icr.org.</span></span></span>

The lack of physical evidence is especially significant because the alleged animal is usually described as elephant-sized. Congo Basin forests certainly contain poorly documented species, but a breeding population of enormous air-breathing vertebrates should leave tracks, remains, feeding traces, dung and genetic material. Repeated searches have produced none.

The responsible conclusion is that this is a genuine tradition and a revealing episode in cultural history, but not a strong biological mystery. Its modern form reflects the meeting of Central African oral narratives, colonial ideas about an “unknown” continent, popular dinosaur imagery and outsiders’ desire to discover a lost world. Treating it simply as a monster hunt obscures the more interesting question: how many distinct local stories were reshaped into the supposedly universal legend known abroad today?

## Why Congo’s mysteries remain difficult to settle

These cases share several obstacles. Political killings were investigated by governments or institutions with interests in the outcome. Bodies and documents were deliberately destroyed. Relevant archives were divided between Congo, Belgium, Britain, the United States, the United Nations and other states. Oral testimony was recorded late or filtered through interpreters. Colonial officials preserved the records useful to their administrations while leaving many Congolese experiences unnamed or absent.

The result is not an empty historical record but an uneven one. It can establish that Lumumba was murdered through a combination of Congolese and foreign action, while leaving individual legal responsibility contested. It can show that Kabila’s assassination trial was unreliable without proving a particular alternative conspiracy. It can justify renewed investigation of Hammarskjöld’s crash without declaring that his aircraft was shot down. It can demonstrate intentional patterning on the Ishango bone without translating the pattern into a definite prehistoric calculation.

Congo’s greatest historical enigmas therefore reward careful distinctions. A documented gap is not proof of a secret plot. A failed investigation is not proof that every suspect was innocent. A meaningful artefact need not have one recoverable meaning. A powerful legend need not describe a real animal.

What keeps these mysteries alive is their connection to unresolved questions of sovereignty and memory: who controlled Congo, who benefited from its resources, who was permitted to write its history, and where the surviving evidence is kept. In Congo, solving the past often depends not upon discovering a dramatic hidden clue, but upon opening archives, testing inherited stories and returning authority over the historical record to the people whose history it contains.

    var exactMarket = '';
    var languageMarket = '';
    try {
      if (navigator.languages && navigator.languages.length) languages = Array.prototype.slice.call(navigator.languages);
      else if (navigator.language) languages = [navigator.language];
    } catch (err) {}
    for (var i = 0; i< languages.length; i += 1) {
      var normalized = normalize(languages[i]);
      if (!normalized) continue;
      if (localeMarketMap[normalized]) {
        exactMarket = localeMarketMap[normalized];
        break;
      }
      var languageOnly = normalized.split('-')[0];
      if (!languageMarket && localeMarketMap[languageOnly]) languageMarket = localeMarketMap[languageOnly];
    }
    return { exact: exactMarket, language: languageMarket };
  }
  function inferFromLocale() {
    var matches = localeMatches();
    return matches.exact || matches.language || '';
  }
  function inferFromTimezone() {
    var tz = '';
    try { tz = String(Intl.DateTimeFormat().resolvedOptions().timeZone || ''); } catch (err) {}
    if (!tz) return '';
    for (var i = 0; i< timezoneRules.length; i += 1) {
      var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {
        if (new RegExp(rule.pattern).test(tz)) return rule.market;
      } catch (err) {}
    }
    return '';
  }

    if (localeMarkets.exact && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.exact) >= 0) return localeMarkets.exact;
    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
    if (options.defaultMarket && available.indexOf(options.defaultMarket) >= 0) return options.defaultMarket;
    return available[0] || '';
  }
