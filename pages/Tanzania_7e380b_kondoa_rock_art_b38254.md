## Introduction

The short answer is that nobody knows exactly who painted the rock shelters of Kondoa. Archaeologists agree that the paintings were not created by a single people or during a single period. Instead, the hundreds of decorated shelters in central [Tanzania]({{ 'what-still-mystifies-us-about-tanzanias/' | relative_url }}) preserve layers of artwork made over many centuries by different communities, including hunter-gatherers, later pastoralists and eventually farming societies. The mystery lies in separating those layers, identifying their creators and understanding what the images originally meant. Although the sites have been used for at least two thousand years and perhaps much longer, securely linking individual paintings to named historical groups remains one of East Africa's most challenging archaeological puzzles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreKondoa Rock-Art Sites - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 12, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 12, 2006</span></span></span>

The Kondoa Rock-Art Sites, recognised as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, are valuable precisely because they preserve evidence of cultural change rather than a single artistic tradition. Their paintings reveal shifting ways of life, yet the identities of many of the artists remain uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreKondoa Rock-Art Sites - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 12, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 12, 2006</span></span></span>

## What the paintings show

Across the Kondoa landscape, more than 150 documented painted shelters contain thousands of images. Many are superimposed, with newer paintings covering older ones, making it clear that generations of artists returned to the same sacred places rather than creating the art all at once.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreKondoa Rock-Art Sites - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 12, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 12, 2006</span></span></span>

The paintings include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Wild animals such as giraffes, elephants, antelope and buffalo.
* Human figures, sometimes dancing, hunting or carrying bows.
* Geometric symbols and abstract patterns.
* White, red, black and occasionally yellow pigments.
* Later images of domesticated cattle, indicating pastoral societies.

</div>

The contrast between wildlife-dominated scenes and paintings featuring livestock is one of the strongest clues that different communities used the shelters over long periods. UNESCO describes the sequence as an exceptional record of changing societies, from hunter-gatherers to agro-pastoral communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreKondoa Rock-Art Sites - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 12, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 12, 2006</span></span></span>

## Why dating the images is difficult

The greatest obstacle to identifying the artists is that the paintings themselves are extremely difficult to date.

Unlike charcoal drawings, most Kondoa paintings were made using mineral pigments such as red ochre, which contain little or no organic material suitable for radiocarbon dating. Researchers therefore rely on indirect evidence, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The order in which paintings overlap each other.
* Weathering and fading.
* Archaeological finds from nearby excavations.
* The appearance of animals, weapons and domestic livestock.
* Comparisons with rock art elsewhere in eastern and southern Africa.

</div>

These methods provide relative dates rather than precise calendar years. As a result, estimates for the oldest paintings have varied enormously. Some early researchers suggested extreme ages stretching back tens of thousands of years, while more cautious archaeological interpretations place the earliest surviving art at around 3,000 years ago or somewhat earlier. There is broad agreement that the shelters continued to be painted well into the recent past, meaning the art represents a very long tradition rather than a single prehistoric event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africanrockart.britishmuseum.org/country/tanzania/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanrockart.britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanrockart.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">African Rock Art Tanzania</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">African Rock Art Tanzania</span></span></span>

Excavations at the nearby Kisese II Rockshelter illustrate the problem. The site preserves evidence of human occupation spanning at least 45,000 years and also contains rock art, but demonstrating exactly which occupants painted which images remains difficult because habitation layers and painted surfaces cannot automatically be matched.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.aber.ac.uk/en/publications/kisese-ii-rockshelter-tanzania/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University Kisese II Rockshelter, Tanzania</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ystwyth UniversityKisese II Rockshelter, Tanzania - Aberystwyth University...</span></span></span>

## Competing ideas about the artists and their meaning

Rather than one accepted answer, archaeologists consider several overlapping explanations.

### Hunter-gatherer artists

Many specialists believe the oldest paintings were produced by hunter-gatherers related culturally, though not necessarily directly, to the ancestors of today's Sandawe and possibly other foraging peoples once widespread across central Tanzania.

This interpretation is based on:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Similar artistic styles found across hunter-gatherer rock art in eastern and southern Africa.
* The prominence of wild animals rather than domestic livestock.
* Fine-line painting techniques characteristic of hunter-gatherer traditions.
* The geographical distribution of similar imagery.

</div>

However, no surviving historical record identifies individual artists, and modern communities cannot simply be assumed to be the direct descendants of those who made every painting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africanrockart.britishmuseum.org/country/tanzania/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanrockart.britishmuseum.org">[African Rock Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanrockart.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">African Rock Art Tanzania</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">African Rock Art Tanzania</span></span></span>

### Pastoral communities

Another clearly recognisable layer appears to belong to pastoral societies that introduced cattle into the region.

These paintings often differ in both subject matter and style. Cattle become central figures, and human figures are sometimes depicted differently from those in earlier hunter-gatherer scenes. Their appearance reflects wider social changes as herding spread through eastern Africa. UNESCO specifically recognises the Kondoa sequence as documenting this transition from hunter-gatherer to agro-pastoral life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreKondoa Rock-Art Sites - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 12, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 12, 2006</span></span></span>

### Farming societies and continued use

Some shelters also contain later paintings associated with farming communities. Importantly, the shelters did not simply become archaeological sites after the last images were painted.

Several continue to have ritual importance for local people, including ceremonies connected with rainmaking, healing and divination. This continuity complicates attempts to separate ancient beliefs from more recent traditions, since sacred places often remain meaningful long after their original creators are forgotten.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreKondoa Rock-Art Sites - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 12, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 12, 2006</span></span></span>

## Why meaning is almost as mysterious as authorship

Knowing who painted the shelters would also help explain why they were painted, but the images themselves rarely provide straightforward answers.

Earlier interpretations often treated the paintings as simple hunting records. More recent research across African rock art suggests they probably served multiple purposes, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* Religious or ceremonial practices.
* Expressions of spiritual beliefs.
* Marking important places in the landscape.
* Recording social identity.
* Reinforcing shared traditions across generations.

</div>

The same shelter may contain paintings created centuries apart for entirely different reasons. Superimposed images show that later artists sometimes respected, reused or deliberately painted over earlier work, suggesting that these places retained spiritual or social importance even as populations changed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreKondoa Rock-Art Sites - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 12, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 12, 2006</span></span></span>

## What the evidence supports today

Modern scholarship is cautious about assigning the entire Kondoa corpus to any single ethnic group or historical population.

The strongest conclusions supported by current evidence are:

* The paintings were created over many centuries rather than during one period.
* Multiple communities contributed to the art, including hunter-gatherers, pastoralists and later farming groups.
* Individual paintings cannot usually be linked with certainty to specific named peoples.
* Dating remains approximate because direct scientific dating of most pigments is difficult.
* The shelters preserve evidence of cultural continuity as well as cultural change, with some locations remaining ritually significant into the present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreKondoa Rock-Art Sites - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 12, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 12, 2006</span></span></span>

## Kondoa Art in the wider historical record
The mystery of who painted Kondoa's rock shelters is important because it reflects a broader challenge in African archaeology: material evidence often survives far better than written history. The paintings reveal that central Tanzania witnessed repeated waves of cultural change, but they do not identify every artist by name.

Rather than diminishing their significance, that uncertainty makes the shelters especially valuable. Each new archaeological excavation, improved dating technique or study of regional settlement patterns has the potential to clarify another part of the story. For now, the best-supported interpretation is that Kondoa's remarkable galleries are the collective work of many generations whose identities can only be reconstructed through careful comparison of art, archaeology and living cultural traditions, not through a single definitive answer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreKondoa Rock-Art Sites - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 12, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 12, 2006</span></span></span>
