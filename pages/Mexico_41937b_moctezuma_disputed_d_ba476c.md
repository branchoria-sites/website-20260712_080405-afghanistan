## Introduction

Moctezuma II, the ruler of the Mexica Empire, died in late June 1520 while being held captive by Hernán Cortés's forces in Tenochtitlan. Nearly five centuries later, historians still cannot say with confidence exactly how he died. The mystery does not arise because the event is poorly documented, but because the surviving accounts directly contradict one another. Spanish eyewitnesses claimed that Moctezuma was fatally wounded by stones thrown by his own people after he tried to calm an uprising. Indigenous traditions recorded after the conquest instead accused the Spaniards of killing him once he had ceased to be useful as a hostage. Both versions were shaped by political interests, memory and the trauma of conquest, leaving no definitive verdict. The debate remains one of [Mexico]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-mexicos-past/' | relative_url }})'s most enduring historical mysteries because it raises broader questions about whose testimony can be trusted when history is written by both victors and survivors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26126401/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Did Emperor Moctezuma II&#x27;s head injury and subsequent death hasten the fall of the Aztec nation? - PubMedJuly 1, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2015</span></span></span>

## What is known about his final days?

Although the cause of Moctezuma's death remains disputed, the broad sequence of events is well established. After Cortés seized him in November 1519, Moctezuma remained effectively a prisoner while continuing to serve as the public face of imperial authority. During the growing rebellion against the Spanish occupation, he became increasingly isolated, caught between the demands of his captors and the anger of many of his own people.

By June 1520, fighting had engulfed Tenochtitlan. According to multiple sources, the Spaniards brought the captive ruler onto a rooftop or terrace overlooking a crowd, hoping his authority would persuade the defenders to stop attacking. Instead, the confrontation ended disastrously. Within days Moctezuma was dead, and shortly afterwards the Spanish were forced into their disastrous retreat from the city during the episode later known as the *Noche Triste* ("Sad Night").<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26126401/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Did Emperor Moctezuma II&#x27;s head injury and subsequent death hasten the fall of the Aztec nation? - PubMedJuly 1, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2015</span></span></span>

What remains uncertain is what happened after Moctezuma appeared before the crowd. The surviving narratives diverge sharply at precisely this crucial moment.

### The Spanish version

Spanish narratives, including those associated with Cortés and later with Bernal Díaz del Castillo, maintain that the fatal injuries came from the Mexica defenders themselves. In this telling, Moctezuma attempted to calm the crowd but instead became the target of stones and missiles. His wounds—particularly a reported head injury—proved fatal after several days.

This account served an obvious purpose. If accepted, responsibility for the emperor's death rested with his own subjects rather than with the Spanish commanders who had held him hostage for months. At the same time, it portrayed Cortés as having tried unsuccessfully to preserve both Moctezuma's life and political authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26126401/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Did Emperor Moctezuma II&#x27;s head injury and subsequent death hasten the fall of the Aztec nation? - PubMedJuly 1, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2015</span></span></span>

### The Indigenous tradition

Indigenous accounts preserved in the *Florentine Codex*, compiled under the direction of the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún using Nahua testimony, present a strikingly different narrative. Rather than describing a fatal attack by the crowd, Book 12 recounts that the Spaniards killed Moctezuma and another captive ruler before throwing their bodies outside the palace for recovery by their people. Other colonial-era Indigenous traditions mention stabbing or strangulation rather than injuries from stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historians.org/resource/the-death-of-moctezuma/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historians.org">[AHA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historians.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AHAThe Death of Moctezuma (Florentine Codex) – AHA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AHAThe Death of Moctezuma (Florentine Codex) – AHA</span></span></span>

These accounts were also shaped by later circumstances. They were recorded decades after the conquest within colonial New Spain, drawing upon oral histories, pictorial traditions and community memory rather than immediate written testimony. They therefore preserve important Indigenous perspectives while still requiring careful historical evaluation.

## Why do the sources disagree?

The contradictory accounts reflect more than simple disagreement. Every surviving narrative emerged from authors with powerful reasons to interpret events in particular ways.

For the Spanish, admitting that captive rulers had been deliberately killed could have damaged Cortés's reputation and potentially exposed him to legal or political consequences before the Spanish Crown. Portraying Moctezuma as rejected by his own people instead reinforced the idea that Spanish actions had not caused his death.

For Indigenous communities, Moctezuma's death became part of a wider story of invasion, captivity and betrayal. Their narratives emphasised Spanish violence rather than internal division within the Mexica leadership. These accounts also reflected the painful process of explaining an unprecedented military catastrophe through collective memory.

Modern historians therefore avoid treating either tradition as completely objective. Instead, they compare multiple independent sources, examine when each account was written, identify political motives and consider where different traditions overlap despite their disagreements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historians.org/resource/the-death-of-moctezuma/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historians.org">[AHA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historians.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AHAThe Death of Moctezuma (Florentine Codex) – AHA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AHAThe Death of Moctezuma (Florentine Codex) – AHA</span></span></span>

## Could the reported injuries have been fatal?

One modern attempt to examine the problem approached it from medicine rather than traditional historical interpretation. A clinical review published in *Neurosurgical Focus* analysed descriptions of Moctezuma's reported head wound and concluded that the injuries described in Spanish accounts were medically capable of causing death.

The study argues that a severe head injury from a stone projected by a sling could plausibly explain the emperor's decline over several days. However, the author also stresses that historical certainty remains impossible because the medical analysis depends entirely upon contradictory written descriptions rather than physical evidence. There is no surviving body, no autopsy and no contemporary clinical record. The medical assessment therefore shows that the Spanish explanation is physically possible, not that it has been conclusively proven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26126401/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Did Emperor Moctezuma II&#x27;s head injury and subsequent death hasten the fall of the Aztec nation? - PubMedJuly 1, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2015</span></span></span>

## Why a definitive verdict remains unlikely

Several factors make the mystery effectively unsolvable.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **No independent eyewitness record** survives from a neutral observer.
* **Every written account had political or cultural motivations** that could influence how events were described.
* **The body cannot be examined**, removing the possibility of modern forensic analysis.
* **The surviving sources were written or recorded under very different circumstances**, making direct comparison difficult.
* **Some narratives evolved over decades**, blending eyewitness testimony, oral tradition and later interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historians.org/resource/the-death-of-moctezuma/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historians.org">[AHA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historians.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AHAThe Death of Moctezuma (Florentine Codex) – AHA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AHAThe Death of Moctezuma (Florentine Codex) – AHA</span></span></span>

</div>

Rather than asking which single account is completely true, many historians instead ask which details appear consistently across different traditions. Most agree on several core facts: Moctezuma died while under Spanish captivity during the siege of Tenochtitlan, his death followed an attempt to use him as an intermediary, and neither side could preserve his authority by that stage of the conflict. Beyond those points, certainty fades rapidly.

## Why the mystery still matters

The debate over Moctezuma's death is about more than identifying a killer. It also shapes how the conquest of Mexico itself is understood.

If the Spanish version is accepted, Moctezuma appears as a ruler abandoned by his own people after losing legitimacy. If the Indigenous version is preferred, he becomes a captive monarch executed by foreign conquerors when his political usefulness ended. Each interpretation carries different implications for responsibility, resistance and memory.

The mystery also illustrates a broader challenge in studying the conquest of Mexico. Much of what is known comes from competing narratives written by people who experienced the same traumatic events from radically different perspectives. Historians therefore treat Moctezuma's death not as a puzzle likely to be solved by a single new discovery, but as a case demonstrating how evidence, memory and political interests can shape the historical record itself.
