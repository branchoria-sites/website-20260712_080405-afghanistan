## Introduction

The Treasure of Lima is one of the world's most famous lost-treasure stories, and [Costa Rica]({{ 'what-really-lies-behind-costa-ricas/' | relative_url }})'s remote Cocos Island is its best-known supposed hiding place. Yet the closer historians examine the evidence, the weaker the documented case becomes. There is little dispute that Cocos Island attracted pirates, privateers, whalers and sailors seeking fresh water over several centuries. What remains unproven is the central claim that an enormous cache of colonial gold, silver and jewels was secretly buried there in 1820 and never recovered. Instead, the legend rests on overlapping narratives, inconsistent witness accounts, missing documentation and repeated retellings that gradually transformed a plausible historical episode into a global treasure myth.

The mystery therefore lies less in whether treasure hunters have searched the island—they certainly have—than in whether the treasure was ever there in the first place.

## How the Treasure Story Developed

The standard version of the legend begins during the struggle for independence in Spanish South America around 1820. According to the familiar story, Spanish authorities in Lima assembled enormous quantities of church and state valuables for transport to safety. They entrusted the cargo to the British merchant captain William Thompson aboard the *Mary Dear*. Thompson supposedly murdered the Spanish guards, diverted the ship to Cocos Island, buried the treasure, and was later captured before escaping with only the secret of its location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/jimdobson/2019/08/27/a-one-billion-dollar-treasure-is-buried-on-this-island-in-costa-rica-and-you-will-never-find-it/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forbes.com">[Forbes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forbes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A $1 Billion Treasure Is Buried On This Island In Costa Rica (And You Will Never Find It)August 27, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 27, 2019</span></span></span>

This narrative has become so widely repeated that it is often presented as established history. Documentary evidence, however, is far less secure.

Several problems appear when researchers compare different versions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The estimated value of the treasure varies enormously between accounts.
* Inventories of the cargo differ, with some including spectacular objects such as a life-sized golden statue of the Virgin Mary while others omit them.
* The details of Thompson's capture, trial and escape change significantly from one retelling to another.
* Contemporary official records confirming the complete sequence have proved elusive.

</div>

These inconsistencies suggest that the modern legend was assembled from several strands of historical memory rather than preserved through a single continuous documentary record.

Adding further confusion, Cocos Island became linked not only with the Treasure of Lima but also with supposed hoards left by other pirates, including Benito Bonito and Captain Bennett Graham. Over time, separate treasure traditions became intertwined, making it increasingly difficult to distinguish one legend from another. ## What Documentary Evidence Actually Exists?

The strongest documented facts concern the island itself rather than the treasure.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://explorecostarica.org/cocos-the-treasure-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: explorecostarica.org">[explorecostarica.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">explorecostarica.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

Historical records show that Cocos Island was a well-known stopping point for ships crossing the eastern Pacific. Its abundant fresh water made it an attractive refuge for pirates, whalers and commercial vessels from the seventeenth century onwards. There is therefore nothing implausible about valuables being hidden there on occasion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/820/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cocos Island National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCocos Island National Park - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

The documentary case becomes much weaker when historians look specifically for evidence of the Treasure of Lima.

The principal difficulties include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **No surviving verified burial record.** No authenticated contemporary document records treasure being buried on Cocos Island.
* **No confirmed Spanish recovery expedition.** Stories of Spanish attempts to recover the treasure rely largely on later narratives rather than securely documented official correspondence.
* **Late appearance of detailed accounts.** Many colourful versions emerged decades after the alleged events, increasing the possibility of embellishment.
* **Conflicting maps and directions.** Numerous supposed treasure maps exist, but their origins are difficult to establish and they frequently contradict one another.

</div>

None of these gaps proves the treasure never existed. They do, however, mean historians cannot treat the standard story as firmly documented history.

## What Searchers Actually Found

The absence of convincing discoveries is one reason the mystery has endured.

From the nineteenth century onward, hundreds of expeditions reportedly searched Cocos Island. Some were organised by private adventurers, while others received official permission from Costa Rica before modern conservation laws prohibited treasure hunting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.enjoycostarica.org/cocos-island-national-park/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: enjoycostarica.org">[Enjoy Costa Rica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">enjoycostarica.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enjoy Costa Rica Cocos Island National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enjoy Costa RicaCocos Island National Park - Enjoy Costa Rica...</span></span></span>

The most famous searcher was the German adventurer August Gissler, who settled on the island in the late nineteenth century after being appointed governor of a short-lived colony. He spent nearly two decades excavating caves, valleys and ravines in pursuit of the Treasure of Lima.

Despite extraordinary persistence, Gissler recovered only a handful of coins and small artefacts rather than the enormous cache promised by legend. Those finds demonstrate that isolated historic objects reached the island at some point, but they fall far short of proving that the legendary treasure was ever buried there. Subsequent expeditions likewise produced dramatic claims but no independently verified recovery of a vast colonial hoard.

## Why the Legend Persisted Despite Thin Evidence

Several factors explain the remarkable durability of the story.

First, Cocos Island is physically suited to treasure legends. Dense rainforest, steep valleys, caves and heavy rainfall constantly reshape the landscape. Even unsuccessful expeditions can plausibly argue that erosion, landslides or vegetation concealed the correct location.

Second, every failed expedition can be interpreted in two opposite ways. Sceptics see repeated failure as evidence that no treasure exists. Believers argue that failure merely proves the hiding place has not yet been found.

Third, later writers often repeated earlier stories without checking their documentary foundations. As details accumulated through books, magazines and popular histories, repetition gradually created an impression of historical certainty.

Finally, the island's genuine maritime history gave the legend an authentic framework. Since pirates and privateers really did visit Cocos Island, fictional or exaggerated treasure stories appeared more believable than they might elsewhere.

## Missing Records and Competing Explanations

Historians generally consider several possibilities more plausible than the traditional treasure narrative.

One possibility is that some valuables were hidden temporarily on Cocos Island, but in far smaller quantities than later legend claimed.

Another is that the treasure was recovered long ago by those who buried it, leaving behind only stories that grew with repeated retelling.

A third explanation is that different historical events became merged. Genuine pirate caches, temporary stores of supplies and rumours surrounding colonial wealth may have combined into a single grand narrative during the nineteenth century.

The most sceptical interpretation argues that although valuable cargo certainly left Lima during the independence wars, there is insufficient documentary evidence to conclude that the famous Treasure of Lima was ever deposited on Cocos Island at all.

Current evidence cannot decisively distinguish between these possibilities because the surviving documentary record is incomplete and many supposed primary sources either cannot be verified or appear only in much later publications.

## Why Treasure Hunting Ended but the Mystery Survives

Modern Cocos Island is valued primarily for its ecological importance rather than its legends. Costa Rica declared it a national park in 1978, and UNESCO recognised it as a World Heritage Site in 1997 because of its exceptional natural environment. Treasure hunting is no longer permitted, and conservation has replaced excavation as the island's principal purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/820/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Cocos Island National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCocos Island National Park - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

This shift has effectively frozen the historical debate. Without new excavations, any dramatic breakthrough is unlikely to come from digging. Instead, the best hope for resolving the mystery would probably be the discovery of overlooked archival records in Spain, Peru, Britain or Costa Rica that clarify what was actually transported from Lima and whether any contemporary witness ever documented its burial.

## The Historical Balance

The Treasure of Lima remains one of Costa Rica's best-known historical mysteries precisely because it sits on the boundary between documented history and enduring folklore.

It is well established that Cocos Island served generations of sailors, pirates and explorers. It is equally well established that countless treasure hunters searched there over more than a century. What has never been demonstrated is the existence of reliable contemporary evidence showing that the legendary Treasure of Lima was buried on the island.

The mystery therefore survives not because archaeologists have failed to uncover overwhelming evidence, but because the documentary record itself remains incomplete, contradictory and surprisingly fragile beneath one of history's most famous treasure stories.
