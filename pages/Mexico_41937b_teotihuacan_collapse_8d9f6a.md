## Introduction

Why did Teotihuacan, one of the largest and most influential cities in the ancient Americas, fall apart? The short answer is that nobody knows with certainty. Archaeologists agree that the city's political system collapsed during the sixth century CE and that many of its most important ceremonial and administrative buildings were deliberately burned or dismantled. What remains disputed is why this happened. Evidence points towards a combination of internal political conflict, growing social inequality, environmental stress and regional instability rather than a single catastrophic event. The mystery endures because Teotihuacan's rulers left no surviving written history explaining the crisis, forcing researchers to reconstruct events from burnt buildings, broken monuments, environmental records and changing settlement patterns. The result is one of [Mexico]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-mexicos-past/' | relative_url }})'s most important archaeological puzzles.

## What archaeology reveals about the destruction

The strongest evidence comes not from legends but from the city's ruins. Around the middle of the sixth century CE, major public buildings along the Avenue of the Dead and the ceremonial centre suffered extensive burning. Archaeologists have identified deliberate destruction rather than accidental fire: sculptures were smashed, elite compounds damaged, and more than a hundred important structures were burned in what some researchers call the "Big Fire". The destruction concentrated on temples, palaces and administrative buildings rather than ordinary residential neighbourhoods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519302119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The collapse of Teotihuacan and the regeneration of Epiclassic societies: a Bayesian approach - ScienceDirectSeptember 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 2020</span></span></span>

This pattern matters because it does not resemble the aftermath of a simple foreign invasion. If an outside army had destroyed the city indiscriminately, archaeologists would expect widespread devastation across all districts. Instead, the damage appears highly selective. Buildings associated with political and religious authority suffered the greatest destruction, while many apartment compounds remained occupied for some time afterwards. Population decline followed over decades rather than overnight, suggesting a prolonged political collapse instead of an instantaneous abandonment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519302119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The collapse of Teotihuacan and the regeneration of Epiclassic societies: a Bayesian approach - ScienceDirectSeptember 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 2020</span></span></span>

The city itself was never completely deserted immediately. Many people continued living among the monumental ruins, but Teotihuacan ceased to function as the dominant regional capital that it had been for centuries. Trade networks weakened, political influence faded and power shifted to emerging centres elsewhere in central Mexico.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/27948/chapter-abstract/211885931" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicTeotihuacan and the Epiclassic in Central Mexico &#124; The Oxford Handbook of Mesoamerican Archaeology &#124; Oxford Academic...</span></span></span>

## Drought, inequality and political conflict

No single explanation accounts for every piece of evidence, so most archaeologists now favour interacting causes.

**Political conflict and elite revolt.** The selective destruction of temples and elite residences suggests that internal factions may have attacked the institutions that upheld the city's rulers. Rather than an invading army destroying Teotihuacan, some scholars argue that dissatisfied groups within the city deliberately dismantled the symbols of state authority after the government lost legitimacy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519302119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The collapse of Teotihuacan and the regeneration of Epiclassic societies: a Bayesian approach - ScienceDirectSeptember 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 2020</span></span></span>

**Social inequality.** Teotihuacan's population may have exceeded 100,000 people, creating enormous demands for food, water and labour. As resources became more difficult to manage, differences between ruling elites and ordinary residents may have widened. Archaeological evidence suggests increasing social tensions during the city's final centuries, making political unrest more likely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev.anthro.26.1.129" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: annualreviews.org">[Annual Reviews]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">annualreviews.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Annual Reviews State and Society at Teotihuacan, Mexico &#124; Annual Reviews</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Annual Reviews State and Society at Teotihuacan, Mexico &#124; Annual Reviews</span></span></span>

**Drought and environmental stress.** Climate evidence from lake sediments, cave deposits and other natural records indicates that central Mexico experienced periods of severe drought during the sixth and seventh centuries. Such conditions could have reduced agricultural yields, strained water supplies and intensified existing political and economic pressures. Importantly, climate researchers generally argue that drought acted as a contributing factor rather than an automatic cause of collapse. Many societies survive droughts; they become dangerous when governments cannot manage the resulting shortages and conflicts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/24694452.2018.1488577" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span>

**Regional rivalry.** As Teotihuacan's authority weakened, competing regional centres gained influence. Long-distance trade routes changed, local rulers asserted greater independence and the political landscape fragmented into several competing powers during the following Epiclassic period. This process probably accelerated the city's decline rather than initiating it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/27948/chapter-abstract/211885931" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicTeotihuacan and the Epiclassic in Central Mexico &#124; The Oxford Handbook of Mesoamerican Archaeology &#124; Oxford Academic...</span></span></span>

## Why no single collapse theory is enough

Earlier generations of researchers often searched for one decisive explanation—a foreign invasion, a devastating drought or a popular rebellion. Today, the evidence points towards a more complicated picture.

The archaeological record shows clear signs of deliberate political destruction, making purely environmental explanations incomplete. At the same time, climate records demonstrate genuine environmental stress, making purely political explanations equally unsatisfactory. Neither line of evidence alone explains why a city that had dominated central Mexico for centuries suddenly lost its governing institutions.

Instead, many scholars see Teotihuacan as an example of cascading failure. Environmental pressures may have reduced agricultural production, increasing hardship and exposing political weaknesses. Social inequality and elite competition could then have triggered internal violence, culminating in the symbolic destruction of state institutions. Once central authority collapsed, trade networks fragmented and rival regional centres emerged, preventing recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519302119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The collapse of Teotihuacan and the regeneration of Epiclassic societies: a Bayesian approach - ScienceDirectSeptember 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 2020</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery remains unsolved

The greatest obstacle is the absence of written testimony from Teotihuacan itself. Unlike the later Maya, whose inscriptions preserve the names of rulers and accounts of wars, Teotihuacan left no deciphered historical texts describing its political history or final crisis. Archaeologists must therefore infer events from architecture, artefacts, environmental records and settlement changes rather than eyewitness accounts.

Dating also presents challenges. Although modern scientific methods have improved the chronology, many destructive events occurred over several decades rather than on a single identifiable date. This makes it difficult to determine which developments came first and which were consequences rather than causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416519302119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The collapse of Teotihuacan and the regeneration of Epiclassic societies: a Bayesian approach - ScienceDirectSeptember 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 2020</span></span></span>

New discoveries continue to refine the debate. Some researchers have proposed that repeated major earthquakes may have damaged important monuments and added to political instability, but this remains a developing hypothesis that has not displaced the broader consensus favouring multiple interacting causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/archaeology/5-catastrophic-megathrust-earthquakes-led-to-the-demise-of-the-pre-aztec-city-of-teotihuacan-new-study-suggests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why Teotihuacan's fall still matters

Teotihuacan's collapse reshaped the history of central Mexico. The disappearance of its central government ended centuries of political dominance and opened the way for a more fragmented landscape of competing regional powers. Later societies inherited aspects of Teotihuacan's religion, urban planning and artistic traditions, but none immediately recreated its extraordinary scale.

The city's decline also serves as a reminder that ancient civilisations rarely disappear for a single reason. Teotihuacan remains one of archaeology's most important unresolved cases precisely because the evidence points towards a complex interaction of environmental change, political conflict, economic disruption and social tension. The mystery is not whether the city fell—that is firmly established—but why so many pressures converged at the same moment to bring down one of the ancient world's greatest urban centres.
