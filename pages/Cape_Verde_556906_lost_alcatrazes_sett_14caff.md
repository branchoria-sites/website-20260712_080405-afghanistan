## Introduction

Alcatrazes is one of [Cape Verde]({{ 'what-secrets-still-haunt-cape-verdes/' | relative_url }})'s most revealing historical puzzles, not because historians lost an entire town, but because they almost accepted its disappearance from the historical record. Long regarded as a failed early colonial settlement on Santiago's east coast, Alcatrazes seemed to vanish after the Portuguese administration transferred its official functions elsewhere in the early sixteenth century. Archaeological work beginning in 2012 has fundamentally changed that picture. Excavations have shown that the community survived far longer than official documents imply and was home to a mixed Luso-African population whose daily lives were only faintly recorded in colonial archives. Rather than uncovering a "lost city", researchers have recovered evidence of a forgotten community, demonstrating how archaeology can restore people and places omitted from written history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/finding-alcatrazes-and-early-lusoafrican-settlement-on-santiago-island-cape-verde/8339B945BB080CA4CFE0C8D84E6ED78A" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentFinding Alcatrazes and early Luso-African settlement on Santiago Island, Cape Verde &#124; Antiquity &#124;...</span></span></span>

## The Forgotten Captaincy and the Ruined Church

Alcatrazes occupied an important position during the earliest decades of Portuguese rule in Cape Verde. It served as the seat of Santiago's second captaincy between approximately 1484 and 1516, when the administration was transferred to Praia. Traditional historical accounts treated this move as marking the effective end of the settlement, reinforcing the impression that Alcatrazes had failed and been abandoned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0003598X2510149X/type/journal_article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentExcavating Alcatrazes, Santiago Island, Cape Verde: early colonial impacts on land, people and mat...</span></span></span>

Yet one conspicuous reminder remained: the ruined Gothic church of Nossa Senhora da Luz. Local people knew its location, and historians recognised its importance, but for many years the surrounding settlement itself remained elusive. Although the church survived as a landmark, the houses, workshops and everyday spaces that once surrounded it disappeared beneath vegetation and later landscape changes, leaving only fragmentary clues in written records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/finding-alcatrazes-and-early-lusoafrican-settlement-on-santiago-island-cape-verde/8339B945BB080CA4CFE0C8D84E6ED78A" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentFinding Alcatrazes and early Luso-African settlement on Santiago Island, Cape Verde &#124; Antiquity &#124;...</span></span></span>

Archaeological investigation of the church before restoration revealed that it had originally been a relatively simple building rather than the elaborate monument one might expect from an administrative centre. Excavations uncovered architectural features including a baptismal font, decorative carved capitals and sixteenth-century ceramic tiles. More strikingly, the church floor contained evidence of extensive burials. Thousands of human teeth recovered during excavation indicate repeated interments over many generations, suggesting that the church remained the focus of an active community well beyond the official relocation of government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0003598X17001041/type/journal_article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentFinding Alcatrazes and early Luso-African settlement on Santiago Island, Cape Verde &#124; Antiquity &#124;...</span></span></span>

## What Excavations Uncovered After 2012

The turning point came in 2012, when systematic field survey finally identified the associated settlement below the church. What had been known largely through place names and architectural ruins became a tangible archaeological landscape containing houses, streets and environmental evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/finding-alcatrazes-and-early-lusoafrican-settlement-on-santiago-island-cape-verde/8339B945BB080CA4CFE0C8D84E6ED78A" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentFinding Alcatrazes and early Luso-African settlement on Santiago Island, Cape Verde &#124; Antiquity &#124;...</span></span></span>

Subsequent excavations overturned the long-standing assumption that Alcatrazes effectively disappeared in 1516. Pottery and other finds demonstrate that occupation continued into the sixteenth century and probably beyond. Instead of a sudden collapse, archaeologists identified a gradual decline in which administrative importance faded while local inhabitants remained. Even after the captaincy moved, nearby church burials and scattered settlements indicate continuing occupation across the surrounding landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/excavating-alcatrazes-santiago-island-cape-verde-early-colonial-impacts-on-land-people-and-material-culture/D8CF4E8A457FF4852ED9EEC66148F8A4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentExcavating Alcatrazes, Santiago Island, Cape Verde: early colonial impacts on land, people and mat...</span></span></span>

Environmental evidence added another dimension to the story. Pollen, charcoal and sediment samples preserve a record of how settlers transformed the valley. Before colonisation the area supported native vegetation, including endemic trees and palms. After settlement, traces of cereal cultivation, livestock grazing, cotton and other introduced species appeared, documenting the rapid reshaping of Santiago's environment as part of the wider Atlantic colonial economy. Rather than relying solely on written descriptions, researchers could observe ecological change directly in the archaeological record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/excavating-alcatrazes-santiago-island-cape-verde-early-colonial-impacts-on-land-people-and-material-culture/D8CF4E8A457FF4852ED9EEC66148F8A4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentExcavating Alcatrazes, Santiago Island, Cape Verde: early colonial impacts on land, people and mat...</span></span></span>

The settlement itself also differs from later colonial centres. Its relatively modest square buildings contrast sharply with the denser urban fabric that developed at Ribeira Grande (today's Cidade Velha). Because early layers at Ribeira Grande are difficult to isolate, Alcatrazes provides unusually clear evidence for the formative phase of Portuguese settlement in Cape Verde.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/excavating-alcatrazes-santiago-island-cape-verde-early-colonial-impacts-on-land-people-and-material-culture/D8CF4E8A457FF4852ED9EEC66148F8A4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentExcavating Alcatrazes, Santiago Island, Cape Verde: early colonial impacts on land, people and mat...</span></span></span>

## Recovering Lives Omitted from Colonial Archives

Perhaps the most important discovery is not architectural but human. Colonial records naturally concentrated on officials, landowners, taxation and trade. They say comparatively little about the ordinary inhabitants who created Cape Verde's first colonial society.

Excavations have instead revealed a community whose material culture reflects constant interaction between Portuguese settlers and West Africans. Archaeologists recovered imported Portuguese ceramics alongside West African pottery and locally produced wares, showing that cultural exchange occurred from the settlement's earliest decades. These finds reinforce the view that Cape Verde was not simply a transplanted European colony but one of the Atlantic world's earliest Luso-African societies, where people, technologies and traditions mixed rapidly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0003598X2510149X/type/journal_article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentExcavating Alcatrazes, Santiago Island, Cape Verde: early colonial impacts on land, people and mat...</span></span></span>

The archaeological evidence also exposes the limitations of surviving documentation. Official records emphasise political decisions, such as relocating the captaincy, while material remains reveal continuity in everyday life. Families continued to bury their dead, cultivate land, use locally made pottery and inhabit the valley after administrators departed. In this sense, archaeology recovers the experiences of people whose existence is only partially visible—or entirely absent—in colonial paperwork.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/excavating-alcatrazes-santiago-island-cape-verde-early-colonial-impacts-on-land-people-and-material-culture/D8CF4E8A457FF4852ED9EEC66148F8A4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentExcavating Alcatrazes, Santiago Island, Cape Verde: early colonial impacts on land, people and mat...</span></span></span>

## Why the Mystery Matters

The mystery surrounding Alcatrazes is no longer where the settlement stood. That question has largely been answered through archaeological survey and excavation. The more significant question is why such an important community faded so completely from Cape Verde's historical narrative.

Part of the explanation lies in the nature of colonial documentation. Administrative archives followed government institutions, and when political power shifted elsewhere, Alcatrazes largely disappeared from official attention. Archaeological evidence demonstrates that administrative decline should not be mistaken for demographic disappearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/excavating-alcatrazes-santiago-island-cape-verde-early-colonial-impacts-on-land-people-and-material-culture/D8CF4E8A457FF4852ED9EEC66148F8A4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentExcavating Alcatrazes, Santiago Island, Cape Verde: early colonial impacts on land, people and mat...</span></span></span>

The site also changes broader interpretations of Cape Verde's early history. For decades, Ribeira Grande dominated discussions of the islands' beginnings because of its later prominence in the Atlantic slave trade. Alcatrazes now offers a complementary perspective by illuminating the experimental, formative stage of settlement before larger colonial towns reached their peak.
