## Introduction

The death of Thomas Warner, often called Carib Warner or Indian Warner, is one of the most disputed episodes in [Dominica]({{ 'what-is-still-unsolved-in-dominicas-past/' | relative_url }})'s early colonial history. The central question is not whether he died during the Antiguan expedition to Dominica in late 1674—he did—but *how* he died. Contemporary English officials left sharply conflicting accounts. One version presents Warner as a casualty of fighting against Kalinago forces. Another alleges that he first assisted the English expedition before being deliberately murdered at a feast organised by his own half-brother, Philip Warner, followed by the slaughter of dozens of Kalinago men, women and children. Because the surviving evidence comes almost entirely from rival colonial administrators with competing political interests, historians cannot resolve the contradiction with certainty. The debate remains important because it illustrates how colonial records can preserve events while obscuring their true nature.

## What happened on Dominica in 1674?

Thomas Warner occupied an unusual position in the eastern Caribbean. He was the son of the English coloniser Sir Thomas Warner and a Kalinago woman from Dominica. Raised partly within English society before returning to his mother's people, he became an influential intermediary who could negotiate with, and at times fight against, both European powers. English authorities even recognised him as their representative on Dominica for a period, despite continuing suspicions about his loyalties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Warner%2C_Thomas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Warner, Thomas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dictionary of National Biography, 1885-1900/Warner, Thomas - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

By late 1674 relations between English settlers in Antigua and the Kalinago had deteriorated. Colonial officials claimed that raids from Dominica justified a punitive expedition. Philip Warner, Lieutenant-Governor of Antigua and Thomas Warner's half-brother, led a force of roughly 300 men to Dominica during the Christmas period of 1674. What happened after the landing is where the historical record divides.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/86546/jdator_1.pdf?isAllowed=y&amp;sequence=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: deepblue.lib.umich.edu">[Deep Blue Repositories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">deepblue.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Deep Blue Repositories Search for a New Land</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deep Blue RepositoriesSearch for a New Land:November 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

The expedition ended with Thomas Warner dead and a large number of Kalinago killed. The place where the killings occurred later became known as Massacre, a name that survives today in western Dominica and preserves public memory of the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacre%2C_Dominica" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Massacre, Dominica</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Massacre, Dominica</span></span></span>

## The rival accounts of Stapleton and Atkins

The dispute centres on two competing official narratives produced within the English colonial administration.

### William Stapleton's military account

Sir William Stapleton, Governor of the Leeward Islands, described the expedition as legitimate retaliation for attacks on Antigua. His report states that Philip Warner's force killed around eighty Kalinago, destroyed food supplies and captured canoes. Thomas Warner appears only briefly, with the claim that he "fell amongst his fellow heathens" during the fighting.

In this version there is no suggestion of deception or assassination. Thomas Warner's death is treated as the natural consequence of military action against hostile opponents. Stapleton also portrayed Warner as unreliable, claiming he had accepted both English and French commissions, implying divided loyalties that made him suspect in English eyes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justworldnews.org/2021/06/04/1675-indigenes-and-enslaved-resist-english-colonists-in-new-england-caribbean/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justworldnews.org">[Just World News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justworldnews.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1675 indigenes and enslaved resist english colonists in new england caribbean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Just World News1675: Indigenes and enslaved resist English colonists in New England, Caribbean &#124; Just World NewsJune 4, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 4, 2021</span></span></span>

### Jonathan Atkins' betrayal narrative

Sir Jonathan Atkins, Governor of Barbados, presented a dramatically different account after receiving testimony from participants in the expedition.

According to the sworn deposition of William Hamlyn, commander of the sloop *Betty*, Thomas Warner initially welcomed the expedition and agreed to assist Philip Warner against another Kalinago group. Hamlyn stated that Warner supplied men, provisions and local support. After the fighting ended, Philip Warner allegedly invited Thomas Warner and around sixty or seventy of his followers—including women and children—to a celebratory feast.

Hamlyn claimed that the guests were deliberately intoxicated with rum before Philip Warner signalled English soldiers to attack them. He further alleged that officers objected to the killings, that one refused an order to kill Thomas Warner personally, and that Warner's own son begged to be executed after discovering his father and relatives had been killed. Hamlyn also claimed the child was subsequently killed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/86546/jdator_1.pdf?isAllowed=y&amp;sequence=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: deepblue.lib.umich.edu">[umich.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">deepblue.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Deep Blue Repositories Search for a New Land</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deep Blue RepositoriesSearch for a New Land:November 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

If Hamlyn's testimony is substantially accurate, Thomas Warner was not killed in battle but in a premeditated act of betrayal.

## Why the colonial record remains disputed

The contradiction between these accounts has never been resolved because every surviving narrative has limitations.

Several factors make the evidence difficult to evaluate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Both principal accounts were produced by English officials.** Neither represents a Kalinago eyewitness account.
* **Political rivalry mattered.** Stapleton and Atkins governed different colonial jurisdictions and had reasons to criticise or defend fellow officials.
* **The reports served administrative purposes.** They were written to justify decisions before authorities in London rather than to produce neutral histories.
* **Independent corroboration is scarce.** No surviving Kalinago written record explains the events from the victims' perspective.

</div>

Despite these problems, historians generally note that Hamlyn's deposition contains numerous specific operational details—numbers of vessels, movements, conversations and named officers—that go beyond a simple accusation. That does not automatically prove every allegation, but it explains why many modern historians regard the betrayal story as plausible rather than dismissing it as political invention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/86546/jdator_1.pdf?isAllowed=y&amp;sequence=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: deepblue.lib.umich.edu">[umich.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">deepblue.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Deep Blue Repositories Search for a New Land</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deep Blue RepositoriesSearch for a New Land:November 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## The aftermath and official response

The dispute did not end on Dominica.

The accusations against Philip Warner reached England, where the Crown reacted seriously enough to order an investigation. King Charles II's government described the incident in unusually strong language, referring to a "barbarous murder or rather massacre" and directing that justice should be pursued. Philip Warner was imprisoned in the Tower of London for a period while the allegations were investigated before later being acquitted or otherwise escaping criminal punishment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Warner%2C_Thomas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[wikisource.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Warner, Thomas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dictionary of National Biography, 1885-1900/Warner, Thomas - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The fact that the English government investigated its own colonial governor suggests that the allegations were considered credible enough to require more than routine administrative review. At the same time, the surviving records do not establish exactly why later proceedings failed to produce a conviction, leaving historians without a definitive legal resolution.

## Why Thomas Warner's death still matters

The mystery surrounding Thomas Warner's death is about more than identifying the precise manner of his killing. It exposes the fragile position of people who occupied both Indigenous and European worlds during the seventeenth century.

Warner's mixed ancestry had once made him useful to English authorities because he could negotiate with the Kalinago while maintaining official connections with colonial governments. Those same connections may also have made him vulnerable when imperial priorities changed. Whether he died in open combat or through calculated betrayal, his death removed an influential intermediary at a moment when English expansion was accelerating across the Lesser Antilles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.divisionofculture.gov.dm/resource-information2/national-figures/carib-warner" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: divisionofculture.gov.dm">[Division of Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">divisionofculture.gov.dm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Division of Culture Carib Warner</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Division of CultureCarib Warner - The Division of Culture...</span></span></span>

The village name Massacre keeps the episode alive in Dominica's historical landscape, while modern Dominican historians increasingly interpret the event as part of the wider displacement and destruction experienced by the Kalinago during European colonisation. Even so, the precise sequence of events on that Christmas expedition in 1674 remains one of the island's enduring documentary mysteries because the surviving evidence preserves two fundamentally incompatible stories, and no surviving source can conclusively reconcile them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.divisionofculture.gov.dm/resource-information2/national-figures/carib-warner" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: divisionofculture.gov.dm">[divisionofculture.gov.dm]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">divisionofculture.gov.dm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Division of Culture Carib Warner</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Division of CultureCarib Warner - The Division of Culture...</span></span></span>
