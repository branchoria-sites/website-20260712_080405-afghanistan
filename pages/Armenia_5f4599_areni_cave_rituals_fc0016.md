## Introduction

Areni-1 Cave in [Armenia]({{ 'what-do-armenias-ancient-mysteries/' | relative_url }})'s Vayots Dzor region is famous for preserving the world's earliest known complete wine-making installation, an exceptionally well-preserved leather shoe and rare organic remains dating mainly to the Late Chalcolithic, around 4500–3500 BC. Yet the cave's greatest mystery is not simply how old these discoveries are. It is why wine production, burials and carefully arranged ritual deposits all appear together in the same underground space. Archaeologists can reconstruct many of the activities that took place inside the cave, but the beliefs linking them remain unknown because the people who used it left no written records. The result is one of Armenia's most intriguing archaeological puzzles: a place where everyday work, remembrance of the dead and ceremonial practice seem to have overlapped in ways that are still debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/chalcolithic-of-the-near-east-and-southeastern-europe-discoveries-and-new-perspectives-from-the-cave-complex-areni1-armenia/A02E202BECCC6BA2A354E12D6F805A7C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe chalcolithic of the Near East and south-eastern Europe: discoveries and new perspectives from...</span></span></span>

## The winery, shoe and preserved remains

The exceptional preservation at Areni-1 is the key to its importance. The cave's dry, stable microclimate protected leather, woven fibres, rope, seeds, wood and plant remains that would normally have decayed thousands of years ago. This allows archaeologists to reconstruct activities with an unusual level of detail rather than relying only on pottery and stone tools.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/chalcolithic-of-the-near-east-and-southeastern-europe-discoveries-and-new-perspectives-from-the-cave-complex-areni1-armenia/A02E202BECCC6BA2A354E12D6F805A7C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe chalcolithic of the Near East and south-eastern Europe: discoveries and new perspectives from...</span></span></span>

The best-known discovery is a complete wine-making installation dating to about 4000 BC. Excavators uncovered:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a shallow clay basin interpreted as a grape press;
* a channel leading into a large buried ceramic jar for fermentation or collection;
* additional storage vessels;
* grape pips, skins and stems;
* drinking vessels nearby.

</div>

Chemical analysis detected compounds derived from red grapes, including evidence linked to the pigment malvidin, supporting the interpretation that wine was deliberately produced rather than grapes simply being stored. Together, these finds represent the earliest known integrated winery yet discovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440310004115" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chemical evidence for wine production around 4000 BCE in the Late Chalcolithic Near Eastern highlands - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Nearby, archaeologists recovered the famous leather shoe, dated to roughly 3600–3500 BC. Stuffed with grass to preserve its shape, it demonstrates that the cave was not exclusively ceremonial. Everyday possessions, storage areas and craft activities all occurred alongside more unusual deposits. The coexistence of ordinary domestic objects with ritual features is one reason the cave remains difficult to interpret through a single explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/chalcolithic-of-the-near-east-and-southeastern-europe-discoveries-and-new-perspectives-from-the-cave-complex-areni1-armenia/A02E202BECCC6BA2A354E12D6F805A7C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe chalcolithic of the Near East and south-eastern Europe: discoveries and new perspectives from...</span></span></span>

## Wine production beside the dead

The most striking feature of Areni-1 is that wine-making installations were found close to mortuary deposits rather than being clearly separated from them.

Excavations uncovered human remains that differ from conventional cemeteries. Instead of complete burials arranged in orderly graves, archaeologists found carefully deposited skulls and disarticulated bones, particularly those of children and adolescents, placed inside ceramic vessels or prepared features. Some deposits were accompanied by animal remains, horns or other offerings. The pattern suggests deliberate ritual activity rather than accidental disturbance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/chalcolithic-of-the-near-east-and-southeastern-europe-discoveries-and-new-perspectives-from-the-cave-complex-areni1-armenia/A02E202BECCC6BA2A354E12D6F805A7C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe chalcolithic of the Near East and south-eastern Europe: discoveries and new perspectives from...</span></span></span>

This association immediately raises questions. Was wine produced for ordinary consumption and simply stored in a convenient cave that also happened to contain burials? Or was the wine itself part of ceremonies honouring the dead?

The archaeological evidence cannot answer that directly. No written texts explain the meaning of the deposits, and no surviving artwork depicts the rituals. Researchers therefore rely on the spatial relationships between finds, the sequence of deposits and comparisons with other prehistoric societies.

The cave also contains evidence of storage, food processing and animal husbandry. These practical activities indicate that the site was not reserved exclusively for funerary ceremonies. Instead, it appears to have combined production, storage and symbolic behaviour within the same protected environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/chalcolithic-of-the-near-east-and-southeastern-europe-discoveries-and-new-perspectives-from-the-cave-complex-areni1-armenia/A02E202BECCC6BA2A354E12D6F805A7C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe chalcolithic of the Near East and south-eastern Europe: discoveries and new perspectives from...</span></span></span>

## Competing explanations for the cave's rituals

Several interpretations have been proposed, but none has achieved universal acceptance.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Armenia_5f4599_areni_cave_rituals_fc0016-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Areni 1 illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Armenia_5f4599_areni_cave_rituals_fc0016-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Armenia_5f4599_areni_cave_rituals_fc0016-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

### Ritual feasting and ancestor ceremonies

One influential explanation is that wine formed part of ceremonies connected with remembering the dead. In many later societies across western Asia and the Mediterranean, alcohol accompanied communal feasts honouring ancestors or marking funerals. The close proximity of the winery to mortuary deposits makes this possibility attractive.

However, there is no direct evidence that the people of Areni-1 shared these later beliefs. Similarity alone cannot prove continuity across thousands of years. Archaeologists therefore treat the comparison as a useful analogy rather than a demonstrated fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/chalcolithic-of-the-near-east-and-southeastern-europe-discoveries-and-new-perspectives-from-the-cave-complex-areni1-armenia/A02E202BECCC6BA2A354E12D6F805A7C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe chalcolithic of the Near East and south-eastern Europe: discoveries and new perspectives from...</span></span></span>

### A cave used for multiple specialised purposes

Another interpretation argues that Areni-1 functioned as a specialised community centre rather than a settlement. Different chambers may have been used for storage, wine production, burial rites and seasonal gatherings.

The cave's stable temperature would have favoured food preservation and fermentation, while its secluded interior may also have made it an appropriate location for ceremonies separated from everyday village life. This explanation accommodates the mixture of economic and ritual evidence without requiring every activity to have been symbolic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/chalcolithic-of-the-near-east-and-southeastern-europe-discoveries-and-new-perspectives-from-the-cave-complex-areni1-armenia/A02E202BECCC6BA2A354E12D6F805A7C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe chalcolithic of the Near East and south-eastern Europe: discoveries and new perspectives from...</span></span></span>

### Evidence of emerging social complexity

Some researchers see the cave as evidence that Late Chalcolithic communities in the southern Caucasus were becoming more socially organised. Constructing a wine-making installation, managing stored produce and carrying out repeated ritual practices would all have required cooperation beyond the household level.

From this perspective, the combination of production and ritual reflects a society in which economic activities and ceremonial authority reinforced one another. Even so, archaeologists disagree about how hierarchical these communities were, and the evidence does not justify assuming the existence of powerful elites or organised priesthoods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/chalcolithic-of-the-near-east-and-southeastern-europe-discoveries-and-new-perspectives-from-the-cave-complex-areni1-armenia/A02E202BECCC6BA2A354E12D6F805A7C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe chalcolithic of the Near East and south-eastern Europe: discoveries and new perspectives from...</span></span></span>

## What remains genuinely mysterious?

Many of the cave's basic facts are now well established through excavation, radiocarbon dating and chemical analysis. The enduring uncertainties concern meaning rather than discovery.

Important unanswered questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Why were many human remains deliberately rearranged instead of buried intact?
* Why do child and adolescent remains appear prominently in several ritual deposits?
* Was wine intended mainly for everyday consumption, ceremonial use or both?
* Did the cave serve one local community or several neighbouring groups?
* Were the rituals seasonal, connected with harvests, funerals or other occasions now impossible to identify?

</div>

Because no written evidence survives, these questions may never receive definitive answers. Archaeology can reveal what people did, but not always why they believed it mattered.

## Why Areni-1 still matters

Areni-1 has transformed understanding of prehistoric Armenia by preserving activities that usually disappear from the archaeological record. The cave demonstrates that sophisticated wine production existed in the southern Caucasus around 4000 BC and provides an unusually complete picture of storage, craft, agriculture and mortuary practice within a single location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440310004115" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chemical evidence for wine production around 4000 BCE in the Late Chalcolithic Near Eastern highlands - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Its greatest historical significance, however, lies in the unresolved relationship between those activities. Rather than revealing a lost civilisation or supporting extravagant claims, the cave presents a more compelling mystery: how one prehistoric community integrated food production, remembrance of the dead and ritual life into a single underground landscape. The physical evidence is remarkably rich, but the beliefs that gave those actions meaning remain silent, making Areni-1 one of Armenia's most important and enduring archaeological enigmas.
