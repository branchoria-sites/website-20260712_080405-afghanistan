## Introduction

Four cases stand out. The collapse of [Tiwanaku]({{ 'tiwanaku/' | relative_url }}) remains debated because climatic stress, political change and social upheaval are difficult to separate. The reported deaths of Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid are probable but still unconfirmed by identified remains. The secret burial of Che Guevara was largely solved through archaeology and forensic science, showing how a political mystery can be manufactured by official concealment. In Bolivia’s Amazonian lowlands, laser mapping has revealed an extensive pre-Hispanic settlement system, replacing an old mystery — whether complex societies existed there — with new questions about how they lived and why their centres declined. Alongside these famous cases is a more painful national enigma: the fate of people forcibly disappeared under military rule, whose families still seek bodies, documents and a complete account of state violence.

## Why Tiwanaku declined

Tiwanaku, near Lake Titicaca in western Bolivia, was one of the most influential political and ceremonial centres in the pre-Inca Andes. Its monumental core included temples, courtyards, gateways, platforms and carefully worked stone buildings. At its height, roughly between the sixth and tenth centuries, its cultural and political influence extended well beyond the city into parts of present-day Peru, Chile and Argentina. UNESCO describes it as the spiritual and political centre of a major Andean civilisation whose agricultural and social organisation enabled a large population to live at high altitude.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/document/153775" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreTiwanaku (Bolivia) No 567revJuly 19, 2016 — 6 Apr 1991 — Tiwanaku was until the 8th century CE the capital of...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 19, 2016</span></span></span>

The mystery is not that Tiwanaku vanished without leaving evidence. Archaeologists can date its buildings, trace its trading connections and study the raised fields that supported farming near Lake Titicaca. The puzzle is why its political system weakened around the end of the first millennium, after centuries of growth. There is no surviving Tiwanaku-written historical account naming a final ruler, civil war or decisive invasion, so explanations must be reconstructed from settlement patterns, human remains, agricultural systems and environmental records.

A long-standing theory holds that prolonged drought undermined Tiwanaku’s food supply. Raised-field agriculture depended on carefully managed water and seasonal rainfall. A 2021 palaeoclimate study identified severe dry conditions around the period when the Tiwanaku state fragmented and argued that drought placed increasing pressure on its agricultural base. Earlier research likewise connected falling water availability with the abandonment of intensive fields.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0277379120306557" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Drought and the collapse of the Tiwanaku Civilizationby TE Arnold · 2021 · Cited by 57 — At the end of the first millennium...</span></span></span>

Yet drought is not a complete answer. Researchers have disputed the precise timing of regional climate records, and some chronologies place the most severe drying after political decline had already begun. Environmental pressure may therefore have worsened an existing crisis rather than single-handedly destroying a stable state. The weakening of elite authority, changing trade networks, local conflict and the movement of people away from major centres may all have contributed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347911174_Drought_and_the_collapse_of_the_Tiwanaku_Civilization_New_evidence_from_Lake_Orurillo_Peru" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Drought and the collapse of the Tiwanaku CivilizationJanuary 1, 2021 — Lake core data identify a drought, which been proposed...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2021</span></span></span>

The most defensible interpretation is that Tiwanaku underwent a prolonged political and social transformation rather than an overnight disappearance. Its central institutions collapsed, but communities and cultural traditions survived. The ruins remained sacred, and later Inca traditions treated the area as a place of origins. That continuity matters because popular descriptions of a “lost race” obscure the relationship between Tiwanaku and the Indigenous history of the Titicaca basin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/26792-tiwanaku.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">500 and 1000 near Lake Titicaca in Bolivia, at an elevation of nearly 13,000 feet. ### Pumapunku and the myth of impossible stonework

Pumapunku, part of the wider Tiwanaku complex, has become an internet-age mystery in its own right. Its precisely shaped stone blocks, repeated geometric forms and fragmented gateways are often presented as evidence of unknown machinery, a vanished global civilisation or extraterrestrial intervention.

The archaeology supports a less sensational but more interesting explanation. Pumapunku was a monumental platform and ceremonial complex built by Tiwanaku craftspeople, probably in several stages after the sixth century. Archaeological reconstruction shows that many apparently isolated blocks formed parts of repeated gateways, walls and architectural units. Digital modelling and three-dimensional printing have helped researchers test how the scattered pieces once fitted together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/crumbling-temple-bolivia-reconstructed-3d-printing-180971047/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

Some details of the construction process remain uncertain, including the full range of tools used to produce exceptionally flat surfaces and fine internal angles. Experimental work has nevertheless shown that hard stone can be shaped accurately using combinations of hammerstones, abrasives and carefully selected cutting tools. Local architectural precedents also demonstrate that Pumapunku did not appear without a regional history. The genuine mystery concerns organisation, craftsmanship and reconstruction — not whether Andean people were capable of building it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumapunku" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Claims of alien builders often depend on an outdated assumption that pre-Columbian communities lacked the knowledge required for monumental engineering. That interpretation removes Indigenous people from their own achievement. The ruins are more revealing when treated as the work of skilled specialists operating within a long Andean tradition of stoneworking, hydraulic engineering and ceremonial design.

## The cities hidden in Bolivia’s Amazon

For much of the twentieth century, influential interpretations portrayed the Amazonian lowlands as too environmentally difficult to support large, organised populations. The flooded plains of the Llanos de Mojos in Bolivia’s Beni region appeared, from ground level, to contain scattered forest islands and earthen rises rather than cities.

Local people, geographers and archaeologists had long known that many of these features were human-made. Raised fields, canals, causeways, mounds, reservoirs and defensive ditches showed that earlier inhabitants had reshaped the landscape on a considerable scale. The unanswered question was how these elements fitted together and whether they represented isolated villages or a coordinated settlement system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/hidden-earthworks-in-the-forests-of-the-bolivian-amazon/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: penn.museum">[Penn Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">penn.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on penn.museum.</span></span></span>

Airborne lidar — a surveying method that uses laser pulses to map the ground beneath vegetation — transformed the evidence. A German–Bolivian research project mapped sites associated with the Casarabe culture, which flourished approximately between AD 500 and 1400. The results revealed large centres, smaller settlements, causeways, canals, reservoirs and monumental platforms arranged in a four-tier hierarchy. Two major sites covered about 147 and 315 hectares, while the broader known Casarabe region extended across roughly 4,500 square kilometres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

This was not a stone-built cityscape like Tiwanaku. The Casarabe centres were made largely from earth and timber, materials that weather easily and disappear beneath forest. Their scale and interconnected layout led researchers to describe them as a form of low-density tropical urbanism: dispersed neighbourhoods and monumental centres integrated with agricultural and water-management systems rather than packed behind continuous city walls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

The discovery solved one question decisively. Complex, organised societies did exist in the Bolivian Amazon before European colonisation, and they altered the landscape extensively. It also exposed the misleading idea that visible stone ruins are the only reliable sign of urban history.

The new mystery is why the largest Casarabe centres ceased to function before Europeans reached much of the region. Archaeologists still need better dates from individual sites, more excavation of residential zones and clearer evidence about food production, political authority and conflict. Climate variability, changes in water systems, resource pressure, warfare or the reorganisation of communities are all possible factors, but present evidence does not justify choosing a single cause. The lidar maps reveal the shape of a society more clearly than its final history.

Nor should the settlements be turned into a South American version of the legendary El Dorado. No evidence points to a fabulous city of gold or a vanished non-Indigenous civilisation. The importance of the discovery lies precisely in what the earthworks demonstrate about Indigenous Amazonian knowledge: people managed seasonal flooding, linked settlements across difficult terrain and built lasting ceremonial landscapes from materials available locally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

## Did Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid die in Bolivia?

The best-known version of the story places the final moments of Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid in San Vicente, a mining settlement in southern Bolivia, in November 1908. After years of robbery and flight through the United States and Argentina, two English-speaking bandits allegedly attacked a courier carrying wages for the Aramayo mining company. Soldiers and local officials later surrounded the pair in a lodging house, where a gun battle continued into the night. Two men were found dead inside the following morning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butch_Cassidy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Butch Cassidy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Butch Cassidy</span></span></span>

Bolivian records strongly support the occurrence of the payroll robbery and shoot-out. The problem is identification. Local officials believed that the dead men were the robbers, but they did not know them as Robert Parker and Harry Longabaugh — the real names of Cassidy and Sundance — and no reliable contemporary identification linked the bodies conclusively to the famous Americans.

The physical evidence has not settled the matter. In 1991, forensic anthropologist Clyde Snow joined an attempt to locate and examine the presumed graves in San Vicente. The remains recovered did not match DNA from relatives of Cassidy or Longabaugh and were considered more likely to belong to a German miner. That result demonstrated that the excavated grave was wrong; it did not prove that the outlaws survived the shoot-out or that their actual remains were absent from the cemetery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/the-mysterious-deaths-of-butch-cassidy-and-the-sundance-kid" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on history.com.</span></span></span>

Stories that Cassidy returned to the United States circulated among relatives, writers and enthusiasts. Various men were later claimed to have been Cassidy in disguise, and alleged memoirs were offered as evidence. None has produced a securely authenticated chain of documentation. One supposed lost manuscript was challenged as a hoax after inconsistencies in its authorship and supporting story emerged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kimmacquarrie.com/butch-cassidys-lost-manuscript-a-hoax/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kimmacquarrie.com">[Kim MacQuarrie Author and Filmmaker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kimmacquarrie.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kim Mac Quarrie Author and Filmmaker Butch Cassidy&#x27;s “Lost Manuscript” a Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kim Mac Quarrie Author and Filmmaker Butch Cassidy&#x27;s “Lost Manuscript” a Hoax</span></span></span>

The balance of probability still favours death in Bolivia. The two bandits’ movements, the timing of the robbery and the lack of dependable evidence for either man after 1908 fit the San Vicente account. But probability is not identity. Until remains are found and conclusively matched, the narrow historical question — exactly who died in that room — remains unresolved.

The endurance of the mystery owes much to popular culture. The 1969 film *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid* transformed a poorly documented ending into one of cinema’s most memorable last stands. San Vicente now has a museum linked to the story, giving a remote Bolivian mining community a place in the international mythology of the American West.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historyhit.com/locations/san-vicente-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyhit.com">[History Hit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyhit.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History Hit San Vicente Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History Hit San Vicente Museum</span></span></span>

## Che Guevara’s missing grave

The disappearance of Ernesto “Che” Guevara’s body was a genuine mystery for nearly three decades, although the central facts of his death were less uncertain. Guevara was captured alive by Bolivian forces near La Higuera in October 1967 and executed the following day. His body was displayed to journalists and local residents in Vallegrande, while the authorities publicly gave misleading or contradictory accounts of how he had died and what had been done with his remains. Contemporary intelligence reporting confirmed his identity through fingerprints.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP69B00369R000200270013-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cia.gov.</span></span></span>

After the public display, Guevara’s hands were removed for identification and his body disappeared. Officials variously suggested that it had been cremated, transported elsewhere or disposed of in a remote location. The secrecy was deliberate: a known grave might have become a political shrine.

A breakthrough came in the 1990s when testimony from a retired Bolivian officer directed investigators to an area near Vallegrande’s airstrip. A forensic excavation in 1997 uncovered a group burial containing a skeleton without hands, alongside the remains of other guerrillas. Guevara’s remains were identified using anatomical information, documentary evidence and forensic comparison before being transferred to Cuba. DNA techniques were also used successfully to identify members of his guerrilla group from the burial sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10741484/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Some critics continued to dispute the identification, partly because the search was entangled with Cuban state commemoration and international politics. Skepticism towards politically managed discoveries is reasonable, but no strong alternative body of evidence has overturned the forensic conclusion. The Guevara case is therefore better described as a solved burial mystery with contested political afterlives than as an open question about whether the grave was found.

Its significance reaches beyond Guevara himself. The episode shows how quickly official secrecy can produce legends. Contradictory statements created decades of speculation, while the eventual discovery depended on witness testimony, excavation and forensic analysis rather than a dramatic confession or newly released master file.

## Bolivia’s unresolved disappearances

The country’s gravest historical mysteries concern people taken during military governments between the 1960s and early 1980s. These are not mysteries in the entertaining sense. They are unresolved state crimes in which families often know who detained a relative, or which security institution was involved, but still do not know where the victim was killed or buried.

One of the most important cases began during the coup led by Luis García Meza on 17 July 1980. Armed forces attacked the headquarters of the Bolivian Workers’ Centre in La Paz. Socialist politician Marcelo Quiroga Santa Cruz and political leader Carlos Flores Bedregal were among those seized or killed during the operation, and their bodies disappeared. Investigations have gathered testimony and reconstructed parts of the assault, yet the location of their remains has never been conclusively established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oas.org/en/iachr/decisions/court/2018/12709fondoen.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oas.org">[Organization of American States]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on oas.org.</span></span></span>

The problem is not simply a lack of witnesses. Military records have repeatedly been inaccessible, incomplete or denied to investigators. A Bolivian court ordered the armed forces to provide documents that could help locate Quiroga Santa Cruz, Flores Bedregal and other victims, but human rights organisations reported continued obstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/ar/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/amr180032010en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Bolivia: Military documents must be handed over if justice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Bolivia: Military documents must be handed over if justice</span></span></span>

Bolivia created an investigative commission after the restoration of democracy in 1982, becoming an early Latin American state to establish such a body. It examined scores of disappearances but lacked funding, technical capacity and reliable access to records. A later truth commission, established in 2016, completed its work in 2020 and issued recommendations on prosecutions, reparations and institutional reform. Nevertheless, families and campaigners continued to report slow investigations, incomplete reparations and closed archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://transitionaljusticedata.org/en/Americas/Bolivia.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: transitionaljusticedata.org">[transitionaljusticedata.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">transitionaljusticedata.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on transitionaljusticedata.org.</span></span></span>

In the Flores Bedregal case, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights found Bolivia responsible for failures connected with the investigation and the family’s right to truth. In 2023, the judgment drew renewed attention to the need to open military archives and conduct an effective search for the remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/news/foia-human-rights/2023-01-24/inter-american-court-orders-opening-military-archives" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[nsarchive.gwu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">inter american court orders opening military archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">inter american court orders opening military archives</span></span></span>

The scale extends beyond the best-known names. Human rights reporting has referred to more than 200 people killed and about 150 forcibly disappeared during Bolivia’s military dictatorships, alongside thousands of victims of detention, torture and political persecution. Exact totals vary by mandate and definition, which is itself part of the archival problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2022/mar/30/bolivian-victims-dictatorships-still-wait-for-justice" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Bolivian victims of dictatorships still waiting for justice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Bolivian victims of dictatorships still waiting for justice</span></span></span>

These cases remain unresolved for identifiable reasons:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Records are unavailable or disputed.** Security organisations frequently operated in secrecy, and surviving files may be incomplete, dispersed or deliberately withheld.
* **Burial locations were concealed.** Victims could be moved after death, making eyewitness testimony difficult to verify decades later.
* **Early investigations lacked resources.** Bolivia’s first disappearance commission did not have the forensic expertise or political support needed to complete its work.
* **Time has damaged the evidence.** Witnesses have died, landscapes have changed and remains may have been disturbed.
* **Political interests persist.** Opening archives may expose institutions and individuals whose reputations or networks survived the return to democracy.

</div>

The uncertainty should not be mistaken for an absence of evidence. In many cases, substantial evidence establishes detention, killing or state responsibility. What remains unknown is often the final sequence of events and the location of the body. For families, that distinction is crucial: a court judgment cannot substitute for recovery, identification and burial.

## What these mysteries reveal about Bolivia

Bolivia’s historical enigmas repeatedly arise where evidence has been damaged, hidden or misunderstood. At Tiwanaku, the absence of written political chronicles leaves archaeology and climate science to reconstruct a complex decline. In the Amazon, earthen cities remained difficult to recognise because researchers expected urban history to look like stone architecture. At San Vicente, weak identification turned two bodies into a century-long outlaw legend. In the cases of Guevara and the dictatorship-era disappeared, authorities actively controlled information about death and burial.

They also show that not every mystery deserves the same verdict. The fate of Tiwanaku is an open scholarly debate with several plausible causes. The Casarabe settlement network is a major discovery whose social organisation is still being investigated. Butch Cassidy’s death in Bolivia is likely, though not forensically proved. Guevara’s burial place was concealed and then credibly identified. The disappearances under military rule are documented violations whose unresolved elements survive because truth and evidence have not been fully released.

That difference between uncertainty, concealment and myth is the key to understanding Bolivia’s mysterious past. The strongest cases do not require lost continents, supernatural intervention or grand conspiracies. Their power comes from the incomplete record itself — abandoned fields beside Lake Titicaca, roads visible only from the air, an unidentified grave in a mining village, and military archives that may still contain answers owed to the living.
