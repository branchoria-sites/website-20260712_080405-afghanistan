## Introduction

The rock engravings of the Lopé-Okanda landscape in central [Gabon]({{ 'what-still-mystifies-us-about-gabons/' | relative_url }}) are among the country's most intriguing archaeological mysteries. Thousands of geometric carvings survive on exposed rock surfaces, yet no written records, oral traditions or surviving cultural explanations reveal exactly what they meant. Archaeologists can estimate when many of the engravings were created and identify the communities most likely responsible, but the symbols themselves remain undeciphered. Rather than being a forgotten script waiting for a simple translation, they are probably part of a symbolic tradition whose cultural context disappeared centuries ago. That combination of abundant physical evidence and missing meaning makes the Lopé engravings one of Gabon's most enduring historical puzzles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1147" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Ecosystem and Relict Cultural Landscape of Lopé-Okanda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreEcosystem and Relict Cultural Landscape of Lopé-Okanda - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## Where the engravings are found

The engravings are concentrated within the UNESCO-listed Ecosystem and Relict Cultural Landscape of Lopé-Okanda, particularly along the middle Ogooué River. Researchers have documented large groups of petroglyphs at sites including Elarmékora, Epona and Kongo Boumba, with additional engraved rocks elsewhere in the valley. Together they form one of Central Africa's richest collections of open-air rock art. UNESCO estimates that around 1,800 petroglyphs survive within the protected landscape, although archaeological publications sometimes cite slightly higher totals as new discoveries have been recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1147" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Ecosystem and Relict Cultural Landscape of Lopé-Okanda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreEcosystem and Relict Cultural Landscape of Lopé-Okanda - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

The engravings are carved into hard rock outcrops and boulders, often close to ancient movement routes beside the river. Their location is significant because the Ogooué Valley served as a major corridor through which successive populations travelled during the Neolithic and Iron Age. The rock art sits within a broader archaeological landscape containing settlements, iron-working sites and evidence of repeated human occupation stretching back hundreds of thousands of years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1147" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Ecosystem and Relict Cultural Landscape of Lopé-Okanda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreEcosystem and Relict Cultural Landscape of Lopé-Okanda - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## How archaeologists estimate their age

Unlike charcoal from a hearth or human bone from a burial, engraved rock cannot usually be dated directly. Archaeologists therefore rely on several independent lines of evidence rather than a single scientific test.

The strongest evidence comes from cultural association. Excavations throughout the middle Ogooué Valley have established a detailed sequence of Iron Age settlements. The engraved rocks occur within landscapes occupied by Iron Age communities, and the carving technique itself strongly suggests the use of iron tools rather than stone implements. Individual peck marks are regular and sharply defined, matching work produced with iron chisels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.panafprehistory.org/images/uploads/THE_ROCK_ART_OF_GABON_TECHNIQUES_THEMES_AND_ESTIMATION_OF_ITS_AGE_BY_CULTURAL_ASSOCIATION_Richard_Oslisly.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: panafprehistory.org">[panafprehistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">panafprehistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aspects of African Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aspects of African Archaeology</span></span></span>

Researchers have also compared engraved motifs with decorative patterns found on Iron Age pottery. Some circular and geometric designs resemble decoration associated with the Okanda cultural tradition, suggesting that at least part of the rock art belongs to the same cultural horizon. This comparison cannot provide an exact date, but it helps place the engravings within a broader archaeological sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.panafprehistory.org/images/uploads/THE_ROCK_ART_OF_GABON_TECHNIQUES_THEMES_AND_ESTIMATION_OF_ITS_AGE_BY_CULTURAL_ASSOCIATION_Richard_Oslisly.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: panafprehistory.org">[panafprehistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">panafprehistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aspects of African Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aspects of African Archaeology</span></span></span>

Another clue comes from settlement history. Archaeological surveys indicate a long interruption in occupation in parts of the middle Ogooué region between roughly 1,400 and 700 years before present. Because present-day communities appear to descend from later populations and possess no surviving knowledge of the engravings, Richard Oslisly and other researchers have argued that much of the rock art probably predates this occupational break, placing many carvings at around 2,000 years old during the Early Iron Age. This remains an informed estimate rather than a fixed date for every individual engraving.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.panafprehistory.org/images/uploads/THE_ROCK_ART_OF_GABON_TECHNIQUES_THEMES_AND_ESTIMATION_OF_ITS_AGE_BY_CULTURAL_ASSOCIATION_Richard_Oslisly.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: panafprehistory.org">[panafprehistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">panafprehistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aspects of African Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aspects of African Archaeology</span></span></span>

## Why their symbols resist interpretation

The greatest mystery is not who carved the engravings, but what they were intended to communicate.

Most motifs are geometric rather than figurative. Archaeologists have recorded circles, concentric circles, spirals, linked chains of circles, branching patterns, meandering lines and cup-like depressions. Figurative images do exist—including occasional animals, reptiles, weapons and rare human forms—but they represent only a small minority of the overall corpus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bradshawfoundation.com/central-africa/index.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bradshawfoundation.com">[Bradshaw Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bradshawfoundation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bradshaw Foundation The Rock Art of Western Central Africa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bradshaw Foundation The Rock Art of Western Central Africa</span></span></span>

Because the designs are overwhelmingly abstract, there is no obvious narrative to interpret. Unlike Egyptian hieroglyphs or Maya inscriptions, the Lopé engravings show no repeated sequences that can confidently be recognised as writing, names or dates. Instead, they resemble symbolic designs whose meaning depended on ceremonies, beliefs or traditions that have not survived.

The problem is compounded by the absence of historical continuity. Archaeological research indicates that the communities now living in the region have no preserved oral explanations for the engravings, and recorded traditions do not describe their creation. Even if later inhabitants noticed the carvings, the original cultural knowledge appears to have been lost long before written documentation reached the region.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.panafprehistory.org/images/uploads/THE_ROCK_ART_OF_GABON_TECHNIQUES_THEMES_AND_ESTIMATION_OF_ITS_AGE_BY_CULTURAL_ASSOCIATION_Richard_Oslisly.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: panafprehistory.org">[panafprehistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">panafprehistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aspects of African Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aspects of African Archaeology</span></span></span>

Weathering creates another obstacle. Although many engravings remain remarkably clear, erosion has damaged others, making it difficult to reconstruct complete compositions or distinguish intentional features from later surface changes. Some rock surfaces continue to exfoliate naturally, gradually removing parts of the ancient carvings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bradshawfoundation.com/central-africa/index.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bradshawfoundation.com">[Bradshaw Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bradshawfoundation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bradshaw Foundation The Rock Art of Western Central Africa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bradshaw Foundation The Rock Art of Western Central Africa</span></span></span>

## What explanations have been proposed?

No interpretation has gained scholarly consensus, but several possibilities fit the available evidence better than others.

**Ritual or religious symbolism.** The predominance of abstract motifs has led many archaeologists to suggest that the engravings expressed beliefs, ceremonial practices or sacred concepts rather than everyday events. Circles, spirals and repeated geometric arrangements may have held symbolic significance understood within particular communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.panafprehistory.org/images/uploads/THE_ROCK_ART_OF_GABON_TECHNIQUES_THEMES_AND_ESTIMATION_OF_ITS_AGE_BY_CULTURAL_ASSOCIATION_Richard_Oslisly.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: panafprehistory.org">[panafprehistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">panafprehistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aspects of African Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aspects of African Archaeology</span></span></span>

**Territorial or gathering places.** Because many engraved rocks lie beside the Ogooué River, they may have marked important meeting places, travel routes or socially significant locations. The river was a major communication corridor throughout prehistory, making such an interpretation plausible even if it cannot be demonstrated directly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1147" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Ecosystem and Relict Cultural Landscape of Lopé-Okanda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreEcosystem and Relict Cultural Landscape of Lopé-Okanda - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

**Connections with Iron Age identity.** Similarities between pottery decoration and some engraved motifs suggest that the carvings may have expressed shared cultural identity rather than conveying literal information. In this view, the rock art functioned more like symbolic decoration than a written language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.panafprehistory.org/images/uploads/THE_ROCK_ART_OF_GABON_TECHNIQUES_THEMES_AND_ESTIMATION_OF_ITS_AGE_BY_CULTURAL_ASSOCIATION_Richard_Oslisly.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: panafprehistory.org">[panafprehistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">panafprehistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aspects of African Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aspects of African Archaeology</span></span></span>

Less convincing ideas—including claims that the engravings represent a lost alphabet or evidence of an unknown civilisation—have not been supported by archaeological evidence. ## Can the Lopé engravings ever be decoded?

A complete "decoding" is unlikely unless entirely new evidence emerges.

Unlike deciphered ancient scripts, the Lopé engravings have no bilingual inscription, no associated written language and no surviving tradition explaining their symbolism. Without such a key, archaeologists must rely on comparisons with other artefacts, settlement patterns and cultural landscapes rather than direct translation.

Future discoveries could still improve understanding. Excavations at nearby habitation sites, better dating techniques, digital recording of weathered surfaces and wider comparison with Iron Age rock art elsewhere in Central Africa may clarify when different groups of engravings were created or whether distinct artistic traditions existed. These advances may narrow the range of interpretations even if they never reveal the exact meanings intended by their creators.

For now, the mystery lies not in whether people made the engravings—archaeology provides a convincing Iron Age context—but in the disappearance of the cultural knowledge that once made the symbols immediately understandable to those who carved them. That lost context, rather than the carvings themselves, is what continues to puzzle historians and archaeologists today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.panafprehistory.org/images/uploads/THE_ROCK_ART_OF_GABON_TECHNIQUES_THEMES_AND_ESTIMATION_OF_ITS_AGE_BY_CULTURAL_ASSOCIATION_Richard_Oslisly.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: panafprehistory.org">[panafprehistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">panafprehistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aspects of African Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aspects of African Archaeology</span></span></span>
