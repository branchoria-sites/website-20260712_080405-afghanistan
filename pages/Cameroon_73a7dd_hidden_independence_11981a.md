## Introduction

[Cameroon]({{ 'what-still-haunts-cameroons-historical/' | relative_url }}) achieved formal independence on 1 January 1960, yet independence did not end the fighting. Instead, a conflict that began with the suppression of nationalist movements continued for more than a decade, leaving tens of thousands dead, displacing civilians and shaping the country's political future. For decades, however, this violence was rarely described as a war in official accounts. It was presented instead as "pacification", policing or the defeat of rebels. Only in recent years, following the opening of French archives and the work of a joint French-Cameroonian historical commission, has broad scholarly and official recognition emerged that Cameroon experienced a prolonged decolonisation war between roughly 1945 and 1971.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/france-in-cameroon-1945-1971/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kclpure.kcl.ac.uk">[kcl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kclpure.kcl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">france in cameroon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">King&#x27;s College LondonFrance in Cameroon (1945-1971) - King&#x27;s College LondonMarch 31, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2026</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery is therefore not whether the conflict occurred. Rather, it concerns why such a significant war remained so poorly known, how much violence actually took place, who ordered it, and why many of its records and memories remain incomplete or contested.

## From banned nationalism to prolonged war

The roots of the conflict lay in the rise of the Union of the Populations of Cameroon (UPC), which campaigned after the Second World War for immediate independence and the reunification of the French- and British-administered territories. Rather than viewing the movement as a legitimate political opponent, the French administration increasingly regarded it as a security threat during the early Cold War.

The turning point came in 1955 when the UPC was banned. Demonstrations, arrests and repression escalated into armed conflict as sections of the movement retreated into rural areas, especially in the Sanaga-Maritime and Bamileke regions. French military forces, colonial administrators and locally recruited auxiliaries mounted counter-insurgency campaigns combining military operations with intelligence gathering, mass arrests and the relocation of civilians.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/france-in-cameroon-1945-1971/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kclpure.kcl.ac.uk">[kcl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kclpure.kcl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">france in cameroon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">King&#x27;s College LondonFrance in Cameroon (1945-1971) - King&#x27;s College LondonMarch 31, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2026</span></span></span>

A distinctive feature of Cameroon's war is that independence did not bring peace. When Ahmadou Ahidjo became president after independence in 1960, the new Cameroonian state continued fighting remaining UPC guerrillas with extensive French military, intelligence and logistical support. The joint historical commission concluded that French involvement extended well beyond formal decolonisation, continuing within post-independence security cooperation until around 1971.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/france-in-cameroon-1945-1971/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kclpure.kcl.ac.uk">[kcl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kclpure.kcl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">france in cameroon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">King&#x27;s College LondonFrance in Cameroon (1945-1971) - King&#x27;s College LondonMarch 31, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2026</span></span></span>

This chronology helps explain why many older descriptions understated the conflict. It did not fit the simple narrative of colonial rule ending neatly on independence day. Instead, it became a hybrid war spanning colonial administration and sovereign government.

## Why was the war hidden?

The phrase "hidden war" refers less to secrecy during the fighting than to decades of political and historical silence afterwards.

Several factors contributed to this obscurity.

First, official language avoided the term "war". Administrative records frequently described military campaigns as restoring order or conducting pacification operations. Such terminology shaped public understanding for decades and obscured the scale of organised violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/france-in-cameroon-1945-1971/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kclpure.kcl.ac.uk">[King&#x27;s College London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kclpure.kcl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">france in cameroon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">King&#x27;s College LondonFrance in Cameroon (1945-1971) - King&#x27;s College LondonMarch 31, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2026</span></span></span>

Second, neither France nor post-independence Cameroon had strong political incentives to publicise the conflict. France promoted an image of relatively peaceful decolonisation in sub-Saharan Africa, while the Cameroonian government had little interest in celebrating armed opponents it had helped defeat. The result was limited discussion in school curricula, public commemorations and official histories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/afrique/article/2025/02/07/karine-ramondy-historienne-du-cameroun-l-histoire-de-cette-guerre-a-ete-noyee-dans-le-mythe-de-la-decolonisation-dite-pacifique-de-l-afrique-subsaharienne_6536276_3212.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le rapport, remis aux présidents Emmanuel Macron et Paul Biya, décrit la répression massive exercée par la France contre l’Union des popu...</span></span></span>

Third, many survivors remained silent. Families affected by arrests, forced relocation or accusations of supporting the UPC often had practical reasons to avoid discussing their experiences publicly, especially during decades of authoritarian government.

Historians therefore reconstructed much of the conflict only by combining scattered archival records with oral testimony, diplomatic correspondence, military reports and local memory.

## What the archives confirm and what they cannot

Recent archival work has transformed understanding of the conflict without eliminating every uncertainty.

The Franco-Cameroonian commission, established in 2022, examined approximately 1,100 archive boxes, more than 2,300 declassified French documents and around one hundred interviews with witnesses and participants. Its report documents widespread repression before and after independence, including forced displacement, internment camps, torture, targeted assassinations and the destruction of villages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ascleiden.nl/news/ascl-seminar-writing-history-cameroon-war-1945-1971-committee-challenges-practices-and-results" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ascleiden.nl">[African Studies Centre Leiden]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ascleiden.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">African Studies Centre LeidenASCL Seminar: Writing the history of the Cameroon War (1945-1971) in committee: challenges, practices and re...</span></span></span>

Yet archives also reveal their own limitations.

Colonial records were created primarily by administrators and military officers. They often provide detailed accounts of troop movements while giving only fragmentary information about civilian casualties or local experiences. Some documents were never preserved, others remain classified or dispersed across French, Cameroonian, British and United Nations collections.

Access is also uneven. French archival openness has increased substantially since 2022, but researchers continue to report more limited access to some Cameroonian state records, making it difficult to reconstruct complete chains of command or verify every reported incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ascleiden.nl/news/ascl-seminar-writing-history-cameroon-war-1945-1971-committee-challenges-practices-and-results" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ascleiden.nl">[African Studies Centre Leiden]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ascleiden.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">African Studies Centre LeidenASCL Seminar: Writing the history of the Cameroon War (1945-1971) in committee: challenges, practices and re...</span></span></span>

As a result, historians increasingly cross-check official documents against eyewitness testimony, local archives and demographic evidence rather than relying on any single source.

## Why the death toll remains disputed

One of the most persistent historical questions concerns the number of people who died.

There is no universally accepted figure. Modern scholarship generally agrees that the conflict caused at least many tens of thousands of deaths, but estimates vary widely because the war unfolded over many years, involved different regions and blurred the distinction between combatants and civilians.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/afrique/article/2025/01/28/des-historiens-pointent-la-violence-extreme-de-la-repression-francaise-lors-de-la-guerre-de-decolonisation-du-cameroun_6520490_3212.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cette répression a inclus des assassinats ciblés, massacres de civils comme celui d’Ekité (1956), torture, déplacements forcés en camps d...</span></span></span>

Several factors make precise calculation difficult:

* Many civilian deaths were never officially registered.
* Population displacement disrupted normal record keeping.
* Different studies count different categories of victims, including combat deaths, executions, deaths in detention and indirect deaths caused by displacement.
* Surviving military records rarely provide complete civilian casualty data.

</div>

For these reasons, historians increasingly prefer explaining the uncertainty itself rather than presenting a single definitive number unsupported by consistent documentation.

## Responsibility and command

Another unresolved issue concerns responsibility for specific acts of violence.

Broad responsibility is much clearer today than it once was. The historical commission concluded that French authorities directed extensive repression during the colonial period and continued supporting counter-insurgency efforts after independence. Recent statements by the French presidency have formally acknowledged France's responsibility for repressive violence during this period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8ac5c6e04edf71403f877ee8cb2d6e62" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The statement follows a report by a French-Cameroonian commission of historians, launched by Macron in 2022, which documented severe huma...</span></span></span>

Individual responsibility, however, remains more difficult to establish.

Operations involved overlapping civilian administrators, military commanders, intelligence officers, locally recruited auxiliaries and, after independence, Cameroonian security forces operating with varying degrees of French assistance. Surviving documentation does not always identify who authorised individual arrests, executions or village destruction.

Consequently, historians can often describe institutional systems of repression with greater confidence than they can reconstruct every decision within those systems.

## Why public memory changed

The understanding of the conflict has changed markedly during the twenty-first century.

Earlier scholarship by historians and investigative journalists challenged the older narrative of peaceful decolonisation, but the opening of archives and the creation of the joint historical commission gave this research unprecedented official support. The commission's report presented the conflict as a genuine decolonisation war rather than a series of isolated security operations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/france-in-cameroon-1945-1971/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kclpure.kcl.ac.uk">[kcl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kclpure.kcl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">france in cameroon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">King&#x27;s College LondonFrance in Cameroon (1945-1971) - King&#x27;s College LondonMarch 31, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2026</span></span></span>

Subsequent public acknowledgements by France represented another significant shift. For many Cameroonians, the issue now extends beyond historical accuracy. It concerns recognition of victims, preservation of testimony, access to archives and how future generations understand the country's path to independence.

## Why the mystery still matters

The hidden war behind Cameroon's independence illustrates how historical mysteries can arise not from a lack of events but from the long suppression of their documentation and memory.

The broad outline is now well established: a nationalist movement was banned, repression evolved into a prolonged war, French involvement continued after independence, and widespread violence affected civilians across several regions. What remains uncertain are the precise death toll, the full reconstruction of command decisions and the completeness of the surviving documentary record.

Those unanswered questions continue to shape historical research, public remembrance and debates over colonial responsibility. Rather than diminishing the importance of the conflict, the remaining gaps highlight how difficult it can be to recover the full history of a war whose records were fragmented, politically sensitive and, for decades, largely absent from official memory.
