## Introduction

The strongest cases survive not because nothing is known, but because the surviving evidence permits several stories without proving any one of them. Security failures point towards an insider in the theft of the [Irish Crown Jewels]({{ 'crown-jewels/' | relative_url }}), yet identify no culprit. Forensic work supports the authenticity of Casement’s diaries, but has not ended arguments about their handling and political use. Archaeology can reconstruct the final hours of Ireland’s [bog bodies]({{ 'bog-bodies-32e121/' | relative_url }}), but not confidently name the beliefs behind their deaths. Modern mythmaking often fills such gaps. The responsible approach is therefore to separate documented uncertainty from folklore, dramatic theory and retrospective conspiracy.

## The Irish Crown Jewels: an apparent inside job without a culprit

Ireland’s most famous lost treasure was not a crown and had no ancient Gaelic origin. The so-called Irish Crown Jewels were the jewelled star and badge worn by the grand master of the Order of St Patrick, accompanied by five gold collars belonging to knights of the order. The insignia contained hundreds of precious stones and formed part of the ceremonial world of British administration in Ireland. They were kept in the Office of Arms at Dublin Castle, one of the most heavily guarded government complexes in the country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dublincastle.ie/the-theft-of-the-irish-crown-jewels/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dublincastle.ie">[Dublin Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dublincastle.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the theft of the irish crown jewels</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dublin CastleThe Theft of the Irish Crown JewelsThe jewels were discovered to be missing on 6 July 1907. The jewels have never been recov...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1907</span></span></span>

They were discovered missing on 6 July 1907, shortly before a visit by King Edward VII. No obvious force had been used on the safe. The jewels had last been positively seen on 11 June, leaving investigators with a window of more than three weeks. During that period, doors to both the building and the strong room were reportedly found open on separate occasions. Sir Arthur Vicars, the Ulster King of Arms and official custodian of the regalia, controlled the safe keys but had failed to respond effectively to the earlier security breaches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dublincastle.ie/the-theft-of-the-irish-crown-jewels/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dublincastle.ie">[Dublin Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dublincastle.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the theft of the irish crown jewels</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dublin CastleThe Theft of the Irish Crown JewelsThe jewels were discovered to be missing on 6 July 1907. A thief needed either a key, a copy of one, access to an inadequately secured safe, or cooperation from somebody inside the Office of Arms. Yet the inquiry became entangled in accusations about incompetence, private relationships and the conduct of members of Dublin Castle society. Francis Shackleton, brother of the explorer Ernest Shackleton, and Captain Richard Gorges have frequently appeared in later theories, but no prosecution established that either man stole the jewels. Dublin Castle’s own presentation of the case distinguishes such reconstructions from proven fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dublincastle.ie/exhibitions-2/splendour-and-scandal-the-office-of-arms-at-dublin-castle/the-kings-room/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dublincastle.ie">[Dublin Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dublincastle.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dublin CastleThe King&#x27;s RoomThey were reunited later in Ireland and, due to Shackleton&#x27;s need for money, hatched a plan to steal the Iris...</span></span></span>

The scandal also acquired political meaning. Claims that the jewels were secretly recovered, broken up, sold abroad or hidden by republican or unionist figures have never produced verifiable objects or a reliable chain of evidence. The most economical conclusion is that the theft depended on serious internal negligence and probably inside knowledge. The identity of the thief, the route by which the jewels left the castle and their ultimate fate remain unknown.

## Roger Casement’s diaries: authentic documents or political weapons?

Roger Casement was a British diplomat celebrated for exposing abuses in the Congo and the Amazon before becoming an Irish revolutionary. Arrested after attempting to secure German support for the 1916 Rising, he was convicted of treason and executed in London. During the campaign over whether his sentence should be commuted, British officials privately circulated extracts from diaries describing homosexual encounters. Their effect was to weaken support for Casement among influential sympathisers at a time when homosexual acts between men were criminalised and heavily stigmatised.

The surviving collection in the UK National Archives consists of three diaries, a notebook and a ledger. The central dispute is whether Casement wrote the sexually explicit material or whether British agents fabricated or altered it to discredit him. The controversy has endured because the documents cannot be separated from the circumstances in which the government used them. Even an authentic diary could have been selectively circulated for political purposes; proof of political manipulation would not, by itself, prove forgery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C9025" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesCatalogue description Diaries of Roger CasementThe diaries of Sir Roger Casement, who was executed for high treason in 1916...</span></span></span>

Forensic handwriting examinations have generally favoured authenticity. A British examination publicised in the 1990s concluded that most of the writing was Casement’s, while a later study by forensic document examiner Audrey Giles compared handwriting, ink, paper and writing habits and likewise attributed the documents to him. RTÉ reported in 2017 that forensic testing had found the diaries authentic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rte.ie/archives/2017/0312/858482-roger-casements-black-diaries/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rte.ie">[RTE.ie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rte.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">858482 roger casements black diaries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">858482 roger casements black diaries</span></span></span>

Critics argue that the tests did not answer every question. Some have challenged the choice and independence of comparison samples, the limited use of advanced material analysis and the way conclusions were communicated. A published critique of the Giles report argued that similarities among documents assumed to be Casement’s do not automatically establish the authorship of every disputed entry. Other sceptics have called for non-destructive testing capable of detecting alterations, overwritten passages or differences between inks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historyireland.com/revision-casement-tried-tested-giles-report-black-diaries/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyireland.com">[History Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historyireland.com.</span></span></span>

The balance of available forensic evidence supports Casement’s authorship more strongly than the forgery theory. Nevertheless, two historical questions should remain separate. One concerns who wrote the documents. The other concerns how state officials obtained, selected and circulated intimate material during a politically charged clemency campaign. The diaries remain an Irish national controversy partly because arguments about authenticity are inseparable from Casement’s reputation, sexuality, execution and later status as a republican martyr.

## The bog bodies: what science can reveal, and what it cannot

Irish peatlands have preserved human remains with extraordinary detail. Among the best-known examples are Oldcroghan Man from County Offaly and Clonycavan Man from County Meath, discovered in 2003 and dated broadly to the centuries around 400–200 BC. Scientific examination has reconstructed aspects of their diet, health, appearance and injuries. It has also established that they died violently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museum.ie/en-ie/collections-research/irish-antiquities-division-collections/collections-list-%281%29/iron-age/bog-bodies-research-project" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ie">[National Museum of Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.ie.</span></span></span>

Oldcroghan Man had suffered severe wounds and mutilation. Clonycavan Man had received fatal blows to the head and had an elaborate hairstyle held in place by a resin-based preparation whose ingredients indicate long-distance trade. Such evidence transforms the bodies from vague archaeological curiosities into individuals who lived within socially complex Iron Age communities. Yet it does not reveal their names, exact status or the motives of those who killed them.

The National Museum of Ireland’s influential interpretation connects some bog bodies with sovereignty and kingship. On this theory, victims may have been failed or deposed rulers killed as part of rituals intended to restore the fertility and well-being of the land. Their locations near territorial boundaries, the exceptional violence of some deaths and the deposition of weapons, regalia, food-related objects and bog butter in similar landscapes provide a wider archaeological pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museum.ie/en-ie/collections-research/irish-antiquities-division-collections/collections-list-%281%29/iron-age/kingship-sacrifice-a-new-theory-of-sacrifice" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ie">[National Museum of Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of Ireland Kingship &amp; Sacrifice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of Ireland Kingship &amp; Sacrifice</span></span></span>

This is a serious archaeological interpretation, not a solved case. Bog bodies elsewhere in northern Europe have been explained as sacrifices, executed criminals, social outsiders, war victims or people subjected to several different practices over many centuries. Damage caused after burial can also be mistaken for injuries inflicted at death. Archaeologists have therefore cautioned against forcing every body into one universal ritual narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/archaeological-dialogues/article/iron-age-bog-bodies-of-northwestern-europe-representing-the-dead/7AA40B22E62DE69F06ABF0899434EB4B" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery is not whether these people died violently; in several cases, that is well established. It is why they were killed, why their bodies were placed in wetlands and what the bog meant to the communities responsible. “Sacrificed kings” is a plausible explanation for some Irish examples, but it remains a model constructed from patterns rather than a conclusion confirmed by written testimony.

## Books from darkness: the Faddan More Psalter and the Book of Kells

In July 2006, a peat worker in County Tipperary uncovered a badly compressed early medieval manuscript. Now known as the Faddan More Psalter, it contains Latin psalms and dates to about AD 800. Its survival was remarkable: parchment, leather and plant material had been compressed into a wet mass that conservators spent years separating and stabilising.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museum.ie/en-ie/collections-research/collection/the-treasury/artefact/the-faddan-more-psalter/7bdd4b29-54de-4d6d-956f-02b41d24eece" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ie">[National Museum of Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.ie.</span></span></span>

The object is sometimes presented as though it were a coded or inexplicable book. Its basic identity is not mysterious. It is a Christian psalter in a leather cover. The more interesting questions concern how it entered the bog, whether its deposition was deliberate and what its binding reveals about links between Irish monasteries and the wider Christian world. Because the manuscript was removed from its original setting by machinery and survived in a severely disturbed state, archaeology cannot reconstruct the circumstances of deposition with certainty.

A different puzzle surrounds Ireland’s most famous manuscript, the Book of Kells. Its connection with the monastery at Kells in County Meath is secure from the medieval period onwards, but scholars have long debated where its production began. The traditional account associates it with the monastic community of Iona, off the Scottish coast, whose members later established themselves at Kells during the Viking age.

In 2025, researcher Victoria Whitworth argued that the manuscript may instead have been produced at the Pictish monastery of Portmahomack in eastern Scotland, drawing attention to archaeological evidence for vellum production, literacy and artistic parallels at the site. Trinity College Dublin art historian Rachel Moss responded that the movement of artists, churchmen, styles and materials around Ireland and Britain makes an exact place of manufacture exceptionally difficult to prove unless a manuscript records it directly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/books/2025/sep/26/new-research-may-rewrite-origins-of-the-book-of-kells-says-academic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The dispute is a useful warning against treating modern national borders as fixed cultural boundaries in the early medieval world. The Book of Kells can be central to Ireland’s heritage without every stage of its creation having occurred in Ireland. Its origin may ultimately be better understood as the product of a connected monastic culture spanning Ireland, western Scotland, northern Britain and continental Europe.

## Larry Griffin: the postman who vanished on Christmas Day

On 25 December 1929, postman Larry Griffin made his rounds around Stradbally in County Waterford and failed to return to his family in nearby Kilmacthomas. His bicycle was subsequently found abandoned. His body has never been recovered.

The case became extraordinary because investigators did not merely suspect an unknown traveller or opportunistic attacker. The emerging allegation was that Griffin had entered a local public house, become involved in an altercation and died either there or nearby. According to the prosecution theory, members of the community, possibly including police officers, then concealed his death to avoid exposure of illegal Christmas Day drinking and other misconduct.

Ten local people, including civic guards, members of the publican’s family and other prominent residents, were arrested or charged. The case collapsed when a central witness withdrew or altered important testimony, leaving prosecutors unable to establish either a killing or the disposal of Griffin’s body.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Missing_Postman.html?id=aFRNLGCfQv4C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

The theory of a local cover-up remains powerful because it explains several otherwise troubling features: the alleged concentration of Griffin’s last movements around one village, the number of apparently connected suspects and the inconsistencies in witness accounts. But it has never been proved in court, and descendants of those accused have disputed the allegations. Without a body, reliable confession or decisive physical evidence, even the presumed fact of murder cannot be reconstructed in full detail.

Griffin’s disappearance matters beyond its dramatic setting. It exposes the difficulties of investigating a tightly connected rural community in the early years of the Irish state, where police, witnesses, publicans and suspects could be socially dependent on one another. The case survives as both an unresolved disappearance and a debate about whether local solidarity protected innocent neighbours from a weak prosecution or protected those responsible for a death.

## The “Vanishing Triangle”: pattern, label or serial offender?

Between the late 1980s and the 1990s, several women disappeared or were murdered in eastern Ireland. The cases commonly grouped under the media label “Vanishing Triangle” include Annie McCarrick, Jo Jo Dullard, Fiona Pender, Deirdre Jacob, Ciara Breen and Fiona Sinnott. The label suggests a single geographical pattern and, often, a single serial killer. Neither proposition has been established.

There are genuine similarities. Most of the women were young, disappeared suddenly and left little or no recoverable physical evidence. Several were last seen travelling alone, walking near home or depending on lifts at a time before mobile telephones and widespread camera coverage. The eastern and midland landscape contained extensive rural ground where a body could be concealed. But the cases occurred over several years, in different counties and under differing circumstances. Geographical proximity alone cannot demonstrate a common offender.

Officially, investigators treat individual disappearances according to the evidence in each case. Deirdre Jacob, last seen near her Newbridge home on 28 July 1998, had her case reclassified as murder in 2018 after a review and the receipt of new information. Jo Jo Dullard, last confirmed in Moone, County Kildare, at about 11.37 pm on 9 November 1995, has been the subject of a murder investigation since 2020. Annie McCarrick’s 1993 disappearance was reclassified as murder in March 2023.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.garda.ie/en/about-us/our-departments/office-of-corporate-communications/press-releases/2018/august/renewed-appeal-for-missing-person-deirdre-jacob.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: garda.ie">[Garda]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">garda.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Renewed Appeal for Missing Person Deirdre Jacob</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renewed Appeal for Missing Person Deirdre Jacob</span></span></span>

The investigations remain active rather than frozen in folklore. A man was arrested on suspicion of Annie McCarrick’s murder in June 2025. Gardaí have not presented that arrest as a resolution of the case. Jo Jo Dullard’s investigation has generated hundreds of review recommendations, and Gardaí continued public appeals on the thirtieth anniversary of her disappearance. In June 2026, investigators searching for evidence connected with the murders of Dullard and Jacob began examining open ground near the Wicklow–Kildare border.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.garda.ie/en/about-us/our-departments/office-of-corporate-communications/press-releases/2025/june/update-investigation-into-the-disappearance-and-murder-of-annie-mccarrick-march-1993-12th-june-2025.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: garda.ie">[Garda]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">garda.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on garda.ie.</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 1993</span></span></span>

These developments do not prove that the women shared a killer. They show that cold-case teams sometimes identify overlapping information, locations or persons requiring joint examination. The distinction matters. Public discussion has repeatedly attached named offenders to the disappearances without charges or evidence sufficient for a court. Such speculation can distort witness memory, encourage false sightings and reduce individual women to characters in a serial-killer narrative.

The documented mystery is grave enough without embellishment: several women disappeared, most have not been found, and their families have waited decades for answers. The responsible conclusion is that some cases may be connected, but no demonstrated theory presently explains the entire group.

## When folklore becomes false evidence

Ireland has a deep tradition of storytelling about hidden gold, cursed sites, fairy interference, prophetic dreams and supernatural punishment. Such traditions are historically valuable because they show how communities remembered dangerous places, unexplained deaths and disruptive discoveries. They are not automatically factual explanations.

A bog body, for example, may acquire a story about a slain king after its discovery. A ruined settlement may be described as having vanished overnight even when archaeological evidence shows gradual abandonment. Lost treasure stories often expand each time an unidentified coin, weapon or metal object is found nearby. In modern retellings, repetition can make a late anecdote appear as old as the original event.

Several simple tests help distinguish an authentic mystery from accumulated legend:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Is the central event documented close to the time?** Contemporary police files, court papers, newspapers, correspondence and museum records normally deserve more weight than recollections recorded generations later.
* **Does the theory explain evidence or merely add drama?** An insider theory in the Crown Jewels case addresses access to the safe. Claims involving secret royal recovery or political ransom require additional evidence that has not appeared.
* **Has science settled only part of the question?** Dating a bog body or identifying a manuscript does not reveal why it was deposited.
* **Are later labels being mistaken for contemporary facts?** “Vanishing Triangle” is a retrospective media description, not proof that investigators discovered one organised series of crimes.
* **Would the claim survive without anonymous testimony?** Stories based entirely on unnamed officials, deathbed confessions or objects allegedly seen but never photographed should be treated cautiously.

</div>

Folklore can preserve traces of genuine events, but it also supplies satisfying endings where archives offer none. Ireland’s historical mysteries are most revealing when the uncertainty is left visible rather than concealed beneath a polished legend.

## Why these mysteries endure

Each case reflects a wider feature of Irish history. The Crown Jewels theft exposes the closed world and administrative weakness of Dublin Castle before independence. The [Casement diaries]({{ 'casement-diaries/' | relative_url }}) show how sexuality, state power and political reputation became entangled during the revolutionary period. The bog bodies reveal an Iron Age society that left vivid human remains but no indigenous written explanation of its rituals. The Faddan More Psalter and Book of Kells belong to a mobile monastic culture that does not fit neatly inside later national boundaries. Larry Griffin’s disappearance raises questions about trust and authority in a small community of the early Free State. The missing women of the 1990s remain a living test of cold-case investigation and public responsibility.

Some of these mysteries may yet be resolved. Lost objects can reappear; modern searches can recover remains; improved forensic techniques can narrow disputed questions. Others may remain permanently open because the decisive evidence was destroyed, never recorded or misunderstood at the time.

That uncertainty is not a failure of history. It is part of the history itself. The most honest account identifies what the sources establish, shows where interpretation begins and resists turning absence of proof into proof of conspiracy. Ireland’s enduring enigmas matter not simply because their endings are missing, but because the search for those endings reveals how institutions, scholars, families and the public decide what counts as evidence—and what they choose to remember.
