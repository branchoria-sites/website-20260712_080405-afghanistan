## Introduction

The question of whether anyone reached the Azores before the Portuguese is one of [Portugal]({{ 'portugal/' | relative_url }})'s most intriguing archaeological debates. Traditional history holds that the islands were uninhabited until Portuguese navigators began settling them in the fifteenth century. However, a series of environmental studies published since 2015 has challenged that view by suggesting that humans may have visited, or even briefly occupied, parts of the archipelago between about AD 700 and 850. The evidence comes not from buildings or written records, but from lake sediments, ancient pollen, chemical traces and the DNA of house mice. Even so, the case remains unresolved. The scientific evidence points towards the possibility of earlier human activity, but it has not yet produced the kind of archaeological proof—such as settlements, tools or burials—that would settle the question beyond reasonable doubt.

## What the lake sediments reveal

The strongest evidence for pre-Portuguese human activity comes from sediment cores extracted from lakes on islands including Corvo, Flores, Pico, São Miguel and Terceira. These sediments preserve layer-by-layer records of environmental change stretching back thousands of years.

A multidisciplinary study published in 2021 examined pollen, microscopic charcoal, fungal spores, chemical compounds and climate data from these cores. Together, the researchers argued that several changes occurred between roughly AD 700 and 850 that are difficult to explain through natural processes alone. These included:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Increased charcoal, suggesting repeated burning rather than occasional natural fires.
* Declines in native forest pollen, implying woodland clearance.
* The appearance of cereal pollen interpreted as evidence of cultivation.
* Biomarkers associated with the waste of grazing livestock such as cattle.
* Ecological changes consistent with sustained human disturbance rather than a single accidental visit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2021/11/viking-mice-norse-discovered-azores-700-years-portuguese" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.cornell.edu">[news.cornell.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 13, 2021</span></span></span>

</div>

The researchers also compared these environmental changes with reconstructed North Atlantic wind patterns. Climate modelling suggested that prevailing winds during this period would have made voyages from northern Europe to the Azores more practical than they became in later centuries, potentially making accidental or exploratory Norse voyages more feasible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eos.org/articles/sediments-suggest-vikings-may-have-been-the-first-to-settle-the-azores" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eos.org">[Eos]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sediments Suggest Vikings May Have Been the First to Settle the Azores</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sediments Suggest Vikings May Have Been the First to Settle the Azores - Eos...</span></span></span>

Importantly, none of these findings identify individual people. Instead, they indicate that the islands' ecosystems may have been altered by humans centuries before the documented Portuguese settlement.

## The Norse and Viking mice theory

The most widely discussed explanation links the environmental evidence to Norse seafarers.

This idea first gained attention through genetic research on the Azores' house mice. A 2015 study found that mice on some islands possess mitochondrial DNA most closely related to populations from northern Europe rather than Portugal. Because house mice commonly travel as stowaways aboard ships, researchers suggested they may have arrived on Norse vessels during the Viking Age rather than with Portuguese colonists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2021/11/viking-mice-norse-discovered-azores-700-years-portuguese" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.cornell.edu">[news.cornell.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 13, 2021</span></span></span>

When the lake sediment study appeared in 2021, many researchers viewed the two datasets as complementary. The mouse genetics suggested an early northern European connection, while the environmental evidence indicated human disturbance during approximately the same period.

This does not necessarily imply a large Viking colony. Several possibilities remain compatible with the evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Seasonal visits rather than permanent settlement.
* Small exploratory expeditions.
* Temporary livestock stations.
* Short-lived occupation that later disappeared without leaving substantial archaeological remains.

</div>

Because no Norse buildings, tools, inscriptions or cemeteries have yet been found, even supporters of the hypothesis generally avoid claiming that the Azores hosted large Viking communities comparable to Iceland or Greenland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2021/11/viking-mice-norse-discovered-azores-700-years-portuguese" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.cornell.edu">[news.cornell.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 13, 2021</span></span></span>

## Why archaeology has not settled the question

The greatest weakness of the early-settlement hypothesis is that environmental evidence is indirect.

Lake sediments can reveal that landscapes changed, but they cannot identify precisely who caused those changes or how long they stayed. Archaeologists therefore continue to look for material evidence that would independently confirm human occupation before the fifteenth century.

So far, no securely dated pre-Portuguese settlement has been excavated. There are no accepted remains of houses, harbours, workshops or cemeteries from the proposed early medieval period. This absence does not prove nobody visited the islands, but it prevents the environmental evidence from becoming a settled historical fact.

The nature of the Azores also complicates the search. Volcanic activity, erosion, later agriculture and centuries of Portuguese settlement may have disturbed or buried fragile traces of any earlier visitors.

## Why many researchers remain cautious

The 2021 study generated immediate debate within the scientific community.

In 2022, another group of researchers published a formal response arguing that the available evidence does not justify concluding there was widespread Norse settlement before the Portuguese. They pointed out several concerns:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Permanent large-scale deforestation appears mainly after documented Portuguese colonisation rather than before.
* Some pollen changes could have natural explanations.
* Certain chemical biomarkers may derive from large seabird colonies rather than livestock.
* Dating lake sediments is inherently complex, and radiocarbon uncertainties may affect the proposed timeline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794843/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 25, 2022</span></span></span>

</div>

The critics did not insist that earlier human visits were impossible. Instead, they argued that the evidence currently supports only the possibility of pre-Portuguese human presence, not a demonstrated episode of sustained colonisation.

This distinction is important because environmental proxies—indirect scientific indicators—are interpreted through models and comparisons rather than observed directly. Different researchers can therefore reach different conclusions from the same underlying data.

## What remains uncertain

Several key questions continue to drive research.

**Who arrived?**

The Norse remain the leading hypothesis because of the mouse genetics, the proposed dates and the known expansion of Viking seafaring across the North Atlantic. However, no artefact directly links the environmental changes to Scandinavians, and alternative visitors cannot be completely excluded.

**How many people came?**

The evidence cannot distinguish between a handful of explorers, seasonal visitors or a small settlement.

**How long did they stay?**

Environmental signals may represent decades of activity, but they do not reveal whether occupation was continuous or intermittent.

**Why did they leave?**

If people did establish a temporary presence, no historical record explains why it ended before Portuguese arrival.

## Why the mystery matters

The debate illustrates how new scientific techniques can challenge long-standing historical narratives without immediately replacing them.

For centuries, the accepted account was straightforward: the Portuguese reached an uninhabited archipelago in the fifteenth century and began permanent settlement. Environmental science has now introduced credible evidence that this picture may be incomplete. Yet archaeology has not produced the decisive physical discoveries needed to rewrite the history books.

The result is a genuine historical mystery rather than a solved case. The environmental record makes pre-Portuguese human activity increasingly plausible, especially during the early Middle Ages, but the identity, scale and permanence of those visitors remain unresolved. Until excavations uncover securely dated archaeological evidence, the question of who first reached the Azores before the Portuguese will remain open.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/8/pgaf215/8219823" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[oup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ord AcademicAugust 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 5, 2025</span></span></span>
