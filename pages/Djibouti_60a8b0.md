## Introduction

The evidence is uneven. The 1995 death of Judge Bernard Borrel is a genuine unresolved criminal case supported by forensic findings and decades of litigation. Mahamoud Harbi’s death in 1960 is historically suspicious to some Djiboutians, but there is no solid public evidence that his aircraft was sabotaged. Abourma and Handoga are archaeological enigmas rather than unsolved crimes: their existence is certain, while their dates, social meanings and historical connections remain under investigation. Together, these cases reveal why Djibouti’s past can be difficult to reconstruct. Colonial records are scattered, archaeological research has been limited, politically sensitive evidence has disappeared, and much of the country’s cultural memory was preserved orally or in archives that were inaccessible for years.

## The death of Judge Bernard Borrel

The most substantial unresolved mystery in modern Djiboutian history began on 19 October 1995, when the partly burned body of Bernard Borrel, a French magistrate working as an adviser to Djibouti’s Ministry of Justice, was discovered below a cliff roughly 80 kilometres from Djibouti City. His vehicle had been found above the site. The initial local investigation concluded that he had poured petrol over himself, set himself alight and fallen down the slope.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/app/conversion/pdf/?filename=003-4765427-5798804.pdf&amp;id=003-4765427-5798804&amp;library=ECHR" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hudoc.echr.coe.int">[HUDOC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hudoc.echr.coe.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HUDOCGrand Chamber hearing Morice v</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">France - HUDOCMay 21, 2014 — 21 May 2014 — He is the lawyer of Elisabeth Borrel, the widow of the French judge Bernard Borrel, whose dead...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 21, 2014</span></span></span>

That explanation did not survive close examination. No autopsy was conducted before Borrel was buried in France. After his widow, fellow magistrate Elisabeth Borrel, secured an exhumation, medical examination found no soot in his airways, indicating that he had probably not been breathing while his body burned. A later examination identified injuries to his skull and forearm consistent with blows from a blunt object. Investigators also found that the flammable substance detected on his body differed from the fuel in the container recovered near the cliff. A multidisciplinary forensic assessment presented in 2017 supported the conclusion that the death was criminal rather than suicidal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/en/le-monde-africa/article/2026/04/10/a-french-judge-was-found-burned-at-a-cliff-in-djibouti-in-1995-how-and-why-he-died-remains-a-mystery_6752298_124.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How and why he died remains a mysteryIn 1995, French judge Bernard Borrel was found partially burned at the base of a cliff in Djibouti u...</span></span></span>

These findings establish a strong minimum conclusion: the original suicide scenario was gravely defective, and the physical evidence points towards an assault followed by an attempt to burn the body. What they do not establish is who killed Borrel or why.

### What might Borrel have known?

Borrel had been sent to Djibouti through a French judicial cooperation programme. Among his duties, he assisted work connected with the 1990 Café de Paris bombing, in which a child was killed and about 20 people were injured. Later investigators explored allegations that people linked to that attack, senior Djiboutian figures or trafficking networks might have had a motive to silence him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/en/le-monde-africa/article/2026/04/10/a-french-judge-was-found-burned-at-a-cliff-in-djibouti-in-1995-how-and-why-he-died-remains-a-mystery_6752298_124.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How and why he died remains a mysteryIn 1995, French judge Bernard Borrel was found partially burned at the base of a cliff in Djibouti u...</span></span></span>

One influential allegation came from Mohamed Saleh Alhoumekani, a former officer in Djibouti’s Republican Guard. He claimed that, after Borrel’s death, he heard men arriving at the presidential compound announce that the troublesome judge was dead and that no trace remained. The allegation helped move the French inquiry towards a murder hypothesis, but it remains witness testimony rather than independent proof of a chain of command.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/en/le-monde-africa/article/2026/04/10/a-french-judge-was-found-burned-at-a-cliff-in-djibouti-in-1995-how-and-why-he-died-remains-a-mystery_6752298_124.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How and why he died remains a mysteryIn 1995, French judge Bernard Borrel was found partially burned at the base of a cliff in Djibouti u...</span></span></span>

Other proposed motives have included Borrel’s possible knowledge of arms dealing, uranium trafficking or politically protected criminal networks. Such theories have circulated for years, but no publicly tested body of evidence has conclusively shown which investigation, transaction or secret placed him in danger. The distinction matters: the forensic case for homicide is far stronger than any single theory about the perpetrators.

### Why the case became an institutional scandal

The mystery was deepened by failures within both the French and Djiboutian systems. Evidence was mishandled, investigative decisions were contested, and judicial cooperation became entangled in a diplomatic dispute between the two countries. Djibouti eventually brought proceedings against France at the International Court of Justice over France’s handling of requests connected with the criminal file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj-cij.org/node/101696" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj-cij.org">[International Court of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj-cij.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Court of JusticeThe Republic of Djibouti seises the International Court...10 Jan 2006 — againstX in the Borrel case, Franc...</span></span></span>

French proceedings surrounding the case also reached the European Court of Human Rights. In litigation involving the press and Borrel family lawyer Olivier Morice, the court records documented the disputed suicide finding, the subsequent murder complaint and serious criticism of the original investigation. The European litigation did not identify Borrel’s killer, but it showed that concern about the inquiry was not confined to rumour or political opposition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-85084" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hudoc.echr.coe.int">[HUDOC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hudoc.echr.coe.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HUDOCJULY AND SARL LIBERATION v</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FRANCE [Extracts] - HUDOC“Bernard Borrel, a judge, died in Djibouti where he had been posted for a few months, on 18 or 19 October 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1995</span></span></span>

The most damaging later revelation concerned the destruction of physical evidence. In 2020, the French state was found liable for serious procedural failings, including the premature burial of Borrel before an autopsy and the loss or destruction of dozens of exhibits. Those materials reportedly included his clothing, a lighter, biological traces and threatening packages sent to his widow. Their disappearance may have permanently reduced the possibility of using modern forensic methods to identify an assailant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/en/le-monde-africa/article/2026/04/10/a-french-judge-was-found-burned-at-a-cliff-in-djibouti-in-1995-how-and-why-he-died-remains-a-mystery_6752298_124.html?srsltid=AfmBOooLP-bmz6za05kFjucP6so9YMiWZBvM80UoqBheeJsH7iXLbWpN" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le Monde.frA French judge was found burned at a cliff in Djibouti...10 Apr 2026 — Three years later, the French state was found liable f...</span></span></span>

More than 30 years after the death, a French murder inquiry remained active in 2026. Borrel’s widow met yet another investigating judge in March of that year. The case endures because the central question has narrowed rather than vanished: the strongest available evidence indicates that Borrel was killed, but the surviving record has not produced a prosecutable account of who organised or carried out the crime.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/en/le-monde-africa/article/2026/04/10/a-french-judge-was-found-burned-at-a-cliff-in-djibouti-in-1995-how-and-why-he-died-remains-a-mystery_6752298_124.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How and why he died remains a mysteryIn 1995, French judge Bernard Borrel was found partially burned at the base of a cliff in Djibouti u...</span></span></span>

## Did Mahamoud Harbi die by accident?

Mahamoud Harbi occupies a central place in Djibouti’s independence history. A trade-union leader and prominent politician in French Somaliland, he opposed continued French rule and favoured association with a wider Somali state. Following his political defeat and departure from the territory, he continued campaigning abroad. On 29 September 1960, he boarded United Arab Airlines Flight 738 from Geneva towards Cairo, with planned stops in Rome and Athens. The Vickers Viscount crashed into the Mediterranean near Elba, killing everyone aboard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://asn.flightsafety.org/wikibase/333709" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asn.flightsafety.org">[asn.flightsafety.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asn.flightsafety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on flightsafety.org.</span></span></span>

The crash became fertile ground for suspicion because Harbi was a high-profile anti-colonial politician who had powerful enemies and died at a decisive moment in the Horn of Africa’s decolonisation. Later accounts have occasionally suggested French extremists or intelligence interests may have arranged his death. Yet the surviving aviation record does not provide evidence of sabotage.

The official accident summary describes the aircraft departing Geneva at 10.05 GMT and losing contact while flying towards Rome. Search aircraft later located wreckage in the sea. The published record could not determine why the aircraft disappeared, largely because the wreck was not recovered in a condition that allowed a complete technical reconstruction. An undetermined accident, however, is not evidence of an assassination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://asn.flightsafety.org/wikibase/333709" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asn.flightsafety.org">[asn.flightsafety.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asn.flightsafety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on flightsafety.org.</span></span></span>

Several possibilities therefore need to be kept separate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Established fact:** Harbi and the other occupants died when Flight 738 crashed near Elba.
* **Documented uncertainty:** investigators did not establish a definitive technical cause.
* **Reasonable historical context:** Harbi was politically important, opposed to French rule and active in international anti-colonial networks.
* **Unsupported claim:** no publicly available credible evidence demonstrates that a bomb, intelligence operation or extremist organisation brought down the aircraft.

</div>

Harbi’s death remains a national enigma because an unexplained crash removed a major independence leader and changed the political landscape. It is legitimate to ask whether he was targeted. It is not legitimate to present deliberate killing as the most likely answer without new documents, forensic material or reliable testimony.

The case also illustrates how political memory works. When a leader dies unexpectedly under colonial rule, the absence of a satisfactory technical explanation can become part of the historical meaning of the event. Suspicion survives not because every accusation is credible, but because the crash occurred within a broader record of repression, contested referendums, exile and unequal access to state archives.

## What do the engravings at Abourma mean?

In the Makarassou Massif of northern Djibouti, the Abourma site extends for nearly three kilometres across a rocky landscape. Its hundreds of engravings include cattle, people and wild animals associated with wetter environments. UNESCO describes it as the largest known rock-art site in Djibouti and one of the most extensive in East Africa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/djibouti-considers-nominating-abourma-rock-art-site-unesco-world-heritage-list" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The most striking mystery is environmental. Today the surrounding region is severely arid, yet the engraved animals include species that imply grasslands, dependable water and richer vegetation. The images preserve cultural evidence of a period when the landscape and the lives it supported were markedly different.

But rock art is difficult to date directly. Engravings contain no written year, and the exposed stone may have been reused over long periods. Researchers must instead compare artistic styles, weathering, superimposed images, nearby archaeological deposits and the known history of livestock and climate in the Horn of Africa. Broad claims that every Abourma image is exactly 7,000 years old therefore go beyond what the evidence can sustain. The site probably contains material from more than one phase, produced by communities whose identities and languages are unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240533206_Pastoral_rock_art_in_the_Horn_of_Africa_Making_sense_of_udder_chaos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The engravings raise several unresolved questions. Were they records of animals seen locally, statements of wealth, markers on seasonal routes, teaching images or expressions of ritual belief? Did particular figures represent individual households or social ranks? Were human and animal images made together, or were older panels repeatedly altered by later pastoralists?

UNESCO’s assessment cautiously emphasises that the engravings reflect the behaviour and social organisation of a vanished pastoral world. That is a defensible interpretation, especially where cattle and human figures appear together. More elaborate claims—that individual scenes record named rulers, specific ceremonies or a lost kingdom—remain speculative unless supported by associated settlements, dated artefacts or inscriptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/djibouti-considers-nominating-abourma-rock-art-site-unesco-world-heritage-list" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Abourma matters because it offers something written sources cannot: a long visual record created by people who left no conventional archive. Its mystery is not simply “who carved these pictures?” but how changing climate, animal herding, movement and social identity transformed the Horn thousands of years before Djibouti appeared as a modern state.

## Why was the medieval town of Handoga abandoned?

West of Dikhil, near the Ethiopian border, lies Handoga, a settlement covering roughly 16 hectares. Archaeologists have mapped numerous circular and oval stone structures, compounds and open areas that may have served as courtyards, squares or communal spaces. Excavation indicates that the medieval town flourished between approximately the 13th and 16th centuries and occupied a strategic position on routes linking the Gulf of Tadjoura with the African interior.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/2613" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[dainst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dainst.org.</span></span></span>

Handoga complicates the old assumption that mobile pastoral communities and urban life were opposites. Beneath some stone buildings, archaeologists found traces of earlier structures made with more temporary construction techniques. The sequence suggests that people familiar with mobility may gradually have adopted permanent architecture without simply abandoning a pastoral identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/2613" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[iDAI Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dainst.org.</span></span></span>

Finds include locally made pottery, glass ornaments, cowrie shells, metalworking remains and a rare Chinese coin recovered from a secure archaeological context. The coin does not prove that Chinese merchants visited Handoga. It more cautiously demonstrates that an object originating far to the east could move through linked Indian Ocean and caravan networks before reaching the Djiboutian interior.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/121523848/A_Chinese_coin_found_in_the_medieval_town_of_Handoga_Djibouti_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A Chinese coin found in the medieval town of Handoga</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Chinese coin found in the medieval town of Handoga</span></span></span>

The mystery is why a settlement of this scale emerged there and then faded from the historical record. Possible explanations include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* changes in caravan routes between the coast and Ethiopian highlands;
* political disruption during the wars that affected the medieval Muslim sultanates and neighbouring Christian kingdoms;
* environmental pressure on water, grazing or cultivation;
* a gradual return to more mobile ways of living;
* the rise of competing ports or inland commercial centres.

</div>

Current excavation has not produced a single destruction layer proving that Handoga ended in one violent event. Abandonment may have been gradual, with households leaving at different times as trade or ecological conditions changed. This is an important corrective to the romantic image of a “lost city” suddenly swallowed by the desert. Handoga is lost mainly because written records have not yet been securely matched to the ruins.

Its archaeological value lies in the possibility of reconnecting inland Djibouti with the wider history of the Red Sea and Indian Ocean. Medieval accounts tend to foreground ports, rulers and wars. Handoga preserves the material lives of less visible people: cooks, herders, craft workers, traders and families living between settled and mobile worlds.

## The archive that was present but unheard

Not every Djiboutian mystery concerns a death or an ancient ruin. For decades, recordings held by the country’s national broadcaster were largely inaccessible outside Djibouti and unfamiliar even to many specialists in African music. When researchers and Ostinato Records gained access to the radio collection in 2019, they encountered an extensive musical record shaped by Somali, Afar, Arabic, Ethiopian and international influences. Selected recordings were subsequently digitised and released.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trtworld.com/article/12764642" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trtworld.com">[TRT World]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trtworld.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trtworld.com.</span></span></span>

The collection was not literally a vanished archive. It had been preserved by the broadcaster, but political controls, limited resources and the absence of international distribution made much of it effectively unheard. Its reappearance challenged the assumption that Djibouti lacked a major recorded musical history.

The archive also exposes a wider historical problem: what survives publicly is not the same as what once existed. Radio recordings may preserve performers, languages, political messages and styles absent from written histories. At the same time, the available releases represent only a selection. Important questions remain about the size and condition of the full collection, how recordings were catalogued, which performances were never retained and how artists should be credited or compensated when old broadcasts circulate commercially.

This is an archival mystery in the most constructive sense. It shows how a country’s cultural past can remain hidden in plain sight—not because it was fictional or deliberately erased, but because fragile tapes, restricted institutions and unequal global attention kept it beyond ordinary hearing.

## What Djibouti’s mysteries actually tell us

The strongest Djiboutian mysteries do not all belong in the same evidential category. Bernard Borrel’s death is an unresolved homicide in which lost evidence and state interests obstructed justice. Mahamoud Harbi’s fatal crash is an unexplained aviation disaster whose political context has encouraged suspicions that remain unproved. Abourma is a prehistoric interpretive puzzle, while Handoga is an archaeological problem of trade, settlement and abandonment. The radio collection is a reminder that archival absence may reflect inaccessibility rather than destruction.

Treating these cases responsibly means resisting two opposite errors. One is to dismiss every unresolved question because no final answer exists. The other is to convert every gap into proof of conspiracy. Djibouti’s history is more revealing between those extremes.

What survives points to a country shaped by movement: pastoral routes, caravan trade, colonial administration, anti-colonial exile, military strategy and cultural exchange across the Red Sea. What remains uncertain often reflects the institutions that controlled the record—colonial governments, courts, broadcasters, diplomatic services and archaeological organisations. The enduring mystery is therefore not only what happened in each case, but why some lives and events were documented in detail while others were left as fragments in stone, damaged evidence, scattered files and half-remembered testimony.
