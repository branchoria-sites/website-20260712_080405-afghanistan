## Introduction

One of the greatest unsolved puzzles in the history of ancient Arabia is not the fall of a city or the disappearance of a treasure, but a king's unexplained decision. Around 552 BC, Nabonidus, the last king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, left Babylon and established himself at the oasis of Tayma in what is now north-west [Saudi Arabia]({{ 'saudi-arabia/' | relative_url }}). He remained there for roughly a decade, governing an empire from hundreds of kilometres away while his son, Belshazzar, managed affairs in Babylon. Contemporary inscriptions confirm that the move happened. What they do not explain is why. The surviving records are damaged, politically biased or frustratingly brief, leaving historians to weigh competing explanations involving trade, military strategy, religion and internal politics. None fully accounts for why a reigning monarch would abandon one of the ancient world's greatest capitals for an Arabian oasis.

## What the surviving Babylonian records actually say

The basic outline is well established. Nabonidus campaigned into north-west Arabia, captured Tayma and made it a royal residence. Later inscriptions state that he also exercised authority over neighbouring oasis settlements including Dedan, Khaybar, Fadak and Yathrib, showing that his presence extended across an important section of the Arabian caravan network.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/anatolian-studies/article/harran-inscriptions-of-nabonidus/3B52586A0B5A294459A200F2E8F60A28" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Harran Inscriptions of Nabonidus &#124; Anatolian Studies &#124; Cambridge CoreDecember 23, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 23, 2013</span></span></span>

The most important written sources include:

* **The Nabonidus Chronicle**, a Babylonian court record, which confirms the king's prolonged absence from Babylon and notes that Belshazzar carried out many royal duties in his place.
* **The Harran inscriptions**, written under Nabonidus himself, which emphasise his devotion to the moon god Sin but say remarkably little about the practical reasons for relocating.
* **The Verse Account of Nabonidus**, written after the Persian conquest, which portrays the king as impious or irrational. Because it was composed under the victorious Persian regime, historians treat it as valuable but politically hostile evidence rather than an impartial account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/anatolian-studies/article/harran-inscriptions-of-nabonidus/3B52586A0B5A294459A200F2E8F60A28" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Harran Inscriptions of Nabonidus &#124; Anatolian Studies &#124; Cambridge CoreDecember 23, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 23, 2013</span></span></span>

Archaeology from Tayma has strengthened the historical picture. Saudi-German excavations uncovered a damaged Babylonian royal stele, inscriptions and other material indicating an unmistakable Babylonian presence. These discoveries prove that Nabonidus was not merely campaigning there temporarily; he invested in establishing an enduring royal base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1600-0471.2006.00269.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Archaeology and epigraphy at Tayma (Saudi Arabia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryArchaeology and epigraphy at Tayma (Saudi Arabia) - Eichmann - 2006 - Arabian Archaeology and Epigraphy - Wiley Onlin...</span></span></span>

Yet despite these discoveries, no surviving inscription clearly states: "I came to Tayma because..."

## Why would a Babylonian king choose Tayma?

Historians generally agree that no single explanation adequately accounts for a ten-year royal residence. Instead, several overlapping motives remain under debate.

### Trade and economic control

Tayma occupied a strategic position on long-distance caravan routes linking southern Arabia with the Levant and Mesopotamia. These routes carried incense, aromatics, precious goods and luxury products that generated considerable wealth.

By occupying Tayma directly, Nabonidus could potentially:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* supervise caravan taxation;
* secure valuable trade revenues;
* extend Babylonian influence deep into Arabia;
* reduce dependence on local rulers.

</div>

Archaeology confirms that Tayma was already a prosperous fortified oasis with extensive regional connections before Nabonidus arrived, making it an attractive administrative centre rather than an isolated desert outpost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1600-0471.2006.00269.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Archaeology and epigraphy at Tayma (Saudi Arabia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryArchaeology and epigraphy at Tayma (Saudi Arabia) - Eichmann - 2006 - Arabian Archaeology and Epigraphy - Wiley Onlin...</span></span></span>

The weakness of this theory is one of scale. Control of trade may explain conquering Tayma, but it does not obviously require the emperor himself to remain there for roughly ten years instead of appointing governors.

### Military and frontier strategy

Another explanation views Tayma as an advanced military headquarters.

During Nabonidus' reign the political map of the Near East was changing rapidly. The Median kingdom remained powerful, while Cyrus the Great was beginning the expansion that would eventually destroy the Babylonian Empire.

From Tayma, Nabonidus could:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://test.livius.org/sources/content/anet/verse-account-of-nabonidus?level=5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: test.livius.org">[test.livius.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">test.livius.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">From the following text, known as the Verse Accou</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Account of Nabonidus – Livius.orgJuly 28, 2022 — VERSE ACCOUNT OF NABONIDUS Nabonidus on a relief from Harran The question what Nabonidus...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2022</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* secure Babylon's south-western frontier;
* oversee campaigns into Arabia and neighbouring territories;
* protect caravan routes from raids;
* maintain influence over regions lying between Mesopotamia, the Levant and Arabia.

</div>

Rock inscriptions elsewhere in Jordan and Saudi Arabia suggest wider Babylonian military activity during this period, although the evidence is fragmentary and difficult to interpret.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/janeh-2019-0003/html?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: degruyterbrill.com">[De Gruyter Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">degruyterbrill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">De Gruyter Brill The Neo-Babylonian Empire: The Imperial Periphery as Seen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">De Gruyter Brill The Neo-Babylonian Empire: The Imperial Periphery as Seen</span></span></span>

This interpretation explains why Babylon invested heavily in the oasis, but it still leaves open the question of why the king himself needed to remain there year after year.

### Religious devotion

The religious explanation has attracted attention since antiquity.

Nabonidus showed unusual devotion to the moon god Sin, whose principal cult centres lay at Harran and Ur. His inscriptions repeatedly praise Sin in language stronger than many earlier Babylonian kings had used, and he sponsored the restoration of temples dedicated to the deity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/anatolian-studies/article/harran-inscriptions-of-nabonidus/3B52586A0B5A294459A200F2E8F60A28" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Harran Inscriptions of Nabonidus &#124; Anatolian Studies &#124; Cambridge CoreDecember 23, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 23, 2013</span></span></span>

Later hostile writers accused him of neglecting Babylon's chief god Marduk and abandoning traditional royal religious duties. The annual New Year festival in Babylon required the king's presence, so his prolonged absence disrupted one of the kingdom's most important ceremonies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://test.livius.org/sources/content/anet/verse-account-of-nabonidus?level=2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: test.livius.org">[test.livius.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">test.livius.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Verse Account of Nabonidus – Livius.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Verse Account of Nabonidus – Livius.org</span></span></span>

Some historians therefore argue that Tayma offered a religious environment more compatible with Nabonidus' personal beliefs, or at least a place where he could pursue policies that met resistance in Babylon.

The difficulty is that even Nabonidus' own inscriptions never explicitly state that religion alone motivated his move, and many modern scholars suspect later critics exaggerated religious conflict to explain Babylon's eventual defeat.

### Domestic politics

Some researchers also consider political tensions inside Babylon.

Nabonidus came to the throne after a period of instability and was not a direct descendant of Nebuchadnezzar II. Powerful priestly groups in Babylon may have viewed him with suspicion, particularly if they believed he favoured Sin over Marduk.

Living in Tayma may therefore have reduced confrontation with influential Babylonian elites while allowing Belshazzar to handle routine administration in the capital.

This interpretation fits some of the political evidence but remains difficult to prove because surviving records rarely describe internal disagreements openly. Most of the detailed accusations come from texts written after Cyrus had conquered Babylon, when portraying the previous king as an unsuitable ruler served Persian political interests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://test.livius.org/sources/content/anet/verse-account-of-nabonidus?level=2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: test.livius.org">[test.livius.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">test.livius.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Verse Account of Nabonidus – Livius.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Verse Account of Nabonidus – Livius.org</span></span></span>

## Why no single theory fully explains the decade-long stay

The enduring mystery exists because every explanation solves part of the puzzle while leaving major questions unanswered.

Economic control explains why Tayma mattered but not why Nabonidus personally stayed there for so long.

Military strategy explains Babylonian expansion into Arabia but not the extraordinary duration of the royal residence.

Religious devotion matches Nabonidus' inscriptions yet does not fully account for governing an empire from a distant oasis.

Political conflict could have encouraged his absence, but the evidence comes largely from hostile or retrospective sources.

Modern historians increasingly favour combinations of these explanations rather than searching for one decisive motive. Tayma may simultaneously have been a strategic trade hub, a military headquarters, a religious centre and a place where Nabonidus found greater political freedom than in Babylon itself. The surviving evidence is simply too incomplete to determine which factor mattered most.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1600-0471.2006.00269.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Archaeology and epigraphy at Tayma (Saudi Arabia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryArchaeology and epigraphy at Tayma (Saudi Arabia) - Eichmann - 2006 - Arabian Archaeology and Epigraphy - Wiley Onlin...</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery still matters

Nabonidus' stay transformed Tayma from a prosperous Arabian oasis into a temporary centre of imperial government. Archaeological discoveries in Saudi Arabia—including the damaged royal stele, Babylonian inscriptions and evidence of long-distance connections—have confirmed that the Arabian Peninsula played a more direct role in Near Eastern politics than historians once appreciated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1600-0471.2006.00269.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Archaeology and epigraphy at Tayma (Saudi Arabia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryArchaeology and epigraphy at Tayma (Saudi Arabia) - Eichmann - 2006 - Arabian Archaeology and Epigraphy - Wiley Onlin...</span></span></span>

At the same time, the missing motive remains one of the most intriguing gaps in the written record of the ancient world. Unlike many historical mysteries, the uncertainty is not caused by folklore or legend but by damaged texts, political propaganda and the silence of surviving evidence. The facts of Nabonidus' residence at Tayma are no longer in doubt; what remains elusive is the reasoning that led Babylon's last king to exchange the palaces of Mesopotamia for an oasis in north-west Arabia.
