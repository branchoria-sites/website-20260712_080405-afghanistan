## Introduction

In each case, the underlying event is real. What remains uncertain is responsibility, motive or meaning. These mysteries matter because they reveal a recurring problem in Central African history: evidence has often been controlled by colonial authorities, authoritarian governments, foreign powers or armed groups. The result is not an absence of history, but a history in which important conclusions must sometimes remain provisional.

## Who killed Barthélemy Boganda?

On 29 March 1959, Barthélemy Boganda boarded an aircraft at Berbérati for a flight to Bangui. The plane crashed near Boda, killing Boganda, the other passengers and the crew. His death came at an exceptionally sensitive moment: he was leading the autonomous Central African Republic towards full independence, which was achieved in August 1960.

Boganda was far more than a conventional party leader. A former Catholic priest, he had campaigned against colonial abuses, founded the Movement for the Social Evolution of Black Africa and imagined a federation linking several territories of French Equatorial Africa. His political stature, undefeated electoral record and central role in creating the new republic made his sudden death a national turning point.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ascleiden.nl/news/historical-long-term-politics-central-african-republic-insights-biography-barthelemy-boganda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ascleiden.nl">[ascleiden.nl]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ascleiden.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">African Studies Centre Leideninsights from the biography of Barthélémy Boganda (1910...29 Oct 2019 — Just more than 60 years ago Barthél...</span></span></span>

### What is firmly established

The aircraft disappeared while travelling from Berbérati towards Bangui, and its wreckage was found the following day. French civil-aviation authorities opened an investigation, but no complete public report definitively explaining the disaster emerged. Contemporary accounts later claimed that investigators had detected traces of explosive material in the wreckage. That reported finding became the foundation of the assassination theory, although the underlying technical evidence has never been made readily available for independent examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barth%C3%A9lemy_Boganda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Barthélemy Boganda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Barthélemy Boganda</span></span></span>

The secrecy surrounding the inquiry is itself important. An unpublished or inaccessible investigation does not prove murder, but it prevents later researchers from evaluating the aircraft’s condition, weather, maintenance, flight path and reported explosive traces together. In an ordinary accident investigation, those details would be central to distinguishing mechanical failure, pilot error, structural break-up and sabotage.

### Why assassination remains plausible

Boganda had powerful opponents. He had attacked forced labour and the practices of colonial concessionary companies, while his political ambitions threatened established commercial and administrative interests. His proposed federation could also have altered the balance of power across French Equatorial Africa. These circumstances provide possible motives, but motive alone cannot identify a perpetrator.

Suspicions have consequently spread in several directions: towards colonial business interests, sections of the French establishment and people within Boganda’s private or political circle. Some later accounts have added claims about insurance, family tensions or intelligence involvement. None has produced a complete chain of verifiable evidence connecting a named suspect to the aircraft.

The fairest verdict is therefore that sabotage is credible but unproved. The reported explosive evidence and missing public report make a simple declaration of “accident” unsatisfactory. Equally, the available record does not justify confidently accusing France, a particular company or an individual conspirator.

### Why the crash became a national “what if”

Boganda’s death left the approaching independence settlement in the hands of David Dacko, his cousin and political successor. The country then passed through repeated coups, authoritarian governments and foreign interventions. This later history has encouraged the belief that Boganda might have placed the state on a different path.

That possibility cannot be tested. Boganda faced severe structural difficulties, including weak infrastructure, a small administrative class, dependence on France and borders inherited from colonial rule. Yet because he died before independence, his political programme was never subjected to the pressures of governing a sovereign state. His reputation could therefore remain associated with an unrealised national future rather than the compromises and failures of office.

The crash is consequently both a forensic mystery and a foundational national question: was the Central African Republic deprived of its most promising leader by an accident, or was its political future deliberately altered before independence had even arrived?

## What happened to Charles Massi?

Charles Massi’s disappearance is a more recent case, but it presents the same central problem: the state never provided a convincing, transparent account of a politically significant death.

Massi had served in government before becoming a leading opponent of President François Bozizé. He was associated with the Convention of Patriots for Justice and Peace, an armed movement active in northern Central African Republic. Around the end of 2009 or beginning of 2010, he reportedly entered Chad, was detained there and was allegedly transferred into Central African custody. He was never seen publicly again.

The United Nations later described Massi’s case as the most thoroughly documented enforced disappearance attributed to the Bozizé period. Amnesty International recorded allegations that Central African government forces killed him after Chadian authorities handed him over. Contemporary United States reporting also noted that no reliable further information about his fate had become available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/zh-hans/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr190012011en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[amnesty.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalCENTRAL AFRICAN REPUBLICSeptember 20, 2011 — disappearance and alleged death of its leader, Charles Massi, in Januar...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 2011</span></span></span>

### The likely sequence

Several accounts agree on the broad outline:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Massi was detained while moving through Chad.
* He was reportedly delivered to Central African authorities.
* He then disappeared while beyond the protection of courts, lawyers or independent observers.
* Officials either denied holding him or failed to explain what happened after the alleged transfer.

</div>

This pattern closely matches an enforced disappearance: detention followed by concealment of the prisoner’s fate or whereabouts. The central uncertainty is not whether Massi vanished, but precisely where he was taken, who interrogated him, when he died and who authorised his treatment.

Reports circulated that he had been tortured and killed in custody, possibly at a military facility. Those claims are plausible and consistent with the findings of later international investigators, but no publicly tested forensic record has settled the details. No body has been formally identified, and no credible judicial process has established individual responsibility.

### Why the case still matters

Massi was not merely a missing private citizen. His disappearance affected an armed conflict. Amnesty reported that attacks by his movement escalated after his alleged death, showing how an unresolved state crime could deepen rebellion rather than remove its cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/zh-hans/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/afr190012011en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalCENTRAL AFRICAN REPUBLICSeptember 20, 2011 — disappearance and alleged death of its leader, Charles Massi, in Januar...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 2011</span></span></span>

The case also illustrates why disappearances are politically useful to abusive governments. A public execution produces a date, a body and an acknowledged decision. A disappearance creates fear while allowing officials to deny responsibility. Families cannot mourn with certainty, witnesses remain vulnerable and later investigators must reconstruct events from fragments.

The most plausible explanation is that Massi died after being transferred into Central African custody. The unresolved questions concern the exact circumstances and command responsibility. Unless official records, testimony from surviving personnel or human remains emerge, those questions may never receive a judicially secure answer.

## What is fact and myth in the Bokassa era?

Jean-Bédel Bokassa, who ruled from 1966 to 1979 and proclaimed himself emperor in 1976, occupies an unusual place in public memory. Documented killings and torture coexist with stories of cannibalism, grotesque extravagance and theatrical cruelty. The result is that genuine crimes are sometimes obscured by the most sensational allegations.

A responsible account must separate three issues: the killing of schoolchildren in 1979, Bokassa’s personal responsibility and the claim that he ate human flesh.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Central_African_Repu_358788-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Central African Republic illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Central_African_Repu_358788-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Central_African_Repu_358788-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

### The schoolchildren’s deaths

In early 1979, students protested against rules requiring them to wear prescribed school uniforms, which many families could not afford and which were linked in public accounts to Bokassa’s commercial interests. Security forces arrested large numbers of children. Many detainees were subsequently killed at Ngaragba Prison in Bangui.

Initial estimates varied, as did descriptions of the method of killing. Contemporary Amnesty reporting alleged that guards had clubbed, bayoneted or stoned scores of children to death. Press reports placed the number at roughly 60 to 100, although the exact total has never been securely established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v17p2/d119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[state.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

A five-country African fact-finding mission later concluded that Bokassa had ordered the killings and had probably personally participated. This was more than press rumour: the mission included jurists from Senegal, Côte d’Ivoire, Liberia, Rwanda and Togo, and its findings influenced the international response to Bokassa’s government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v17p2/d122" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

Bokassa was overthrown in September 1979 during a French-backed operation. When he returned from exile and stood trial in Bangui in 1986–87, survivors gave evidence about the prison killings. He was convicted of multiple murders and sentenced to death, later commuted to imprisonment. The exact number of children killed and the full extent of Bokassa’s direct physical involvement remain disputed, but the massacre itself is not a mystery invented by hostile foreign media. It is a well-supported historical crime.

### The cannibalism allegation

The claim that Bokassa consumed human flesh is much less secure. It became one of the best-known stories about him, repeated through testimony, press reports and political propaganda. During his trial, witnesses described bodies or human remains allegedly stored at his properties, while a former cook was reported to have made accusations about preparing human flesh.

Yet Bokassa denied cannibalism, and the proceedings did not produce a conviction on that allegation. Contemporary trial reporting also recorded contradictory testimony, including evidence from a former minister who said that he had found no bodies during a search.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.upi.com/Archives/1987/03/02/Bokassa-testifies-I-am-not-a-cannibal/2158541659600/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upi.com">[upi.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bokassa testifies: &#x27;I am not a cannibal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bokassa testifies: &#x27;I am not a cannibal</span></span></span>

The distinction matters. There is substantial evidence that Bokassa’s regime imprisoned, tortured and killed opponents and that he bore responsibility for the schoolchildren’s massacre. Cannibalism, by contrast, remains an allegation supported by contested testimony rather than a firmly established judicial fact.

The lurid story has often proved more memorable internationally than the documented victims. That can distort history by turning a brutal political system into a tale about an exotic “mad emperor”. Bokassa’s real abuses require no embellishment.

## What do the Bouar stones reveal?

West of Bangui, around Bouar and extending towards neighbouring Cameroon, stand groups of ancient upright stones. Archaeologists commonly call the monuments tazunu. Hundreds have been recorded, often arranged as stone settings incorporated into low burial mounds.

Their existence is not mysterious in the sense of being inexplicable. Excavation has demonstrated that people deliberately constructed them and used at least some of them in funerary or commemorative settings. The unresolved questions concern who built them, how their use changed over time and what social meaning the monuments carried.

Archaeologist Nicholas David’s major study re-examined earlier excavations and investigated two mounds containing megalithic structures. The work showed that the monuments formed part of a wider archaeological tradition across western Central African Republic and the Cameroonian highlands. It also challenged earlier assumptions about their chronology, illustrating how limited samples and uncertain layers can produce very different dates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00672708209511299" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span>

UNESCO surveys identify the Bouar megaliths as an important element of Central Africa’s archaeological heritage. Broader archaeological writing has also linked the region with early farming and metalworking societies, although particular sites and phases should not be merged into one supposedly continuous civilisation without evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark%3A/48223/pf0000133843" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesdoc.unesco.org">[UNESCO Document Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesdoc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

### What archaeologists can and cannot say

The strongest interpretation is that the stones were connected with burials, remembrance and claims to place. Building a mound and erecting heavy stones required organised labour. The monuments therefore suggest communities capable of coordinating work and maintaining shared customs over time.

What cannot yet be recovered with confidence is the builders’ identity in modern ethnic terms. Present-day communities may preserve names, stories or cultural associations with the stones, but a direct line between an ancient archaeological population and a modern group requires evidence rather than assumption.

Claims that the monuments were observatories, remnants of a vanished advanced civilisation or products of outsiders are also poorly supported. Some alignments may have been deliberate, but a repeated astronomical system has not been demonstrated strongly enough to replace the more grounded funerary interpretation.

The genuine mystery is subtler and more valuable: the stones preserve evidence of societies whose spoken explanations have not survived in written archives. Archaeology can reconstruct construction, burial practice and approximate chronology, but it cannot yet restore the names, beliefs and political relationships of the people who raised them.

## Did Bokassa’s diamonds expose a larger secret?

The diamonds affair linked the Central African Republic’s resources, Bokassa’s personal diplomacy and French domestic politics. In October 1979, the French newspaper *Le Canard enchaîné* published documents alleging that Bokassa had given valuable diamonds to Valéry Giscard d’Estaing before and during his French presidency.

The basic fact that gifts were made became difficult to deny. The unresolved argument concerned their value, frequency and political significance. Giscard maintained that the stones were worth far less than alleged and later said that proceeds from their sale had gone to charity. Bokassa, by then deposed and seeking revenge against his former French ally, claimed that the gifts were extensive. Contemporary reporting recorded that Giscard did not deny receiving diamonds, but challenged the valuations attached to them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1979/10/11/diamond-gift-to-giscard-from-bokassa-alleged/16f8dbca-3cf1-48df-899c-cf8e877a17bf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[washingtonpost.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Washington Post Diamond Gift to Giscard From Bokassa Alleged</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington Post Diamond Gift to Giscard From Bokassa Alleged</span></span></span>

The affair also generated suspicions about missing or removed Central African state archives after Bokassa’s overthrow. French officials denied certain allegations of military involvement in transferring records, while witnesses reportedly gave conflicting accounts. Without a complete, publicly accessible documentary trail, it remains difficult to determine whether the surviving papers represented all the gifts or only a selection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/archives/article/1979/10/11/l-elysee-ne-fait-pas-de-commentaires-sur-les-assertions-du-canard-enchaine-a-propos-de-diamants-offerts-par-bokassa_2792360_1819218.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lemonde.fr.</span></span></span>

This was not simply gossip about jewellery. France had maintained deep political, military and commercial influence in its former colony. Personal gifts between leaders therefore raised wider questions: were they private tokens, undeclared benefits or part of an informal system through which access and protection were exchanged?

The scandal damaged Giscard politically and helped turn Bokassa from a former French partner into evidence of the compromises behind France’s African relationships. Yet the evidential picture remains untidy. Gifts are documented; the highest valuations and some of Bokassa’s later claims are contested. The broader pattern of intimate, poorly scrutinised relations is clearer than the precise history of every stone.

## Why so many questions remain open

The Central African Republic’s unresolved history is shaped by the condition of its archives as much as by the events themselves. Colonial records were produced largely by French institutions. Later governments repeatedly fell through coups or rebellion. Ministries, courts and museums suffered neglect, looting and destruction, while witnesses were displaced or killed.

Conflict has also scattered evidence across several countries and institutions. Relevant material may be held in Bangui, French archives, church collections, United Nations files, diplomatic records or private papers. Boganda’s previously little-known diaries, for example, survived in the archives of the Spiritan religious order rather than in a Central African state collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/diaries-of-barthelemy-boganda-priest-and-politician-in-french-equatorial-africa-19101959/92E93CCD6C0C7059B77C674785411649" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Diaries of Barthélémy Boganda, Priest and Politician in...by K van Walraven · 2017 · Cited by...</span></span></span>

This fragmentation encourages two opposite errors. One is to accept official silence as proof that nothing suspicious happened. The other is to fill every gap with conspiracy. Neither approach is sound.

The best-supported conclusions are more measured:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Boganda’s crash was inadequately explained, and sabotage remains a serious but unproved possibility.
* Charles Massi probably died after entering state custody, although the precise circumstances and perpetrators have not been judicially established.
* Bokassa’s responsibility for grave abuses, including the 1979 killing of schoolchildren, is strongly documented; the cannibalism story is far less certain.
* The Bouar stones are authentic ancient monuments, probably associated with burial and commemoration, but their builders’ identities and beliefs remain largely unknown.
* Bokassa gave diamonds to Giscard, but their value, extent and relationship to French policy remain disputed.

</div>

These are not mysteries because no evidence exists. They endure because the surviving evidence is incomplete, politically compromised or capable of supporting more than one interpretation. Their importance lies precisely in that tension between what can be responsibly concluded and what the historical record still refuses to disclose.
