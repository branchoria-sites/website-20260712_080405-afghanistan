## Introduction

On 26 June 1944, Allied aircraft bombed the neutral Republic of [San Marino]({{ 'which-san-marino-mysteries-still-defy/' | relative_url }}), killing dozens of civilians and causing extensive damage to one of Europe's smallest states. The raid has remained one of the most disputed wartime episodes involving a neutral country because it sits at the intersection of flawed intelligence, military necessity and the legal protections normally associated with neutrality. The central mystery is not whether the bombing occurred, but whether the intelligence that justified it was accurate and whether the attack could have been avoided. Later fighting showed that German forces did eventually occupy San Marino, yet the evidence suggests that this occupation came weeks after the bombing, leaving historians to debate whether Allied commanders acted on mistaken reports or on intelligence that could not be reliably verified at the time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/d305" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianJuly 4, 1944...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1944</span></span></span>

## The raid and its civilian toll

San Marino had declared itself neutral throughout the Second World War. Although surrounded by Fascist Italy and later by German-controlled territory, its government repeatedly insisted that no belligerent forces were being allowed to use the republic for military purposes.

Despite those assurances, on 26 June 1944 aircraft of the British Desert Air Force attacked the capital and surrounding areas. Bombs struck the railway, buildings and civilian districts. Contemporary Sammarinese protests reported at least 35 deaths immediately after the raid, while later official commemorations and historical accounts generally place the final death toll at around 60–65 civilians as more victims succumbed to injuries or were later identified. The bombing also damaged infrastructure that had little direct military value, including parts of the remarkable electric railway linking San Marino with Rimini, much of which was never rebuilt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/d305" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianJuly 4, 1944...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1944</span></span></span>

For the people of San Marino, the attack became a defining national trauma. Thousands of refugees from nearby Italian fighting had also sought shelter inside the republic, believing neutrality would offer protection. Instead, the bombing demonstrated how fragile neutrality could become when military intelligence suggested otherwise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/d305" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianJuly 4, 1944...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1944</span></span></span>

## Why did Britain order the attack?

The British justification rested on intelligence reports indicating that German troops and military stores were using San Marino. Allied commanders feared that the republic's position on Mount Titano offered observation points and communications routes that could support German operations as the Italian campaign moved north.

The difficulty is that surviving evidence suggests these reports were highly contested.

Before the raid, San Marino emphatically denied that German troops, ammunition depots or military installations were present. Following the bombing, its government issued formal protests stating that the republic had maintained strict neutrality and that no military objectives existed within its territory. These protests were circulated diplomatically and reached Allied governments through neutral channels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/d305" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianJuly 4, 1944...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1944</span></span></span>

Historians have identified several possible reasons why Allied intelligence reached a different conclusion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Refugees moving across the frontier may have been mistaken for troop concentrations.
* Reports from resistance networks or aerial reconnaissance may have been inaccurate or outdated.
* Allied planners may have assumed that German forces were using San Marino because its territory lay directly beside the advancing front.
* Intelligence from multiple sources may have reinforced one another despite sharing the same original error.

</div>

No single surviving document has emerged proving that substantial German military forces were actually inside San Marino on 26 June 1944. That absence of definitive evidence is the core reason the bombing remains disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/ch3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

### What happened afterwards?

The later military history complicates the picture.

In late August and early September 1944, during the Allied advance towards the Gothic Line, German forces did occupy San Marino and fought defensive actions there before being driven out during the Battle of San Marino. This genuine occupation has sometimes been used retrospectively to justify the earlier bombing.

However, the chronology matters. The confirmed German occupation occurred roughly two months after the June air raid. That does not automatically prove that German military use already existed in June, nor does it vindicate the original intelligence on which the bombing decision was based. Instead, it shows that San Marino's neutrality ultimately collapsed under the pressure of the wider campaign.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/ch3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

## Neutrality, responsibility and later British doubts

The diplomatic correspondence that followed illustrates how uncertain even Allied governments were about San Marino's legal position.

American diplomatic documents show officials discussing whether San Marino should still be regarded as neutral and whether Britain had ever formally recognised that neutrality. British officials argued that treatment of the republic depended upon whether it remained genuinely independent or whether German military use had effectively nullified its neutral status. American officials, by contrast, noted that the United States did not consider itself at war with San Marino and generally recognised it as an independent state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/d310" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianJuly 10, 1944...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 1944</span></span></span>

These exchanges reveal that the legal and military assessments were not fully aligned. Military commanders focused on operational intelligence, while diplomats questioned whether the legal conditions for attacking a neutral state had actually been met.

After the war, San Marino sought compensation for the destruction. The British government consistently rejected legal liability, maintaining that the bombing had been justified by the information available at the time, which suggested German military use of the republic. Nevertheless, Britain acknowledged the suffering caused and repeatedly offered an ex gratia payment—a payment made without admitting legal responsibility—as a humanitarian gesture rather than compensation for wrongdoing. Parliamentary debates in the 1950s and 1960s show this position remained unchanged for many years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hansard.parliament.uk/%E2%80%8CCommons/1952-07-02/debates/dd4c440d-dc5f-42f2-8ea6-a480399f69cb/SanMarino%28WarDamageClaim%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hansard.parliament.uk">[Hansard]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hansard.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hansard San Marino (War Damage Claim</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">San Marino (War Damage Claim) - Hansard - UK Parliament...</span></span></span>

## Why the debate remains unresolved

The bombing continues to occupy a distinctive place in San Marino's history because the available evidence supports several conclusions simultaneously.

Certain points are firmly established:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* San Marino was officially neutral.
* Allied aircraft deliberately bombed the republic on 26 June 1944.
* Civilians were killed and significant damage resulted.
* German forces later occupied San Marino during the fighting in September 1944.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/d305" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianJuly 4, 1944...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1944</span></span></span>

</div>

Other questions remain unresolved:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Were German troops or military stores actually present in significant numbers when the bombing occurred?
* How reliable were the intelligence reports received by Allied commanders?
* Could additional reconnaissance or diplomatic verification have prevented the raid?
* Did wartime urgency lead commanders to accept intelligence that would not have met peacetime standards of proof?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/ch3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

</div>

Because wartime intelligence records are incomplete and many operational decisions were made under intense pressure, historians cannot provide a definitive answer. Most modern scholarship avoids portraying the raid either as a deliberate attack on an innocent neutral state or as an unquestionably necessary military strike. Instead, it is generally understood as a case where uncertain intelligence, legal ambiguity and the rapid movement of the Italian campaign combined to produce a tragic outcome.

For San Marino, the episode remains more than a military incident. It is a reminder that even centuries of recognised neutrality could offer only limited protection once a major war reached the republic's borders, and that intelligence failures—whether genuine mistakes or flawed assessments—can have lasting consequences for civilians caught between opposing armies.
