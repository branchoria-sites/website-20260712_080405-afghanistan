## Introduction

The story of Thiess, an elderly peasant tried in seventeenth-century Livonia, is one of the most unusual episodes in Baltic history. Rather than denying accusations that he was a werewolf, Thiess insisted they were true—but with an astonishing twist. He claimed that he and other werewolves were not servants of the Devil. Instead, they were "the hounds of God", descending into Hell to battle witches and recover grain, livestock and prosperity for ordinary people. His testimony survives in court records from Swedish-ruled Livonia, covering parts of modern [Latvia]({{ 'latvia/' | relative_url }}) and Estonia, making it one of the rarest first-hand accounts of popular belief from the period. The enduring mystery is not whether Thiess literally became a wolf, but what his extraordinary testimony reveals about rural religion, older folk traditions and the uneasy relationship between local belief and official Lutheran Christianity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why Did Thiess Call Himself God's Werewolf?

Thiess appeared before the provincial court at Jürgensburg (today's Jaunjelgava area within historical Livonia) in 1692 after being questioned during proceedings connected with a church robbery. By then he was around eighty years old and already known locally for earlier claims that he had fought witches. Rather than retracting those stories, he expanded them into a remarkable account of supernatural service.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

According to Thiess, werewolves transformed on three nights each year and travelled to Hell carrying iron rods. There they fought the Devil and witches, who had stolen the community's crops, livestock and agricultural fertility. If the werewolves won, the coming harvest would be plentiful; if they lost, famine and hardship could follow. This inverted almost every assumption held by educated church authorities, who regarded werewolves as agents of Satan. Thiess instead presented them as defenders of Christian society acting under God's authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

One striking detail involved an old injury. Thiess explained that a neighbour had broken his nose during one of these supernatural battles because the neighbour was secretly a witch. Years earlier he had made the same allegation in court. While the judges dismissed the supernatural explanation, they confirmed that his nose genuinely had been broken, adding another layer of curiosity to the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

## What Thiess Told the Livonian Court

The surviving trial transcript allows historians to reconstruct Thiess's beliefs with unusual precision. He repeatedly distinguished himself from witches rather than identifying with them.

Several themes stand out:

* **Werewolves protected society.** Their task was to recover what witches had stolen from humanity, especially grain, livestock and agricultural abundance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>
* **Their battles served the common good.** Thiess claimed that successful struggles in Hell ensured healthy harvests and prosperity for ordinary villagers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>
* **He denied serving the Devil.** When questioned, he insisted that werewolves belonged to God rather than Satan, directly contradicting accepted Lutheran theology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>
* **He mixed these beliefs with practical healing.** Witnesses described him as someone who blessed grain, treated animals and recited protective charms used by local people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiess_of_Kaltenbrun" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thiess of Kaltenbrun</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thiess of Kaltenbrun</span></span></span>

Ironically, the judges were less troubled by the fantastic claim of travelling to Hell than by Thiess's religious practices. He admitted that he rarely attended church and instead relied on folk rituals and healing charms. The court ultimately punished him for promoting practices viewed as superstitious and outside Lutheran doctrine, sentencing him to flogging and lifelong banishment rather than execution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

## Folk Belief, Personal Myth or Courtroom Strategy?

The greatest historical mystery surrounding Thiess is not whether he believed in werewolves, but how historians should interpret his testimony.

One possibility is that Thiess genuinely reflected an older layer of Baltic rural religion. Livonia retained many pre-Christian customs well into the seventeenth century, despite centuries of official Christian rule. Scholars such as Carlo Ginzburg have compared Thiess's account with other European traditions in which selected individuals claimed to fight supernatural enemies to protect crops and fertility. Rather than isolated fantasy, the testimony may preserve fragments of an older religious worldview that survived beneath outward Christianity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

Another interpretation sees Thiess as adapting familiar folklore into a courtroom defence. By presenting himself as God's servant instead of the Devil's accomplice, he may have hoped to distance himself from the far more dangerous accusation of witchcraft. Although this strategy did not spare him punishment, it may explain why his testimony consistently emphasised helping the community rather than harming it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiess_of_Kaltenbrun" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thiess of Kaltenbrun</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thiess of Kaltenbrun</span></span></span>

A third explanation focuses on social identity. Some modern historians argue that Thiess's story reflected the outlook of Livonian peasants living under Baltic German elites and Swedish administration. In this reading, the "good werewolf" became a symbolic defender of ordinary rural society against forces blamed for misfortune and scarcity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ccsenet.org/journal/index.php/res/article/view/0/48122" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ccsenet.org">[CCSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ccsenet.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ccsenet.org.</span></span></span>

None of these interpretations commands universal agreement. The surviving record is unusually rich but still incomplete, leaving historians to balance folklore, religious history and legal context without access to Thiess's own private thoughts.

## What the Trial Reveals About Rural Religion

The Thiess trial offers a rare window into beliefs that normally went unrecorded because they belonged to illiterate villagers rather than clergy or officials.

Several important features emerge from the evidence.

First, the boundaries between Christianity and older folk traditions were far less clear than official church teaching suggested. Thiess spoke naturally about God, Hell, witches and supernatural battles while also practising charms that the court regarded as magical rather than Christian. His worldview blended biblical concepts with local traditions instead of separating them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

Second, the case illustrates the growing confidence of Swedish Livonia's Lutheran authorities. The judges were not conducting a spectacular witch trial. Instead, they examined Thiess's beliefs carefully, questioned witnesses about his mental state and focused on religious conformity. Their concern lay less with physical transformation into a wolf than with unauthorised spiritual authority exercised through folk healing and charms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

Finally, the testimony demonstrates that rural communities could interpret familiar supernatural figures very differently from educated elites. For Thiess and perhaps some neighbours, a werewolf was not necessarily a monster but a guardian whose dangerous journeys protected the harvest. That reversal remains one of the most striking examples of how local belief could diverge from official religious doctrine in early modern Europe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why Thiess Still Fascinates Historians

Thiess's story has endured because it sits at the intersection of folklore, religion and legal history. Unlike many alleged werewolves, he left behind detailed court testimony in which he explained his own beliefs instead of having them reconstructed solely through hostile accusations.

For historians of Latvia and the wider Baltic region, the case illuminates a society where official Lutheran institutions coexisted with resilient village traditions. It also cautions against assuming that terms such as "werewolf", "witch" or "magic" meant the same thing to everyone. The court, the clergy and Thiess himself all attached different meanings to those words.

The mystery therefore is cultural rather than supernatural. The surviving documents cannot tell us exactly where Thiess's beliefs originated or how widely they were shared. What they do reveal is an exceptionally rare glimpse into a hidden world of rural imagination and belief that official records usually sought to suppress rather than preserve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/sites/thiess/old_thiess_transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Chicago Press Old Thiess,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chicago PressOld Thiess,April 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2026</span></span></span>
