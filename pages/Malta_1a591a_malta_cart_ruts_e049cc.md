## Introduction

[Malta]({{ 'what-really-lies-behind-maltas-greatest/' | relative_url }})'s cart ruts are among the country's most enduring archaeological puzzles. Hundreds of pairs of parallel grooves cut into exposed limestone criss-cross both Malta and Gozo, sometimes running for hundreds of metres, crossing each other in dense networks, or apparently ending at cliffs. The broad consensus is that they relate to transport, but archaeologists still disagree about exactly when they were created, what kind of vehicles used them, and what economic system they served. Those unanswered questions make them one of Malta's most important historical mysteries.

The cart ruts are not mysterious because nobody has proposed explanations. Rather, they are mysterious because several plausible explanations fit parts of the evidence while none explains every site. Modern research increasingly suggests that the grooves were practical transport infrastructure rather than ceremonial or mythical features, yet even recent large-scale surveys have shown that their chronology may be more complex than previously believed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X21004995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The morphological variability of Maltese ‘cart ruts’ and its implications - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## What the grooves reveal

The cart ruts consist of two parallel channels worn or cut into Malta's soft limestone bedrock. They occur at dozens of locations across the islands, with the largest concentration at Misraħ Għar il-Kbir, often nicknamed "Clapham Junction" because of its dense web of intersecting tracks. Similar examples occur at Binġemma, Xemxija, San Pawl tat-Tarġa and many other sites.

Several characteristics are remarkably consistent:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Most occur as paired grooves separated by a fairly standard distance.
* Many follow practical routes over ridges and through valleys rather than symbolic alignments.
* Networks frequently branch, merge and overlap, suggesting repeated traffic over long periods.
* Some grooves reach considerable depths, implying prolonged use or deliberate cutting followed by continued wear.

</div>

Detailed measurement of hundreds of ruts has shown that, despite local variation, many share similar widths and depths. Researchers argue that this degree of standardisation is difficult to explain if every rut had an entirely different origin. At the same time, differences in shape indicate that erosion, repeated use and geological weathering have modified many examples after they were first created.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X21004995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The morphological variability of Maltese ‘cart ruts’ and its implications - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

One particularly important observation is that some tracks climb over ridges rather than following the easiest terrain. This weakens suggestions that they were channels for water management or natural geological formations, while strengthening interpretations involving controlled movement of vehicles or heavy loads.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X21004995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The morphological variability of Maltese ‘cart ruts’ and its implications - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## Competing transport explanations

The greatest debate is not whether the ruts were used for transport, but exactly how that transport worked.

### Wheeled carts

The traditional explanation is that two-wheeled carts repeatedly travelled along the same routes until iron- or wood-rimmed wheels gradually cut into relatively soft limestone. During wet winters the rock becomes easier to wear, allowing grooves to deepen over generations.

Supporters point out that many rut pairs are compatible with the width expected for practical carts, while the branching networks resemble transport corridors rather than isolated tracks. Geomorphological modelling has shown that repeated use by moderately loaded two-wheeled vehicles could realistically create grooves of the observed dimensions. Once a route became too deeply worn for a cart's axle clearance, travellers may simply have started another adjacent path, explaining why multiple parallel tracks often appear together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/cart-ruts-of-malta-an-applied-geomorphology-approach/6FF91DFDAA71C46F0B45994DA6754F64" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe cart ruts of Malta: an applied geomorphology approach &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Core...</span></span></span>

### Guided transport routes

Another possibility is that at least some grooves were intentionally cut to guide vehicles rather than being entirely produced by wear.

On steep slopes or uneven limestone surfaces, shallow channels would help keep heavily loaded carts or sledges aligned. Under this interpretation, deliberate cutting and later erosion may both have contributed to the finished appearance, making the surviving grooves a combination of engineering and long-term use rather than one or the other.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X21004995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The morphological variability of Maltese ‘cart ruts’ and its implications - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

### Quarry and agricultural transport

Many cart ruts occur close to ancient quarries, agricultural terraces and rural settlements. This has encouraged the idea that they formed part of an economic landscape linking stone extraction, farmland, storage areas and coastal landing places.

Heavy limestone blocks are an obvious candidate, but soil, timber, harvested crops and water containers have also been proposed. Rather than serving a single industry, different parts of the network may have supported several kinds of transport across different periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6853483" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: papers.ssrn.com">[SSRN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">papers.ssrn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Chronology of the Maltese Cart Ruts: A Critical Evaluation of the Available Dating Evidence by Joel Grima:: SSRN...</span></span></span>

### Sledges instead of wheeled vehicles

Some researchers have questioned whether wheeled carts alone could explain every feature. Wooden sledges dragged across limestone might also produce parallel grooves, especially when carrying exceptionally heavy loads.

However, while sledges could account for some situations, they do not clearly explain the consistency of many paired tracks or the extensive branching systems observed across the islands. Most recent studies therefore continue to favour wheeled or guided vehicle transport as the principal explanation, even if sledges were occasionally used.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X21004995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The morphological variability of Maltese ‘cart ruts’ and its implications - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## Why the dating remains uncertain

Dating the cart ruts has proved far more difficult than explaining their probable function.

Unlike buildings or burials, the grooves rarely contain organic material suitable for radiocarbon dating. Archaeologists therefore rely on indirect evidence, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* whether a rut cuts through or is cut by another archaeological feature;
* nearby pottery or artefacts;
* relationships with quarries, tombs or roads;
* patterns across the wider landscape.

</div>

These methods establish relative rather than absolute dates, allowing competing interpretations.

For much of the twentieth century the cart ruts were widely regarded as prehistoric, often linked with Malta's famous temple-building culture. That idea became popular partly because the grooves appear ancient and because some occur near prehistoric monuments.

More recent research has challenged that assumption. A comprehensive island-wide reassessment published in 2026 concluded that several cart rut systems can be securely associated with the Roman period, while many others fit more broadly within the Classical era. The study found no conclusive evidence proving that any existing cart rut pair is definitely prehistoric, although it also stressed that a prehistoric origin cannot yet be ruled out for every example.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6853483" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: papers.ssrn.com">[SSRN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">papers.ssrn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Chronology of the Maltese Cart Ruts: A Critical Evaluation of the Available Dating Evidence by Joel Grima:: SSRN...</span></span></span>

This represents an important shift in the debate. Instead of asking whether all cart ruts belong to one civilisation, researchers increasingly consider whether similar transport systems were created and reused during multiple periods.

## Why some tracks seem to lead nowhere

Among the most striking features are cart ruts that appear to end abruptly at cliff edges or continue towards the sea.

These dramatic examples have inspired claims involving Atlantis, submerged civilisations or lost prehistoric ports. Yet there are more ordinary explanations that fit the geological evidence better.

Over thousands of years:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* coastlines have retreated through erosion;
* sea level has changed;
* landslides have altered cliff faces;
* quarrying and modern development have destroyed sections of ancient routes.

</div>

A transport line that today appears to end in empty space may originally have continued to a shoreline, harbour, quarry or settlement that no longer survives. Likewise, tracks disappearing beneath shallow coastal waters do not necessarily imply a vanished civilisation; they may reflect later coastal change or local geological processes. None of these observations requires extraordinary explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X21004995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The morphological variability of Maltese ‘cart ruts’ and its implications - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## What modern research has changed

Recent studies have transformed understanding of the cart ruts by moving beyond isolated observations.

Large-scale mapping has recorded thousands of individual tracks across more than two hundred surveyed locations, allowing archaeologists to compare entire networks instead of individual sites. Digital landscape modelling has revealed relationships between cart ruts, ancient quarries, burial sites and rural infrastructure that were previously difficult to detect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6853483" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: papers.ssrn.com">[SSRN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">papers.ssrn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Chronology of the Maltese Cart Ruts: A Critical Evaluation of the Available Dating Evidence by Joel Grima:: SSRN...</span></span></span>

Morphological analysis has also shown that many commonly repeated claims deserve caution. For example, the frequently quoted comparison between cart rut spacing and modern railway gauge turns out to depend on inconsistent methods of measurement. While many ruts are broadly similar in width, the evidence does not support the idea of a precisely standardised "ancient railway gauge."<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X21004995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The morphological variability of Maltese ‘cart ruts’ and its implications - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Rather than solving the mystery outright, these studies have narrowed the range of plausible explanations. They strongly support an interpretation centred on transport while showing that the surviving networks may represent more than one historical phase.

## Why the mystery endures

Malta's cart ruts remain fascinating because the physical evidence is abundant while the historical context has largely disappeared. The grooves survive, but the carts, animals, goods, roads and written records that would explain them have vanished.

The strongest current interpretation is that they formed part of practical transport systems serving everyday economic life, particularly the movement of heavy loads across limestone landscapes. The greatest remaining uncertainty concerns chronology rather than purpose: whether all the networks belong to one era or represent repeated adaptations over centuries.

That distinction matters because it changes how archaeologists understand Malta's past. If the cart ruts span several historical periods, they are not the signature of one lost civilisation but evidence that successive communities repeatedly solved the same transport problem in similar ways. Their mystery lies not in impossible engineering, but in how ordinary infrastructure can outlast the society that created it, leaving only the tracks behind.
