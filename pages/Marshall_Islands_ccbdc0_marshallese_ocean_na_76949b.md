## Introduction

How did Marshallese navigators find tiny coral atolls scattered across one of the world's largest ocean regions without compasses or modern instruments? The answer is both well documented and still partly mysterious. Experienced navigators read the movement of ocean swells, currents, winds, stars, clouds and wildlife, while also recognising how distant islands subtly altered wave patterns. The existence of this navigation system is not in doubt: ethnographers, surviving Marshallese navigators and modern scientific expeditions have all confirmed its effectiveness. The mystery lies elsewhere. Researchers still disagree about exactly how some of the most famous wave signals worked, particularly a phenomenon known as the *dilep*, a supposedly detectable swell pattern linking two islands across open sea. Modern oceanography explains part of the system, but not every detail, leaving one of the [Marshall Islands]({{ 'which-marshall-islands-mysteries-still/' | relative_url }})' most intriguing historical puzzles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tos.org/oceanography/article/wave-navigation-in-the-marshall-islands-comparing-indigenous-and-western-sc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tos.org">[tos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 2, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2015</span></span></span>

## How navigators used swells and island wave patterns

Before European navigation methods reached the Marshall Islands, expert navigators routinely travelled between low coral atolls that disappear below the horizon after only a few kilometres. Success depended on perceiving the ocean itself as a moving landscape rather than an empty space.

Marshallese navigators did not rely on a single clue. Instead, they combined multiple observations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The direction and rhythm of long ocean swells.
* Changes in wave motion caused by distant islands.
* Prevailing winds and seasonal weather.
* The rising and setting positions of stars.
* Bird movements and cloud formations that hinted at nearby land.
* The motion of the canoe itself, felt through the body as much as seen with the eyes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tos.org/oceanography/article/wave-navigation-in-the-marshall-islands-comparing-indigenous-and-western-sc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tos.org">[tos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 2, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2015</span></span></span>

</div>

One of the most remarkable skills involved recognising the "shadow" an atoll cast across surrounding seas. Although the islands themselves are extremely low, they interrupt incoming swell. Waves bend, reflect and interfere with one another, creating distinctive patterns extending many kilometres beyond visible land. Skilled navigators learned these patterns through years of training rather than mathematical calculation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tos.org/oceanography/article/wave-navigation-in-the-marshall-islands-comparing-indigenous-and-western-sc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tos.org">[tos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 2, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2015</span></span></span>

Modern measurements have confirmed that islands do create detectable wave transformations. Satellite observations, wave buoys and numerical models show that refracted wave trains can extend tens of kilometres into the lee of an atoll, providing a genuine physical basis for at least part of traditional wave navigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tos.org/oceanography/article/wave-navigation-in-the-marshall-islands-comparing-indigenous-and-western-sc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tos.org">[tos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 2, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2015</span></span></span>

## What stick charts recorded—and what they did not

The famous Marshallese stick charts are often misunderstood as primitive maps. They were something quite different.

Constructed from coconut midribs tied together with fibre and marked with shells representing islands, the charts served primarily as teaching devices. They helped apprentices memorise relationships between islands, prevailing swells and current systems before voyages began. Experienced navigators generally did not carry them aboard canoes. Instead, they relied on knowledge committed to memory and interpreted through direct experience at sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ocean.si.edu/human-connections/history-cultures/navigating-waters-micronesian-stick-charts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ocean.si.edu">[si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ocean.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

Several forms of chart existed, each serving different purposes. Some represented only local routes, while others illustrated broader concepts about swell behaviour across larger parts of the archipelago. Their meaning depended heavily on oral instruction from experienced navigators.

This distinction is important because the charts were never intended to record every wave or every voyage. They represented conceptual models of how navigators should think about the ocean. Modern museums sometimes display them as maps, but their true function was closer to a mnemonic framework for remembering complex environmental relationships.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/etho.12056" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Complementarity of Cognitive and Experiential Ways of Knowing the Ocean in Marshallese Navigation - Genz - 2014 - Ethos - Wil...</span></span></span>

## Why the *dilep* remains controversial

The greatest mystery concerns the *dilep*, sometimes translated as a "backbone" or pathway of swell supposedly connecting two islands.

According to some Marshallese navigators, this pattern could guide a canoe travelling between distant atolls by providing a subtle line that remained perceptible over long distances. The tradition is well documented in interviews with experienced navigators, yet exactly what physical phenomenon created it remains disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tos.org/oceanography/article/wave-navigation-in-the-marshall-islands-comparing-indigenous-and-western-sc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tos.org">[tos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 2, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2015</span></span></span>

Several explanations have been proposed.

One idea suggests that swells reflected from two islands interfere to produce a relatively stable pattern that a trained navigator can detect through the canoe's motion. Another proposes that intersecting swells generate elongated bands of consistent movement. Others argue that the *dilep* is not a single physical wave at all but an experienced navigator's interpretation of multiple changing signals that together indicate a preferred course.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09151" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that modern instruments have struggled to detect a wave pattern matching every traditional description. Researchers have successfully measured several wave effects around islands, particularly refracted and crossing swell systems, but these do not fully account for all reported characteristics of the *dilep*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tos.org/oceanography/article/wave-navigation-in-the-marshall-islands-comparing-indigenous-and-western-sc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tos.org">[tos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 2, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2015</span></span></span>

Equally significant is the fact that surviving Marshallese experts have not always agreed among themselves. Different navigators sometimes described the phenomenon differently, suggesting either regional variation, differences in teaching traditions or the gradual loss of specialised knowledge during the twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://escholarship.org/uc/item/43h1d0d7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: escholarship.org">[eScholarship]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">escholarship.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on escholarship.org.</span></span></span>

## Why modern science has not settled the question

At first glance, the puzzle seems surprising. Oceanographers possess satellites, sophisticated wave models and powerful computers. Why should a navigation technique remain only partly explained?

Several factors make the problem unusually difficult.

First, ocean swells constantly interact with changing winds, currents and weather. No two days produce exactly the same sea surface.

Second, traditional navigation depends on human perception rather than instruments alone. A navigator experiences the ocean through the canoe's rolling, pitching and vibration, combining sensations over many hours with prior knowledge. Modern sensors record physical variables but may not capture the same integrated experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/etho.12056" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[AnthroSource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Complementarity of Cognitive and Experiential Ways of Knowing the Ocean in Marshallese Navigation - Genz - 2014 - Ethos - Wil...</span></span></span>

Third, much traditional knowledge nearly disappeared during colonial rule, missionary influence and the decline of long-distance canoe voyaging. By the time researchers began recording detailed explanations in the late twentieth century, only a handful of master navigators remained, making it difficult to compare multiple independent traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.physics.upenn.edu/events/department-colloquium-wave-piloting-marshall-islands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: physics.upenn.edu">[physics.upenn.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">physics.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on upenn.edu.</span></span></span>

The result is an unusual situation in which Indigenous knowledge clearly worked in practice, while the complete physical explanation remains incomplete.

## The mystery without the myth

The enduring mystery should not be mistaken for evidence of supernatural powers or lost technology.

There is broad agreement on several important points:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Marshallese navigators successfully completed long-distance voyages using environmental observation.
* Stick charts were educational models rather than portable navigational maps.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://worldhistorycommons.org/marshall-islands-stick-charts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldhistorycommons.org">[worldhistorycommons.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldhistorycommons.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* Islands genuinely modify surrounding wave fields in measurable ways.
* Modern scientific studies have confirmed several of the mechanisms described by navigators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tos.org/oceanography/article/wave-navigation-in-the-marshall-islands-comparing-indigenous-and-western-sc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tos.org">[tos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 2, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2015</span></span></span>

</div>

What remains uncertain is whether one celebrated feature—the *dilep*—corresponds to a single physical wave phenomenon, several interacting wave processes or an expert navigator's interpretation of numerous subtle environmental cues.

Rather than diminishing Marshallese navigation, this uncertainty highlights its sophistication. The unresolved question is not whether the navigators possessed remarkable practical knowledge—they demonstrably did—but whether modern science has yet developed the right models and measurements to describe everything they perceived on the open ocean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09151" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arxiv.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>
