## Introduction

The disappearance of the Princes in the Tower remains one of the [United Kingdom]({{ 'united-kingdom/' | relative_url }})'s most enduring historical mysteries because the central question has never been answered with direct evidence: what happened to the young King Edward V and his brother Richard, Duke of York, after they vanished from public view in 1483? Most historians conclude that the boys probably died while confined in the Tower of London, but there is no contemporary record of their deaths, no verified burial, and no surviving order identifying who was responsible. Richard III has long been regarded as the most likely culprit because he benefited most from their disappearance, yet the case remains unproven. Centuries of political propaganda, lost records, disputed testimony and incomplete forensic evidence have ensured that the mystery continues to shape debates about Richard III's reputation and the turbulent end of the Wars of the Roses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrp.org.uk/tower-of-london/history-and-stories/the-princes-in-the-tower/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrp.org.uk">[Historic Royal Palaces]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrp.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## What Happened in 1483

Edward V became king in April 1483 after the death of his father, Edward IV. At only 12 years old, he was travelling to London for his coronation when his uncle, Richard, Duke of Gloucester, assumed control of the government as Lord Protector.

Within weeks, Richard declared that Edward IV's marriage had been invalid, making his children illegitimate and removing their claim to the throne. Parliament later confirmed this position through the statute known as *Titulus Regius*. Richard was crowned King Richard III in July 1483.

Edward V and his younger brother Richard, Duke of York, were lodged in the Tower of London, then both a royal residence and a state prison. Initially they were seen by servants and visitors playing in the grounds and appearing at windows. By late summer their public appearances stopped altogether. Contemporary observers noticed their disappearance but left no definitive explanation. Rumours that they had died circulated surprisingly quickly, both in England and abroad, although no official announcement was ever made.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrp.org.uk/tower-of-london/history-and-stories/the-princes-in-the-tower/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrp.org.uk">[Historic Royal Palaces]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrp.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The disappearance mattered because the boys remained the strongest dynastic challenge to Richard's claim. As long as they lived, rival factions could rally around them, making their fate a matter of national politics rather than merely a family tragedy.

## Richard III and the Other Suspects

The mystery has never centred on whether the princes disappeared—they unquestionably did—but on who, if anyone, ordered their deaths.

### Why Richard III remains the leading suspect

Most modern historians still regard Richard III as the most probable person responsible, though probability is not proof.

Several factors point towards him:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* He gained the throne directly through the princes' removal from the succession.
* The boys disappeared while under his protection and effective control.
* No convincing evidence exists that they reappeared during his reign.
* Foreign diplomats reported widespread rumours that the princes had died.

</div>

None of these points proves murder, but together they explain why Richard remains the principal suspect. Modern historians generally distinguish between evidence of motive and evidence of direct guilt, noting that no contemporary document records an order from Richard to kill the boys.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrp.org.uk/tower-of-london/history-and-stories/the-princes-in-the-tower/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrp.org.uk">[Historic Royal Palaces]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrp.org.uk.</span></span></span>

### Henry Stafford, Duke of Buckingham

Some historians have suggested that Henry Stafford, Duke of Buckingham, may have acted independently.

Buckingham possessed royal blood, enjoyed considerable influence in 1483 and later rebelled against Richard. Supporters of this theory argue that he may have believed eliminating the princes would strengthen his own ambitions or secure Richard's position without explicit orders.

The main weakness is that no contemporary evidence links Buckingham directly to the princes' disappearance.

### Henry VII

Another theory shifts responsibility to Henry Tudor, later Henry VII.

Supporters argue that if the princes survived Richard's reign, Henry would have had an equally strong motive to remove rival claimants after taking the throne in 1485.

This explanation faces a major difficulty: by the time Henry became king, there is no reliable evidence that the princes were still alive. Contemporary rumours about their deaths had already spread during Richard's reign, making a post-1485 killing less persuasive for many historians.

### Did the princes survive?

A minority of researchers argue that one or both boys escaped.

This interpretation gained renewed attention through the "Missing Princes Project", which proposes that later pretenders—especially Perkin Warbeck and perhaps Lambert Simnel—may have represented genuine surviving princes rather than impostors. While this research has introduced new documents into the debate, most historians remain unconvinced, arguing that the evidence is circumstantial and fits more comfortably with established explanations of Tudor-era pretenders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/princes-in-tower-solved-g650v0qrr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times Has the Princes in the Tower mystery finally been solved?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Through a decade-long investigation called the &quot;Missing Princes Project,&quot; Langley has uncovered numerous documents indicating the princes...</span></span></span>

## The Bones, DNA and the Limits of Proof

In 1674, workmen demolishing a staircase in the Tower of London reportedly discovered the skeletons of two children in a wooden chest. King Charles II ordered the remains placed in an urn in Westminster Abbey, where they remain today.

The discovery appeared to fit the long-standing story that the princes had been murdered and secretly buried within the Tower. However, the excavation was poorly documented by modern standards, making it impossible to establish exactly where the bones originated or whether they belonged together.

The remains underwent scientific examination in 1933. Investigators concluded that they were probably those of two children of approximately the right age, but the bones were incomplete, had been handled repeatedly over centuries and could not be securely identified. The examination therefore failed to answer either who the children were or how they died.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrp.org.uk/tower-of-london/history-and-stories/the-princes-in-the-tower/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrp.org.uk">[Historic Royal Palaces]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrp.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Since the successful identification of Richard III's own remains through archaeology, genealogy and ancient DNA analysis in 2012–2013, there have been repeated calls for the Westminster Abbey bones to undergo similar testing. Richard III's identification demonstrated how mitochondrial DNA, archaeological context and historical records can together produce exceptionally strong conclusions when suitable evidence survives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://le.ac.uk/richard-iii/identification/genetics/overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: le.ac.uk">[University of Leicester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">le.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of LeicesterOverview of the genetics and genealogical techniques used to identify the remains &#124; Richard III: Discovery and ide...</span></span></span>

Yet the comparison is not straightforward.

Several obstacles remain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The bones' archaeological context was disturbed centuries ago.
* Ancient DNA may be too degraded or contaminated.
* Suitable comparative DNA would require carefully chosen descendants.
* Permission would be required from the authorities responsible for Westminster Abbey before any further examination.

</div>

Even if DNA established that the bones belonged to Edward V and his brother, it would almost certainly not reveal who caused their deaths. Likewise, if the remains proved not to be the princes, historians would still not know what ultimately happened to them.

## Why the Mystery Endures

The Princes in the Tower have become more than a medieval crime story because almost every surviving source raises questions of reliability.

Much of the familiar narrative comes from writers working decades after the events. Sir Thomas More's influential *History of King Richard III*, written roughly thirty years later, vividly described the boys being smothered on Richard's orders. Although enormously influential—and later adapted by William Shakespeare—More wrote under the Tudor dynasty, whose legitimacy benefited from portraying Richard as a tyrant. Historians therefore treat his account as an important but problematic source rather than unquestioned fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrp.org.uk/tower-of-london/history-and-stories/the-princes-in-the-tower/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrp.org.uk">[Historic Royal Palaces]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrp.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Recent discoveries continue to add pieces to the puzzle without solving it. For example, historian Tim Thornton has argued that a newly identified fifteenth-century will strengthens the traditional murder theory by linking a chain believed to have belonged to Edward V with relatives of Sir James Tyrell, the man later accused of carrying out the killings. Supporters see this as valuable corroboration; critics argue it still falls short of direct proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/princes-in-the-tower-murder-theory-v33lj9js8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times Princes in the Tower: &#x27;smoking gun&#x27; evidence bolsters murder theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The will reveals that a chain belonging to Edward V was part of the estate of Lady Margaret Capell, who was linked to Sir James Tyrell, o...</span></span></span>

The case therefore remains a model historical mystery. The broad outline is widely accepted: two royal boys disappeared during a succession crisis and almost certainly died young. What remains unresolved is the crucial question that has fascinated readers for more than five centuries—whether Richard III ordered their deaths, whether someone else acted independently, or whether events unfolded in a way that no surviving source fully records. Until new documentary evidence or decisive scientific discoveries emerge, that final answer is likely to remain beyond reach.
