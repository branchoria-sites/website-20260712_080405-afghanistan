## Introduction

The discovery of Toumaï in northern Chad transformed one of the biggest questions in human evolution: when did our ancestors first begin walking upright? Found in 2001 in the Djurab Desert and later classified as *Sahelanthropus tchadensis*, the fossil dates to around seven million years ago, close to the estimated split between the human and chimpanzee lineages. If Toumaï was habitually bipedal, it could represent one of the earliest known members of the human family. If it was not, it may instead have been an extinct ape that lived near the common ancestor of both humans and chimpanzees. More than two decades after its discovery, that debate remains one of palaeoanthropology's most important unresolved questions because the available evidence is both remarkably significant and frustratingly incomplete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04901-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; NatureAugust 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 24, 2022</span></span></span>

## The Skull That Changed Human Origins

When the Toumaï skull was announced in 2002, it immediately challenged long-held assumptions about where the earliest hominins evolved. Most of the oldest known candidates for the human lineage had been discovered in East Africa. Toumaï, by contrast, came from central Africa in what is now Chad, suggesting that early hominins may have occupied a much broader geographical range than previously believed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2025/may/27/the-curse-of-toumai-ancient-skull-disputed-femur-feud-humanity-origins" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the curse of toumai ancient skull disputed femur feud humanity origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This assertion challenged prevailing theories that humanity originated in East Africa. Brunet named the species Sahelanthropus tchadensis...</span></span></span>

The skull itself presents a striking mixture of primitive and apparently human-like features. Its brain was only about the size of a modern chimpanzee's, yet the face is comparatively short and the canine teeth are smaller than expected for an ape of similar age. The feature that generated the greatest excitement was the position of the foramen magnum—the opening underneath the skull through which the spinal cord passes. In living mammals, this opening tends to lie farther forward in species that habitually hold the head above an upright spine. Toumaï's discoverers argued that its position supported habitual bipedalism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04901-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; NatureAugust 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 24, 2022</span></span></span>

That conclusion has never been universally accepted. The skull was badly distorted during fossilisation and required extensive digital reconstruction. Critics argue that small differences in reconstruction can alter measurements of the skull base and therefore affect interpretations of posture. As a result, the skull alone has never been considered decisive proof of upright walking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2022/sep/03/oldest-human-or-just-another-ape-row-erupts-over-7m-year-old-fossil" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianOldest human or just another ape? ## What the Femur and Forearm Bones Suggest

The debate became even more intense because the skull was not the only important fossil recovered from the site. Researchers also discovered a partial thigh bone (femur) and two forearm bones (ulnae), but these remains were not formally described until 2022. Their delayed publication fuelled years of speculation about whether the limb bones supported or contradicted the original interpretation of Toumaï.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2025/may/27/the-curse-of-toumai-ancient-skull-disputed-femur-feud-humanity-origins" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the curse of toumai ancient skull disputed femur feud humanity origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This assertion challenged prevailing theories that humanity originated in East Africa. Brunet named the species Sahelanthropus tchadensis...</span></span></span>

The 2022 study concluded that the femur shows several anatomical features most consistent with habitual bipedalism. At the same time, the forearm bones display adaptations for climbing, suggesting an animal that still spent a considerable amount of time in trees. Rather than depicting a fully modern style of walking, the researchers proposed that *Sahelanthropus* combined efficient upright movement on the ground with regular arboreal climbing—a locomotor pattern unlike either modern humans or living chimpanzees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04901-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; NatureAugust 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 24, 2022</span></span></span>

This interpretation fits an increasingly common view of early human evolution. Bipedalism may not have appeared as an abrupt replacement for tree climbing but as one element of a mixed lifestyle in woodland environments. In this scenario, early hominins retained many climbing abilities while gradually becoming more efficient walkers on the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04901-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; NatureAugust 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 24, 2022</span></span></span>

## Why the Hominin Claim Remains Disputed

The central problem is not simply whether the femur looks human-like or ape-like. It is whether the available fossils can confidently be assigned to the same individual—or even the same species.

The skull and limb bones were recovered from the same fossil locality among numerous animal remains, but they were not physically connected. No anatomical join proves that the femur belonged to Toumaï itself. Researchers attribute the bones to *Sahelanthropus* largely because no other hominin species is known from the site, yet critics argue that this remains an inference rather than direct proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2025/may/27/the-curse-of-toumai-ancient-skull-disputed-femur-feud-humanity-origins" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the curse of toumai ancient skull disputed femur feud humanity origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This assertion challenged prevailing theories that humanity originated in East Africa. Brunet named the species Sahelanthropus tchadensis...</span></span></span>

Interpreting the femur is also difficult because it is incomplete and damaged. Scientists disagree over which anatomical features are reliable indicators of habitual bipedalism and which could also occur in extinct apes with different forms of locomotion. Much of the disagreement therefore concerns methodology as well as anatomy. The same fossil can be measured in different ways and compared with different living or extinct species, producing contrasting conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adv0130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Earliest evidence of hominin bipedalism in Sahelanthropus tchadensis &#124; Science AdvancesJanuary 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 2, 2026</span></span></span>

The debate has also been unusually public. Disputes over access to the fossils, delayed publication of the limb bones and competing interpretations led to years of personal and scientific conflict within the research community. Although the controversy sometimes appears dramatic, it ultimately reflects a normal challenge in palaeoanthropology: major evolutionary claims often depend on extremely small numbers of fragmentary fossils.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2025/may/27/the-curse-of-toumai-ancient-skull-disputed-femur-feud-humanity-origins" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the curse of toumai ancient skull disputed femur feud humanity origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This assertion challenged prevailing theories that humanity originated in East Africa. ## New Evidence Has Strengthened the Case—but Not Ended the Debate

In 2026, an independent team reanalysed the published limb fossils using additional imaging and comparative anatomy. They argued that the femur possesses a small bony attachment—the femoral tubercle—associated with the ligament system that stabilises the hip during upright walking. Combined with other aspects of the femur and limb proportions, the researchers concluded that *Sahelanthropus* was most likely an early biped while still retaining adaptations for climbing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adv0130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Earliest evidence of hominin bipedalism in Sahelanthropus tchadensis &#124; Science AdvancesJanuary 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 2, 2026</span></span></span>

The authors presented this as the strongest evidence yet that habitual bipedalism existed around seven million years ago, potentially pushing the earliest convincing evidence for upright walking farther back than previously accepted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adv0130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Earliest evidence of hominin bipedalism in Sahelanthropus tchadensis &#124; Science AdvancesJanuary 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 2, 2026</span></span></span>

Even so, scepticism remains. Other specialists argue that the proposed diagnostic features are subtle, the fossil's preservation is poor, and several characteristics remain compatible with an ape-like form of locomotion. Until more complete skeletons are discovered in Chad or elsewhere, many researchers believe the question cannot be settled conclusively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/jan/02/fresh-bone-analysis-earliest-ancestor-of-humankind-but-doubts-remain" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Researchers led by Dr. Scott Williams from NYU have found signs that the species may have walked upright based on features in its thigh a...</span></span></span>

## Why the Mystery Still Matters

The mystery surrounding Toumaï is larger than a single fossil. It influences how scientists reconstruct one of the defining transitions in human evolution: the emergence of habitual walking on two legs.

If Toumaï was truly bipedal, then upright walking evolved very close to the divergence between humans and chimpanzees and may have appeared before many other recognisably human characteristics, including larger brains. If the fossil instead represents an extinct ape outside the human lineage, then the search for the earliest unquestioned hominin shifts elsewhere.

For Chad, the discovery remains one of the country's greatest archaeological and palaeoanthropological contributions. Regardless of where the debate ultimately ends, Toumaï demonstrated that central Africa must be part of any serious account of early human evolution. The continuing argument over its posture illustrates how scientific mysteries often endure not because evidence is absent, but because a handful of extraordinary fossils can support more than one plausible interpretation until new discoveries tip the balance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04901-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Postcranial evidence of late Miocene hominin bipedalism in Chad &#124; NatureAugust 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 24, 2022</span></span></span>
