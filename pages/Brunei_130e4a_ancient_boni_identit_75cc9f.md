## Introduction

Was the ancient kingdom called **Boni** in Chinese records actually [Brunei]({{ 'what-bruneis-fragmentary-past-still/' | relative_url }})? The short answer is **probably yes for many later references, but not for every early one**. This is one of the most important historical puzzles in Brunei's early history because Chinese chronicles provide some of the earliest written descriptions of a trading state on the north coast of Borneo. However, those records were compiled over centuries, used different spellings, and sometimes described places whose locations remain uncertain.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Brunei_130e4a_ancient_boni_identit_75cc9f-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Ancient Boni illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Brunei_130e4a_ancient_boni_identit_75cc9f-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Brunei_130e4a_ancient_boni_identit_75cc9f-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Modern historians generally agree that the **Song and Ming dynasty references to Boni (or Po-ni)** are closely connected with the polity that developed into historical Brunei. The greater controversy concerns whether earlier names such as **Poli** referred to the same kingdom. Careful re-examination of the original Chinese texts suggests they probably did not, meaning Brunei's documented history cannot simply be extended backwards by treating every similar place-name as an earlier version of the same state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brill.com/view/journals/bki/169/2-3/article-p213_2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brill.com">[Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">article p213 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- enNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## What Chinese records actually say

Chinese dynastic histories are among the most valuable written sources for early Southeast Asia because many local kingdoms left few contemporary written records of their own. Officials in imperial China recorded diplomatic missions, tribute embassies, trade goods and descriptions of foreign rulers.

For Boni, the evidence becomes much firmer from the late tenth century onwards. The earliest widely accepted reference appears in the **Taiping Huanyu Ji**, compiled during the Song dynasty, followed by later entries in official dynastic histories. These records describe a maritime kingdom engaged in long-distance trade that maintained diplomatic contacts with China and periodically sent tribute missions. The first clearly documented embassy reached China in **977**, with further missions recorded during the Song period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/290627800_Boni_in_Chinese_sources_from_the_tenth_to_the_eighteenth_century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Boni in Chinese sources from the tenth to the eighteenth centuryJanuary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2014</span></span></span>

The evidence becomes especially detailed under the Ming dynasty. In 1408, the ruler known in Chinese records as **Ma-na-jih-chia-na** travelled to the Ming court but died in Nanjing before returning home. His tomb still survives there, providing one of the strongest pieces of evidence linking Ming-period Boni with the Brunei Sultanate recognised today. His son later visited China to honour the grave, and diplomatic exchanges continued under imperial patronage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265084717_Boni_in_Chinese_Sources_Translations_of_Relevant_Texts_from_the_Song_to_the_Qing_Dynasties" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

These records leave little doubt that a significant kingdom called Boni existed and maintained official relations with China. The real debate is how far back that identification can safely be extended.

## Why Boni, Po-ni and Poli may differ

The greatest source of confusion is that Chinese writers used several names that appear superficially similar when rendered into the Latin alphabet.

Earlier historians sometimes argued for a straightforward sequence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Poli in the sixth century
* Boni or Po-ni in later centuries
* historical Brunei thereafter

</div>

This creates an attractive continuous national history stretching back more than 1,400 years. Yet closer study of the original Classical Chinese texts has challenged that interpretation.

Historian Johannes Kurz has shown that **Poli and Boni are treated as separate places within the Chinese sources themselves**, rather than successive names for the same kingdom. The texts never explicitly identify Poli as becoming Boni, and geographical descriptions often fit poorly with such an equation. Some references may instead describe different parts of Island Southeast Asia or entirely separate trading polities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brill.com/view/journals/bki/169/2-3/article-p213_2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brill.com">[Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">article p213 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- enNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

Another complication is that Chinese officials did not necessarily describe states in the modern sense. A recorded name might refer to:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a trading port;
* the ruler's residence;
* a coastal kingdom whose political centre later moved;
* a wider region recognised by merchants rather than fixed borders.

</div>

As maritime trade expanded and declined, these political centres could shift considerably. Two records using similar names centuries apart do not automatically describe exactly the same state.

This explains why historians today distinguish between **identifying a probable location** and **proving political continuity**. Similar names alone are not sufficient evidence.

## What archaeology adds to the debate

Archaeology provides an independent line of evidence that helps evaluate the written sources.

Excavations around **[Kota Batu]({{ 'kota-batu/' | relative_url }})** and other sites along the Brunei River have uncovered large quantities of imported Chinese ceramics, coins and luxury goods dating from the Song and later periods. These finds demonstrate that the Brunei River formed part of an active international trading network linking China with maritime Southeast Asia.

Another important discovery is a Chinese tombstone dated to **1264**, indicating the presence of people connected with Chinese culture in Brunei well before the best-documented phase of the Islamic sultanate. These discoveries support the picture presented by later Chinese records of an established commercial centre engaged in overseas trade.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brill.com/view/journals/bki/169/2-3/article-p213_2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brill.com">[Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">article p213 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- enNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

However, archaeology also has limits. Imported ceramics prove sustained trade, but they do not reveal what Chinese officials actually meant when they used names such as Boni or Poli centuries earlier. Material remains can confirm extensive contact with China without resolving every question of historical geography.

## Why the debate still matters

At first glance the dispute may appear to concern little more than changing spellings. In reality, it affects how Brunei's earliest history is reconstructed.

If every early Chinese place-name referred to Brunei, then the kingdom's documented history could extend securely into the sixth century. If Poli was somewhere else, then the earliest reliable written evidence for Brunei becomes significantly later, beginning with the tenth-century references to Boni.

Most current scholarship favours the second interpretation. Rather than seeing this as diminishing Brunei's history, historians argue that it produces a more accurate picture of a dynamic maritime world in which ports, kingdoms and political centres evolved over time instead of remaining fixed for a millennium.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brill.com/view/journals/bki/169/2-3/article-p213_2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brill.com">[Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">article p213 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- enNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

The debate also illustrates a broader challenge in Southeast Asian history: many early states are known primarily through foreign observers, whose descriptions depended on merchants, translators and diplomatic conventions that were not designed to satisfy modern historical questions.

## The safest conclusion

The balance of evidence supports a cautious position.

The **later Chinese references to Boni are very likely describing the polity that developed into historical Brunei**, particularly from the Song and Ming dynasties onward. The diplomatic missions, archaeological discoveries along the Brunei River and the surviving Ming-period royal tomb in Nanjing all reinforce that connection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/290627800_Boni_in_Chinese_sources_from_the_tenth_to_the_eighteenth_century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Boni in Chinese sources from the tenth to the eighteenth centuryJanuary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2014</span></span></span>

The earlier identification of **Poli** with Brunei is much weaker. Modern analysis of the original Chinese texts finds no convincing proof that Poli was simply an earlier name for Boni, and many specialists now regard them as different entities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brill.com/view/journals/bki/169/2-3/article-p213_2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brill.com">[Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">article p213 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- enNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

The mystery therefore is not whether ancient Brunei existed, but **how far back the surviving evidence allows historians to trace it with confidence**. The most responsible conclusion is that Brunei's early history is genuine but more complex than a single uninterrupted line of names stretching back into antiquity.
