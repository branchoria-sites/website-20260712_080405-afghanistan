## Introduction

The story that founded [Monaco]({{ 'which-monaco-mysteries-survive-the/' | relative_url }}) is as famous as it is uncertain. According to tradition, on the night of 8 January 1297, François Grimaldi gained entry to the fortress on the Rock of Monaco by disguising himself as a Franciscan monk. Once inside, he and his companions overpowered the guards and opened the gates to waiting supporters, allowing the Grimaldis to seize the stronghold. The episode remains central to Monaco's identity, with two armed monks still supporting the Grimaldi coat of arms. Yet historians distinguish between two separate questions: whether the Grimaldi seizure of the fortress really happened, and whether the dramatic monk disguise can be confirmed from contemporary evidence. The first is well established. The second is far less certain, making this one of Monaco's most enduring historical legends rather than a straightforward historical fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.palais.mc/en/the-institution/the-prince-s-palace-of-monaco-1-16.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: palais.mc">[palais.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">palais.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span></span></span>

## What Happened on 8 January 1297?

The political background is much less mysterious than the famous disguise. During the late thirteenth century, the Republic of Genoa was divided between the Guelphs, who supported the papacy, and the Ghibellines, who backed the Holy Roman Emperor. The Grimaldi family belonged to the defeated Guelph faction and had been driven into exile after losing control in Genoa. Capturing the fortified Rock of Monaco offered a valuable military and political base on the Mediterranean coast.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.palais.mc/en/the-institution/the-prince-s-palace-of-monaco-1-16.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: palais.mc">[palais.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">palais.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span></span></span>

According to the traditional account, François Grimaldi—later nicknamed "Malizia", usually translated as "the Cunning" or "the Crafty"—approached the fortress dressed as a Franciscan friar seeking shelter. Once admitted, he revealed his true identity, overcame the gatekeepers and enabled armed followers hidden nearby to storm the fortress. Monaco's official historical narrative continues to commemorate this event as the symbolic beginning of Grimaldi rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.palais.mc/en/the-institution/the-prince-s-palace-of-monaco-1-16.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: palais.mc">[palais.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">palais.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span></span></span>

However, the capture was not the permanent triumph that later legend sometimes suggests. Genoese forces recovered the fortress only a few years later, in 1301. Lasting Grimaldi control emerged gradually over subsequent decades, particularly under Charles I, who consolidated the family's position and expanded its territories. This distinction is important because Monaco's founding story compresses a lengthy struggle into a single memorable night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.palais.mc/en/the-institution/the-prince-s-palace-of-monaco-1-16.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: palais.mc">[palais.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">palais.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span></span></span>

## Which Details Are Actually Documented?

The central historical fact—that the Grimaldis seized Monaco during the Guelph-Ghibelline conflicts—is widely accepted. The uncertainty concerns the theatrical details that transformed a military raid into one of Europe's best-known dynastic legends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://daily.jstor.org/monaco-a-mediterranean-principality-shaped-by-the-middle-ages/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: daily.jstor.org">[JSTOR Daily]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">daily.jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Daily Monaco, a Mediterranean Principality Shaped by the Middle Ages</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JSTOR DailyMonaco, a Mediterranean Principality Shaped by the Middle Ages - JSTOR Daily...</span></span></span>

Several features deserve separate treatment.

**The seizure itself:** Historians broadly agree that a Grimaldi-led capture of the fortress occurred in January 1297. This forms part of the accepted chronology of Monaco's early history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.palais.mc/en/the-institution/the-prince-s-palace-of-monaco-1-16.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: palais.mc">[palais.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">palais.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span></span></span>

**The monk disguise:** This is the famous element that is hardest to verify independently. Modern official histories repeat the story, but surviving public accounts rarely identify a contemporary eyewitness description from 1297 itself. Much of the detailed narrative appears in later tradition rather than securely dated contemporary records. Modern historians therefore often describe the disguise as legendary or disputed rather than established fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince%27s_Palace_of_Monaco" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span></span></span>

**The assault itself:** Stories differ in minor details. Some versions describe François personally killing a guard before opening the gates. Others simply state that he admitted waiting soldiers after gaining entry. These variations are typical of medieval legends that evolved through repeated retelling rather than careful contemporary reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince%27s_Palace_of_Monaco" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span></span></span>

The result is not a mystery because historians doubt that fighting occurred, but because the best-known feature of the episode cannot be traced with confidence to contemporary evidence. The disguise may preserve a genuine tactic, or it may have become increasingly dramatic as the dynasty's official history developed.

## Why Would the Story Have Been Embellished?

Whether or not the disguise occurred exactly as described, it served several powerful symbolic purposes.

First, it presented the Grimaldis as clever survivors rather than overwhelming conquerors. Monaco has almost always depended on diplomacy and political agility rather than military strength. A founding myth centred on intelligence instead of brute force suited the principality's later self-image remarkably well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://daily.jstor.org/monaco-a-mediterranean-principality-shaped-by-the-middle-ages/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: daily.jstor.org">[JSTOR Daily]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">daily.jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Daily Monaco, a Mediterranean Principality Shaped by the Middle Ages</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JSTOR DailyMonaco, a Mediterranean Principality Shaped by the Middle Ages - JSTOR Daily...</span></span></span>

Second, medieval ruling families commonly strengthened their legitimacy through memorable origin stories. A dramatic seizure achieved through wit created a clearer dynastic beginning than decades of shifting alliances, repeated occupations and gradual consolidation.

Finally, the image of the disguised monk transformed an episode of factional warfare within Genoa into a distinctive national story. Many medieval fortresses changed hands through surprise attacks, bribery or deception. Monaco's version endured because it became inseparable from the identity of the ruling house.

## How the Story Shaped Grimaldi Identity

Regardless of its exact historical accuracy, the disguised monk became one of the most successful pieces of dynastic symbolism in Europe.

The most obvious reminder appears in Monaco's coat of arms, where two sword-bearing monks support the shield. They represent the legendary disguise rather than ordinary religious figures, making the founding story visible on official state symbols centuries after the event is supposed to have occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.monte-carlo.mc/en/general/important-dates/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: monte-carlo.mc">[Monte-Carlo.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">monte-carlo.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Important dates</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monaco Monte-Carlo...</span></span></span>

The Prince's Palace also commemorates François Grimaldi as the dynasty's symbolic founder, while statues and official historical displays reinforce the association between the family and the famous exploit. Although Charles I established more durable political control, François remains the better-known figure because his story provides the dramatic beginning of the dynasty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.palais.mc/en/the-institution/the-prince-s-palace-of-monaco-1-16.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: palais.mc">[palais.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">palais.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prince&#x27;s Palace of Monaco</span></span></span>

This distinction illustrates how national memory often differs from administrative history. The individual who created the legend is not necessarily the one who secured the state.

## Why the Legend Still Matters

The disguised monk remains important not because historians expect to discover a hidden conspiracy, but because it demonstrates how historical memory is constructed.

Few readers question that the Grimaldis captured Monaco in the late thirteenth century. The genuine historical puzzle lies in separating the documented military event from the symbolic narrative that followed. Like many medieval foundation stories, the tale combines credible political circumstances with details that became increasingly polished through centuries of repetition.

For Monaco, the uncertainty has become part of the legend's appeal rather than a weakness. Even if the monk's habit cannot be proved beyond doubt, the story captures a larger historical truth: a small dynasty survived in a dangerous political landscape through calculated opportunism, careful alliances and remarkable resilience. That broader lesson has endured far longer than certainty about exactly how one winter night's assault unfolded.
