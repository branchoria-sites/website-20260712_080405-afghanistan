## Introduction

The story of “Red Legs” Greaves is one of the Caribbean’s most colourful pirate tales. According to the popular version, Greaves was a reformed buccaneer living on Nevis who was condemned to death, only to escape when an earthquake swallowed the town where he was imprisoned. The dramatic survival story has become closely associated with the wider legend of the “lost” town of [Jamestown]({{ 'jamestown/' | relative_url }}) on Nevis and is often repeated in books, websites and tourist retellings.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Saint_Kitts_and_Nevi_a66de5_red_legs_pirate_myth_88ab3d-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Red Legs illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Saint_Kitts_and_Nevi_a66de5_red_legs_pirate_myth_88ab3d-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Saint_Kitts_and_Nevi_a66de5_red_legs_pirate_myth_88ab3d-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The problem is that the evidence for Greaves himself is remarkably weak. No reliable seventeenth-century records have yet been found that place him among the documented pirates of the period, nor do contemporary accounts of Caribbean earthquakes mention his miraculous escape. Instead, the character appears to emerge only in the early twentieth century, before being repeated so often that fiction gradually acquired the appearance of history. That transformation—from an entertaining story into accepted local folklore—is the real historical mystery.

## Was Red Legs Greaves Ever a Real Pirate?

### The Pirate Story Told Today

The standard version follows a familiar adventure narrative.

Greaves is said to have been born in Barbados around the middle of the seventeenth century, the son of transported Scottish or Irish prisoners. His nickname supposedly came from the term "Red Legs", used for poor white labourers in Barbados whose sunburned bare legs distinguished them from wealthier settlers.

After escaping an abusive master, he allegedly boarded a pirate ship by mistake, eventually becoming captain himself. Unlike many fictional pirates, Greaves is portrayed as unusually humane, avoiding unnecessary violence and refusing to mistreat civilians. His greatest exploit is usually described as the capture of Margarita Island off Venezuela, after which he supposedly retired as a planter on Nevis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

The final act is the most memorable. Recognised by a former victim, Greaves is arrested, convicted of piracy and imprisoned while awaiting execution. Before he can be hanged, an earthquake destroys or submerges the town, allowing him to escape. He is rescued by a passing ship, later becomes a pirate hunter, receives a royal pardon and dies a respected philanthropist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

It is an excellent adventure story—but historians have struggled to find evidence that it actually happened.

## Why the Seventeenth-Century Record Is Missing

The strongest argument against Greaves being a historical pirate is not a contradictory document but the absence of supporting evidence where it ought to exist.

The Caribbean in the later seventeenth century generated extensive documentation. Colonial governors reported pirate attacks, Spanish authorities recorded raids, admiralty courts tried captured pirates, and many well-known buccaneers appear repeatedly in official correspondence. Yet Greaves is absent from these contemporary sources.

This silence is especially striking because several episodes attributed to him would have been major events if genuine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the capture of Margarita Island;
* his supposed retirement as a planter on Nevis;
* his arrest and trial for piracy;
* his dramatic escape during an earthquake.

</div>

Each of these should have left documentary traces, yet researchers have not identified reliable contemporary records confirming any of them. Modern summaries of Greaves almost invariably acknowledge that his story does not appear in period pirate records and may be entirely fictional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

Even the earthquake varies from one retelling to another. Some versions place the escape on Nevis during the earthquake of 1690, while others transfer the event to the destruction of Port Royal, Jamaica, in 1692. Still others mention an earthquake in 1680. Such shifting details are typical of folklore rather than eyewitness history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

## How the Legend Entered Print

One of the most revealing clues is when Greaves first appears in published literature.

Rather than emerging from seventeenth- or eighteenth-century sources, the story appears to surface in the work of American travel writer and naturalist A. Hyatt Verrill in 1923. Verrill presented Greaves as though he were historical but supplied no documentary evidence for the narrative. Soon afterwards, Philip Gosse included essentially the same biography in *The Pirates' Who's Who* (1924), giving the story much greater visibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

Because Gosse's book was widely read and frequently mined by later authors, Greaves entered the popular pirate canon despite the uncertain foundations of his biography.

Researchers have also noted how closely Greaves resembles fictional pirate heroes popular in the early twentieth century. He is brave but compassionate, rejects unnecessary cruelty, reforms his life, survives impossible danger and ends his days as a respected gentleman. These characteristics echo literary adventure fiction far more than the documented careers of most Caribbean pirates. Several commentators have observed similarities with Rafael Sabatini's enormously successful novel *Captain Blood*, published only a few years before Gosse's work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

This does not prove deliberate invention, but it does suggest that literary influence may have shaped the story as much as historical memory.

## How Tourism and Folklore Created a Legend

Greaves became especially attractive once his story merged with the legend of Jamestown on Nevis.

The earthquake that damaged Nevis in 1690 is historically real, but later folklore exaggerated the disaster into the image of an entire town disappearing beneath the sea. Once that dramatic setting existed, it naturally attracted equally dramatic human stories.

A pirate escaping execution because an earthquake destroyed his prison provides exactly the kind of memorable anecdote that visitors remember and guides enjoy recounting. As the Jamestown legend grew during the twentieth century, Greaves increasingly became one of its most recognisable characters.

Archaeological work has complicated that narrative. Investigations around Morton's Bay suggest a more gradual and complex history for the settlement than the popular image of a prosperous town suddenly swallowed by the sea. Without the spectacular destruction imagined in folklore, the setting for Greaves' miraculous escape becomes much less convincing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

This illustrates an important feature of historical mythmaking: two uncertain stories can reinforce one another. The uncertain history of Jamestown made Greaves' escape seem plausible, while Greaves' dramatic survival helped make the lost-town legend more memorable.

## What Is Most Likely?

Based on the available evidence, several explanations remain possible.

The least likely is that every detail of the familiar biography is historically accurate but somehow escaped all surviving contemporary records.

More plausible possibilities include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A local folk tale** that A. Hyatt Verrill heard while travelling in the Caribbean and retold as history.
* **A heavily embellished account** loosely inspired by a real but otherwise unknown colonial figure.
* **A largely literary creation**, assembled from pirate stereotypes, earthquake folklore and romantic adventure writing before being repeated as fact.

</div>

None of these explanations can be proven conclusively because the documentary record is so sparse. What can be said with confidence is that the evidence supporting Greaves is dramatically weaker than the evidence supporting the genuine pirates of the seventeenth-century Caribbean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Legs_Greaves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Red Legs Greaves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Legs Greaves</span></span></span>

## Why the Story Still Matters

Red Legs Greaves remains significant not because historians have confirmed his exploits, but because his legend shows how historical myths develop.

His story demonstrates how:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* an undocumented character can become accepted through repeated publication;
* colourful adventure narratives often outlive their original evidence;
* tourism and local folklore can reinforce one another;
* archaeological research sometimes challenges stories that have become part of popular memory.

</div>

Within the wider historical mysteries of [Saint Kitts and Nevis]({{ 'saint-kitts-and-nevis/' | relative_url }}), Greaves therefore occupies an unusual place. Unlike the debates surrounding Jamestown or colonial violence on the islands, the mystery is not primarily what happened in the seventeenth century. Instead, it is how a pirate with little or no contemporary documentary footprint became one of the Caribbean's most enduring legendary buccaneers.
