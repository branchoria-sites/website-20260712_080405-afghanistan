## Introduction

The **Port au Prince** is one of [Tonga]({{ 'what-secrets-still-survive-in-tongas/' | relative_url }})'s best-known maritime mysteries, but the mystery is often misunderstood. The historical facts are well documented: the British privateer and whaling ship was seized at Lifuka in the Ha'apai Islands in 1806, most of its crew were killed, and the teenage survivor William Mariner later produced one of the most important early accounts of Tongan society. What remains uncertain is not whether the ship existed or how it met its end, but whether any significant treasure still lies in its wreck. Modern stories frequently describe a lost fortune waiting beneath Tongan waters, yet the available historical and archaeological evidence is far less dramatic than the legend suggests. The real puzzle lies in separating documented cargo from later treasure-hunting mythology.

## The ship's final voyage and capture at Lifuka

The *Port au Prince* began life as an East Indiaman before being converted into a British privateer during the Napoleonic Wars. Its mission combined commerce raiding with whaling in the Pacific. By the time it reached Tonga in late 1806, the vessel had endured a difficult voyage, changes of command and extensive wear.

Historical accounts agree that the ship anchored off Lifuka in the Ha'apai group, where its officers believed they had established friendly relations with local chiefs. Instead, the vessel was attacked under the authority of the powerful chief Fīnau ʻUlukālala. Most of the crew were killed, while a handful of survivors, including the fourteen-year-old clerk William Mariner, were spared. Mariner remained in Tonga for roughly four years before eventually returning to Britain. His recollections became one of the foundational written sources for pre-Christian Tongan history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Mariner_%28writer%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">William Mariner (writer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">William Mariner (writer</span></span></span>

The attackers stripped the vessel of useful materials before destroying it. Timber, iron, cordage and other practical resources were far more valuable to the Tongans than European luxury goods. The ship itself was burned and ultimately lost, creating the historical basis for later claims of a treasure-filled wreck.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Mariner_%28writer%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">William Mariner (writer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">William Mariner (writer</span></span></span>

## What William Mariner recorded about the wreck

Mariner's account remains the closest thing to eyewitness evidence. It provides extensive detail about the capture, his adoption into Tongan society and the fate of the ship.

Importantly, his narrative does **not** describe an enormous cache of untouched treasure disappearing beneath the sea. Instead, it shows that valuable items were actively removed after the capture. Practical materials such as metal fittings, weapons and equipment were eagerly salvaged because they had immediate value in Tonga. Mariner also described the destruction of the vessel, rather than a mysterious disappearance with its cargo untouched.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/an-account-of-the-natives-of-the-tonga-islands-in-the-south-pacific-ocean/A730FA4B992452F499F000316EB0E772" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn Account of the Natives of the Tonga Islands, in the South Pacific Ocean...</span></span></span>

His account nevertheless leaves room for uncertainty. The ship had previously taken prizes during privateering operations and reportedly carried:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Silver objects captured from Spanish shipping.
* Copper and other metal cargo.
* Religious artefacts such as candlesticks, chalices and crucifixes.
* Commercial cargo accumulated during the voyage.

</div>

Because Mariner did not produce a complete inventory of what was removed before the wreck was destroyed, historians cannot state with certainty whether every valuable object was recovered. That gap in the record is the foundation upon which later treasure stories were built.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Mariner_%28writer%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">William Mariner (writer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">William Mariner (writer</span></span></span>

## How valuables became a modern treasure legend

Over time, a modest historical uncertainty evolved into a much grander legend.

Several factors encouraged this transformation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The ship had genuinely served as a privateer, leading many people to assume it carried spectacular wartime loot.
* References to Spanish silver and religious objects became exaggerated into stories of enormous chests of gold.
* The exact resting place of the wreck was never conclusively identified, allowing speculation to flourish.
* Popular books and treasure-hunting publications often preferred dramatic possibilities over cautious historical interpretation.

</div>

As a result, the *Port au Prince* became associated with the familiar idea of a lost pirate-style treasure ship, despite not actually being a pirate vessel. The historical evidence supports the existence of valuable cargo but not the existence of a vast hidden fortune.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://natlib.govt.nz/records/20662254" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: natlib.govt.nz">[National Library of New Zealand]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">natlib.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of New ZealandLooking for William Mariner and the t... &#124; Items &#124; National Library of New Zealand &#124; National Library of N...</span></span></span>

## Claimed discoveries and why they remain disputed

Interest in locating the wreck has continued into the twenty-first century. In 2012 divers announced that they believed they had found the *Port au Prince* off Foa Island in the Ha'apai group and suggested that significant treasure might still be present.

The announcement attracted international attention, but historians immediately urged caution. The principal objections were straightforward:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The identification of the wreck was not conclusively demonstrated through archaeological evidence.
* Ha'apai contains remains of numerous nineteenth-century vessels, making identification difficult.
* Public claims about treasure substantially exceeded what the historical record supports.

</div>

Historian Ian Campbell noted that many ships were lost in Tongan waters during the nineteenth century, particularly after commercial shipping increased, and warned that the wreck could easily belong to another vessel altogether. Without systematic archaeological investigation, the claimed identification remains unproven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rnz.co.nz/international/pacific-news/206470/tonga-treasure-claim-doubted" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rnz.co.nz">[RNZ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rnz.co.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">RNZTonga treasure claim doubted &#124; RNZ News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 10, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 10, 2012</span></span></span>

## Does the Port au Prince still hold treasure?

The most careful answer is **possibly—but there is no convincing evidence that a great treasure remains**.

Several points are reasonably well established:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The ship almost certainly carried valuable cargo when it arrived in Tonga.
* Tongans removed many useful and valuable objects before or during the vessel's destruction.
* No verified archaeological excavation has produced a major cache attributable to the *Port au Prince*.
* The exact identity of wrecks proposed as the *Port au Prince* has not been universally accepted by maritime archaeologists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Mariner_%28writer%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">William Mariner (writer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">William Mariner (writer</span></span></span>

</div>

It is entirely plausible that scattered artefacts remain on the seabed. Ships destroyed in shallow water often leave anchors, cannon, ballast, fastenings and isolated cargo buried beneath coral or sediment. What lacks evidence is the more dramatic claim that untouched chests of precious metals still await discovery.

## Why the mystery still matters in Tonga

The enduring fascination of the *Port au Prince* lies less in hypothetical treasure than in what the wreck represents. It marks a pivotal encounter between Tonga and the expanding European maritime world, produced William Mariner's extraordinary account of Tongan society, and illustrates how historical uncertainty can grow into enduring legend.

The genuine mystery is therefore archaeological rather than mythical. Until a wreck can be securely identified, carefully excavated and matched with documentary evidence, questions about the ship's final resting place and any surviving cargo will remain open. The available evidence supports curiosity, but not certainty, and it gives far stronger support to a story of historical encounter and selective salvage than to the image of an untouched fortune lying beneath the waters of Ha'apai.
