## Introduction

The strongest cases come from the civil war and the political violence surrounding it. The disappearance of children, the murder of poet [Roque Dalton]({{ 'roque-dalton/' | relative_url }}), the assassination of Archbishop Óscar Romero and the long struggle over the El Mozote massacre all involve substantial documentary or forensic evidence. Their mystery lies less in whether violence occurred than in the missing details: where victims went, who gave particular orders, what institutions knew and why accountability was delayed. Older puzzles, including the evacuation of Joya de Cerén and the meaning of unusual figurines found at San Isidro, offer a different kind of uncertainty—archaeological questions that can be narrowed by evidence but may never receive a single final answer.

## The children who vanished during the civil war

During El Salvador’s armed conflict from 1980 to 1992, children disappeared amid massacres, forced displacement and large military operations in rural areas. Some were killed. Others became separated from their families, were taken to institutions or passed into domestic and international adoption systems. Amnesty International reported that children were sent for adoption within El Salvador and to countries including the United States, France, Germany and the United Kingdom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/fr/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/amr290042003en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International El Salvador: Where are the &quot;disappeared&quot; children?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hundreds were assassinated in massacres committed by the armed forces; others were...Read more...</span></span></span>

This is not one mystery but hundreds of individual cases. The central questions are often painfully simple: did a child die during an attack, remain in military custody, enter an orphanage under another name or grow up abroad without knowing their identity?

The disappearances were difficult to investigate because records were incomplete, names had been changed and relatives often possessed little more than eyewitness memories. DNA testing later made some reunifications possible. Pro-Búsqueda, the organisation established to investigate the cases, has used family testimony, documentary research and genetic comparison to locate adults who had been separated from their families as children. Medical research on reunited families shows that discovery does not neatly repair the original loss: reunification can bring relief, grief, cultural dislocation and complicated questions about belonging.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asociacionprobusqueda.org/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asociacionprobusqueda.org">[asociacionprobusqueda.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asociacionprobusqueda.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Home &#124; Pro-BúsquedaPro-Búsqueda promotes the investigation of cases of forced disappearance of minors during the civil war to establish t...</span></span></span>

### The Serrano Cruz sisters

One of the best-documented cases concerns Ernestina and Erlinda Serrano Cruz, aged seven and three. They disappeared during a military operation in Chalatenango in June 1982. Their family said soldiers captured the girls after relatives became separated while fleeing the operation. The case eventually reached the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, which examined El Salvador’s failure to investigate their whereabouts effectively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.corteidh.or.cr/docs/casos/articulos/seriec_120_ing.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: corteidh.or.cr">[CorteIDH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">corteidh.or.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inter-American Court of Human Rights Case of the Serrano...August 7, 2009 — In its application, the Inter-American Commission in...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 7, 2009</span></span></span>

The judgment did not reveal what ultimately happened to the sisters. That limitation captures the wider problem. A court can establish that the state failed a family, order searches and require reparations, yet still be unable to reconstruct a child’s route through a chaotic or concealed system.

Several explanations may apply to different children:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* some died during military attacks without being identified;
* some were taken by soldiers and later placed in institutions;
* some entered adoption networks through irregular or falsified paperwork;
* some survived locally under changed identities;
* others may still be unaware that the family history given to them was incomplete.

</div>

There is no sound basis for treating every case as part of one centrally directed adoption conspiracy. There is, however, substantial evidence of repeated forced separation, institutional failures and obstruction. The Salvadoran military’s reluctance to open relevant wartime archives has repeatedly hindered investigators, while United Nations experts have stressed the importance of state search bodies and access to records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.voanews.com/a/el-salvador-missing-kids/4272321.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: voanews.com">[voanews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">voanews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Voice of America El Salvador&#x27;s Military Not Opening Archives For Missing Kids</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Voice of AmericaEl Salvador&#x27;s Military Not Opening Archives For Missing KidsFebruary 27, 2018 — 27 Feb 2018 — The country&#x27;s military has...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 27, 2018</span></span></span>

The disappearance of the children remains El Salvador’s most consequential unresolved historical puzzle because some answers may still be recoverable. Files can be opened, DNA databases expanded and adults approached sensitively. Each identification changes the historical record from an anonymous disappearance into a traceable human life.

## Roque Dalton: a known killing without a recovered body

Roque Dalton, one of El Salvador’s most celebrated poets and revolutionary intellectuals, was killed in May 1975 by members of the People’s Revolutionary Army, the guerrilla organisation he had joined. He had been accused by fellow militants of disloyalty and of acting as an intelligence agent—claims that have never been convincingly substantiated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://revista.drclas.harvard.edu/roque-dalton/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revista.drclas.harvard.edu">[ReVista]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revista.drclas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Re Vista Roque Dalton &#124; Re Vista</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Re Vista Roque Dalton &#124; Re Vista</span></span></span>

The broad responsibility is therefore less mysterious than it is sometimes presented. Dalton was not an inexplicable missing person. He was subjected to an internal revolutionary proceeding and executed by his own comrades. What remains unsettled is the precise sequence of decisions, who physically carried out the killing and what happened to his remains.

Accounts differ over the place and manner of burial. One reconstruction, based on information examined after the civil war, held that Dalton and another prisoner were placed in a shallow grave and that their exposed remains were later removed and thrown into the El Playón ravine. Other accounts have identified different possible locations or participants. No remains have been conclusively recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://typeinvestigations.org/investigation/2010/06/22/killed-roque-dalton/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: typeinvestigations.org">[typeinvestigations.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">typeinvestigations.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">killed roque dalton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">killed roque dalton</span></span></span>

Dalton’s family pursued legal action against former guerrilla leaders Joaquín Villalobos and Jorge Meléndez. A Salvadoran court closed the domestic case in 2012, but in 2021 the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights declared the family’s petition admissible, allowing international examination of allegations that the state had failed to investigate properly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalissues.org/news/2012/01/11/12400" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalissues.org">[Global Issues]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalissues.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Global Issues Salvadoran Poet Roque Dalton&#x27;s Murder</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global Issues Salvadoran Poet Roque Dalton&#x27;s Murder</span></span></span>

Several layers of uncertainty should be kept separate:

**Well established:** Dalton was detained, condemned and killed by members of his own organisation.

**Strongly alleged:** senior leaders approved or directly participated in the execution.

**Still unresolved:** the complete decision-making process, the identity of every participant, the exact disposal route of the bodies and whether recoverable remains survive.

The case matters because it challenges a politically convenient division of memory in which state forces committed atrocities while revolutionary organisations are remembered only as victims or liberators. El Salvador’s Truth Commission attributed the overwhelming majority of serious wartime abuses to state agents and associated forces, but that does not erase killings committed inside the armed left. Dalton’s fate remains a warning that clandestine movements can reproduce secrecy, fabricated accusations and impunity within their own ranks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/news/daily-comment/archbishop-oscar-romero-becomes-a-saint-but-his-death-still-haunts-el-salvador" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on newyorker.com.</span></span></span>

## Romero’s assassination: solved in outline, unpunished in court

Archbishop Óscar Romero was shot on 24 March 1980 while celebrating Mass in a hospital chapel in San Salvador. The assassination is sometimes called an unsolved murder, but that description needs qualification. The identity of the gunman has never been judicially established beyond dispute, yet extensive investigations have identified the network believed to have organised the attack.

The United Nations-backed Truth Commission concluded that former army major Roberto d’Aubuisson ordered Romero’s assassination and that members of his security and political circle organised the operation. Later reporting and testimony from former captain Álvaro Saravia provided further details about the vehicle, sniper and logistical group involved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/news/daily-comment/archbishop-oscar-romero-becomes-a-saint-but-his-death-still-haunts-el-salvador" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on newyorker.com.</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery concerns the full architecture around the killing. Who selected the gunman? Which military, business or political figures financed and protected the plot? Which officials knew of preparations? Why did domestic institutions repeatedly prevent an effective prosecution?

Contemporary and declassified material shows that foreign officials were collecting information about d’Aubuisson and death-squad activity, although intelligence reports varied in reliability and interpretation. Such documents support scrutiny of what governments knew, but they do not prove more expansive claims that the United States or the Central Intelligence Agency ordered the murder. Evidence of political tolerance, concealment or ignored warnings is not the same as evidence of authorship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP90-00965R000100170065-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[cia.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cia.gov.</span></span></span>

A 1993 amnesty law shielded civil-war perpetrators shortly after the Truth Commission reported its findings. Although El Salvador’s Supreme Court overturned the amnesty in 2016, the Romero case has still not produced a full domestic criminal reckoning. Many central figures died, fled or benefited from decades of delay.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/news/daily-comment/archbishop-oscar-romero-becomes-a-saint-but-his-death-still-haunts-el-salvador" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on newyorker.com.</span></span></span>

Romero’s murder therefore sits between a solved case and an unresolved one. The most credible account of who initiated the crime is well established. What remains missing is the complete evidential chain and a court judgment testing the responsibility of all surviving participants.

## El Mozote: what happened is clear, but the archive remains contested

Between 9 and 13 December 1981, Salvadoran troops killed almost 1,000 civilians in El Mozote and neighbouring communities in Morazán. Many victims were women and children. Soldiers from the Atlacatl Battalion separated residents, interrogated them and carried out systematic executions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dplf.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-DPLF-El-Salvador-El-Mozote-Massacre-43-years-later-why-we-mustnt-give-up-the-fight-for-accountability.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dplf.org">[Due Process of Law Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dplf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Due Process of Law Foundation EL SALVADOR&#x27;S EL MOZOTE MASSACRE: 43 years later,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Due Process of Law Foundation EL SALVADOR&#x27;S EL MOZOTE MASSACRE: 43 years later,</span></span></span>

For years, however, the massacre was publicly treated as uncertain. Journalists Raymond Bonner and Alma Guillermoprieto reported from the area in early 1982, describing bodies, burned buildings and survivor testimony. United States and Salvadoran officials challenged or minimised their reports while military aid to El Salvador was under political scrutiny in Washington.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1993/12/06/the-truth-of-el-mozote" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the truth of el mozote</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the truth of el mozote</span></span></span>

Forensic excavations in the 1990s decisively altered the argument. Investigators recovered the remains of victims, including large numbers of children, from sites such as the church sacristy. Ballistic and skeletal evidence was consistent with the eyewitness descriptions of civilians being confined and shot, rather than killed during a battle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eaaf.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/elsalvador_ar_1992.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eaaf.org">[EAAF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eaaf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">el salvador</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">el salvador</span></span></span>

El Mozote is thus not a genuine “did it happen?” mystery. The denial has been disproved. The remaining questions concern responsibility, documentation and the full number and identities of the dead.

Researchers continue to examine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the orders transmitted through the military command;
* the knowledge of senior civilian and military officials;
* the role of foreign training and military assistance;
* discrepancies among victim lists;
* missing operational records;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.ohchr.org/Documents/Publications/HR_PUB_14_4_Archives_en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ohchr.org">[ohchr.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ohchr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HR PUB 14 4 Archives en</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HR PUB 14 4 Archives en</span></span></span>
* whether institutions deliberately withheld or destroyed evidence.

</div>

The case has repeatedly encountered resistance over access to military and church archives. Investigators have also relied on declassified United States records because significant parts of the documentary story are easier to reconstruct outside El Salvador than within it. A University of Washington archive has assembled batches of declassified material relating to the massacre and the wider war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncronline.org/news/salvadoran-archbishop-refuses-open-records-el-mozote-massacre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncronline.org">[ncronline.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncronline.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ncronline.org.</span></span></span>

After the 1993 amnesty was struck down, a Salvadoran court reopened proceedings against former officers. Judicial upheaval and the removal of the judge overseeing the evidential phase produced further delay. In December 2025, victims’ representatives reported that thirteen former officers had been ordered to stand trial, a major development after decades without a full prosecution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/news/daily-comment/is-el-salvadors-president-trying-to-shut-down-a-hearing-on-the-infamous-el-mozote-massacre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on newyorker.com.</span></span></span>

El Mozote demonstrates how a well-proven event can retain unresolved dimensions. Forensic science settled the basic truth, but missing records, institutional resistance and delayed trials continue to obscure how decisions travelled down the chain of command.

## The uncertain numbers and silenced memory of 1932

In January 1932, a peasant and communist-led uprising in western El Salvador was followed by mass state killing. The repression, commonly known as the 1932 massacre, targeted suspected rebels and rural communities, with Indigenous people particularly vulnerable because clothing, language and place of residence could be treated as signs of disloyalty.

Estimates of the dead vary widely, often ranging from roughly 10,000 to 30,000. The highest figure is frequently repeated as though it were exact, but no complete victim list exists. Records are fragmented, local burials were poorly documented and witnesses lived for decades under military rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/30023895" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

The main historical dispute is not whether mass killing occurred. It is how many people were killed, how centrally the repression was organised and how far victims were selected because they were Indigenous rather than solely because they were suspected of rebellion.

These explanations overlap. The uprising emerged from severe land inequality, falling coffee prices, political repression and organised communist activity. Yet the response also struck communities whose Indigenous identity had already been weakened by land loss and pressure to abandon distinctive language and dress. The killings accelerated a long process in which many people concealed that identity for safety.

The numerical mystery is unlikely to be solved by discovering a single hidden list. Progress depends on comparing municipal records, church registers, military documents, family testimony and local burial evidence. The very absence of a reliable count is historically meaningful: it reflects both the speed of the repression and the success of later silence.

## Joya de Cerén: how did the villagers escape?

Joya de Cerén, in the Zapotitán Valley, preserves a farming settlement buried by volcanic ash around the seventh century. Houses, stored food, tools, gardens and religious buildings survived in exceptional condition, giving archaeologists an unusually intimate view of ordinary life in ancient Central America. UNESCO compares its preservation to Pompeii, although the Salvadoran site tells a markedly different human story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/675/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

For many years, excavators found no human bodies within the settlement. Everyday objects appeared to have been left behind during a sudden departure, yet the inhabitants evidently escaped the destructive phase of the eruption. This raised a compelling question: what warned them in time?

Archaeologists have proposed several possible signals. A preceding earthquake may have alarmed residents. Steam, noise or an initial ash phase may have indicated escalating danger. The arrangement of tools and household items suggests people left during ordinary activity rather than after a planned abandonment. The lack of bodies supports rapid evacuation, but it cannot reveal the precise warning or route taken.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://popular-archaeology.com/article/joya-de-ceren-an-intimate-portrait-of-the-ancient-maya/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: popular-archaeology.com">[Popular Archaeology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">popular-archaeology.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">joya de ceren an intimate portrait of the ancient maya</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">joya de ceren an intimate portrait of the ancient maya</span></span></span>

Human remains reported near the site in 2018 complicated the popular claim that no person connected with the eruption had ever been found, but they did not overturn the central interpretation that the excavated village itself was evacuated. Nor is there evidence for a vanished population or supernatural disappearance. The puzzle is a practical archaeological one: how an ancient community read natural warning signs successfully enough to flee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archaeology.wiki/blog/2018/11/26/first-human-remains-found-in-el-salvadors-joya-de-ceren/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeology.wiki">[archaeology.wiki]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeology.wiki</span><span class="citation-popover-title">first human remains found in el salvadors joya de ceren</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">first human remains found in el salvadors joya de ceren</span></span></span>

## The expressive figures of San Isidro

In 2022, archaeologists working at San Isidro discovered five ceramic human figures deposited near the summit of a pyramid dating to approximately 410–380 BC. The larger figures have movable heads and open mouths. Their faces appear to change expression when seen from different angles, shifting between smiling, fearful and stern appearances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/news/2025/pre-columbian-puppets-indicate-ritual-connections-across-central-america" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pre columbian puppets indicate ritual connections across central america</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pre columbian puppets indicate ritual connections across central america</span></span></span>

No human remains accompanied the deposit, making a straightforward burial interpretation less likely. Researchers have suggested that the figures were arranged in ritual scenes or manipulated like puppets during performances. One smaller figure can be positioned partly within a larger hollow body, perhaps representing birth, emergence or ancestry. These remain interpretations rather than deciphered meanings.

The figures resemble artefacts found elsewhere in Central America, including parts of Guatemala, Nicaragua, Costa Rica and Panama. That pattern challenges older assumptions that communities in what is now El Salvador were culturally isolated during the Preclassic period. It suggests movement of ideas, artistic conventions or ritual practices across considerable distances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/news/2025/pre-columbian-puppets-indicate-ritual-connections-across-central-america" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pre columbian puppets indicate ritual connections across central america</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pre columbian puppets indicate ritual connections across central america</span></span></span>

What story did the figures represent? Were they gods, ancestors, rulers or characters from a now-lost oral tradition? Archaeology can establish their age, materials, placement and regional parallels, but without accompanying writing, their identities may remain unknowable. The responsible conclusion is not that they depict a secret civilisation or coded prophecy. They are evidence of a sophisticated performance tradition whose language and stories have not survived.

## Why El Salvador’s mysteries endure

El Salvador’s historical mysteries have several different causes. Archaeological meaning was lost because oral traditions and ritual contexts did not survive. Political mysteries endured because information was deliberately concealed, witnesses were threatened, courts were blocked and archives remained closed. In the cases of disappeared children, missing records were compounded by altered names and international adoption. In the Dalton and Romero cases, delay allowed participants to die or avoid questioning.

The best evidence also produces different levels of certainty. At Joya de Cerén, several evacuation scenarios remain plausible. At San Isidro, the figures’ function is a carefully argued hypothesis. By contrast, El Mozote is not an open contest between massacre and battle narratives: survivor accounts, documents and forensic work overwhelmingly establish that civilians were systematically killed. Romero’s assassination and Dalton’s death likewise have credible responsibility frameworks even though important details and judicial findings remain absent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1993/12/06/the-truth-of-el-mozote" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the truth of el mozote</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the truth of el mozote</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. Treating documented atrocities as spooky legends weakens the evidence and diminishes victims. Treating every missing detail as proof of a vast conspiracy does the same. El Salvador’s enduring enigmas are most compelling when approached more carefully: identifying what has been demonstrated, stating where the record breaks off and recognising that an unopened archive, an unnamed grave or an unrecovered child may represent not merely a puzzle, but an unfinished obligation.
