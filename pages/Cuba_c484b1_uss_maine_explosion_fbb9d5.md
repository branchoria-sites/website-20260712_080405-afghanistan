## Introduction

On the night of 15 February 1898, the American battleship *USS Maine* exploded while anchored in Havana Harbour, killing 266 crewmen immediately; several more later died from their injuries. The disaster became one of the defining moments in the run-up to the Spanish–American War, but the central mystery has never been resolved beyond doubt: was the ship destroyed by a Spanish mine, or by an accidental internal explosion? More than a century of investigations have produced conflicting conclusions. Today, most naval historians and technical specialists regard an accidental internal coal-bunker fire as the most likely explanation, yet the surviving evidence is incomplete, and the destruction and later disposal of much of the wreck mean absolute certainty is probably impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil/Recent/Article-View/Article/3290776/why-did-the-uss-maine-explode/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil">[usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Why did the USS Maine explode? &gt; The Sextant &gt; Article View</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 7, 2023</span></span></span>

## The Havana Harbour Explosion

The *Maine* had arrived in Havana during Cuba's struggle for independence from Spain. Officially, the ship was present to protect American citizens and interests amid rising tensions, not to take part in the conflict itself. Shortly before 10 p.m., a massive explosion ripped through the forward section of the battleship. Witnesses reported two blasts in quick succession, with the forward ammunition magazines detonating almost instantly after the initial explosion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil/Recent/Article-View/Article/3290776/why-did-the-uss-maine-explode/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil">[usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Why did the USS Maine explode? &gt; The Sextant &gt; Article View</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 7, 2023</span></span></span>

The violence of the explosion destroyed much of the bow, while the stern remained comparatively intact. This uneven pattern of damage became the heart of every later investigation. Investigators sought to answer one crucial question: did an explosion beneath the hull trigger the magazines, or did something inside the ship explode first?

The timing also gave the disaster immense political significance. Relations between the United States and Spain were already strained by events in Cuba, and newspapers rapidly portrayed the sinking as an act of Spanish aggression long before investigators had reached firm conclusions.

## Mine Theory Versus Internal Fire

The mystery has endured because different investigations interpreted the damaged hull in different ways, often using the same physical evidence.

### Why early investigators believed a mine caused the explosion

The first official United States Naval Court of Inquiry, chaired by Captain William T. Sampson in 1898, concluded that an external mine had detonated beneath the ship, causing the magazines to explode. The court deliberately avoided assigning responsibility, but many politicians and newspapers treated the finding as proof that Spain had attacked the vessel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil/Recent/Article-View/Article/3290776/why-did-the-uss-maine-explode/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil">[usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Why did the USS Maine explode? &gt; The Sextant &gt; Article View</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 7, 2023</span></span></span>

A second investigation in 1911 became possible after engineers surrounded the wreck with a cofferdam and exposed much of the hull. That board again concluded that plates in part of the bottom had been forced inward, which it interpreted as evidence of an underwater mine exploding beneath the ship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil/Recent/Article-View/Article/3290776/why-did-the-uss-maine-explode/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil">[usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Why did the USS Maine explode? &gt; The Sextant &gt; Article View</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 7, 2023</span></span></span>

Supporters of the mine theory have argued that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* inward-bent hull plating suggested an external blast;
* eyewitnesses described what sounded like two separate explosions;
* Havana Harbour contained military defences capable of laying mines;
* the first explosion appeared to originate below the forward magazines.

</div>

Even among those who favour this explanation, however, there has never been convincing evidence identifying who, if anyone, planted or detonated a mine.

### Why later studies favoured an accidental coal fire

The strongest challenge to the mine theory came from Admiral Hyman G. Rickover's review, published in 1976. Rather than conducting a new excavation, Rickover assembled naval engineers and explosion specialists to reanalyse photographs, plans and testimony from the earlier investigations.

Their conclusion differed sharply from both official naval inquiries. They found no persuasive structural evidence requiring an external explosion and argued that the damage was fully consistent with an internal blast. The most likely chain of events, they suggested, began with spontaneous combustion in a coal bunker located immediately beside a reserve ammunition magazine. Heat from the slow-burning coal could have passed through the steel bulkhead until the ammunition ignited catastrophically.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1998/april/special-report-what-really-sank-maine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteA Special Report: What Really Sank the Maine? &#124; Naval History Magazine - April 1998 Volume 12 Number 2...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1998</span></span></span>

This explanation was not invented decades later. Even in 1898, some naval ordnance experts pointed out that coal-bunker fires were a recognised hazard aboard warships and questioned whether a mine could have produced the observed damage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/research/publications/documentary-histories/united-states-navy-s/destruction-of-the-m/analysis-of-the-dest-0.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">analysis of the dest 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage CommandAnalysis of the Destruction of the U.S.S. Maine, 2/18/1898...</span></span></span>

Coal fires were particularly plausible because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* bituminous coal could ignite spontaneously if stored under certain conditions;
* *Maine*'s coal bunker lay directly beside sensitive ammunition storage;
* similar coal-bunker fires had occurred aboard other ships of the period;
* an internal magazine explosion would account for the extraordinary destruction of the bow.

</div>

### Why the debate did not end

The debate reopened in 1998 when a computer-modelling study commissioned by *National Geographic* suggested that an external mine might fit the hull damage better than Rickover's analysis had concluded. The study argued that a mine was somewhat more probable than previously believed.

However, the report itself did not establish that a mine definitely exploded, and several engineers—including participants in Rickover's earlier investigation—criticised aspects of its assumptions and modelling. The resulting technical debate demonstrated that reasonable specialists could interpret the surviving evidence differently rather than proving either explanation beyond doubt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1998/april/special-report-what-really-sank-maine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteA Special Report: What Really Sank the Maine? &#124; Naval History Magazine - April 1998 Volume 12 Number 2...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1998</span></span></span>

## Why certainty is probably impossible

One reason the mystery persists is that much of the physical evidence no longer exists.

After the 1911 investigation, the damaged vessel was refloated, towed out to sea and deliberately scuttled in deep water. Although this allowed Havana Harbour to be cleared, it also prevented future investigators from applying modern forensic techniques directly to the wreck. Today's analyses rely largely on photographs, engineering drawings, witness testimony and reports produced more than a century ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil/Recent/Article-View/Article/3290776/why-did-the-uss-maine-explode/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil">[usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usnhistory.navylive.dodlive.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Why did the USS Maine explode? &gt; The Sextant &gt; Article View</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 7, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 7, 2023</span></span></span>

Historians therefore face several unavoidable limitations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the explosion itself destroyed much of the critical evidence;
* investigators in 1898 and 1911 worked with the scientific knowledge available at the time;
* later analysts had to interpret photographs rather than inspect the original structure;
* the remaining wreck lies at great depth after its deliberate sinking.

</div>

Because the surviving record is incomplete, neither the mine theory nor the internal-fire theory can be tested conclusively.

## How the Disaster Helped Build a War Narrative

Although the technical mystery remains unresolved, the political consequences are much clearer.

Sensational newspapers popularised the slogan "Remember the Maine!" and presented the explosion as evidence of Spanish hostility, often before official investigations had been completed. Public outrage increased pressure on political leaders already debating intervention in Cuba. While the destruction of the *Maine* was not the sole cause of the Spanish–American War, it became its most emotionally powerful symbol.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1998/april/special-report-what-really-sank-maine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteA Special Report: What Really Sank the Maine? &#124; Naval History Magazine - April 1998 Volume 12 Number 2...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1998</span></span></span>

Modern historians generally see the war as the product of several interacting forces, including humanitarian concern over Cuba's conflict, American strategic interests, expansionist politics and deteriorating relations with Spain. The explosion acted less as a single cause than as the event that transformed existing tensions into overwhelming public demand for action.

This distinction matters because even if the *Maine* was destroyed accidentally, the disaster still reshaped international history. A mistaken interpretation of a tragedy can have consequences every bit as significant as a deliberate attack.

## What is the strongest explanation today?

The broad balance of modern technical opinion favours an accidental internal explosion initiated by a coal-bunker fire. Rickover's review remains highly influential, and many naval historians regard it as the most persuasive engineering explanation currently available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/content/history/nhhc/news-and-events/news/2023/nhm-111423.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Naval History and Heritage Command Navy History Matters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage Command Navy History Matters</span></span></span>

Nevertheless, describing the case as "solved" would overstate the evidence. Computer modelling has shown that some aspects of the damage can still be interpreted differently, and the loss of the original wreck means decisive proof is unlikely ever to emerge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1998/april/special-report-what-really-sank-maine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteA Special Report: What Really Sank the Maine? &#124; Naval History Magazine - April 1998 Volume 12 Number 2...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1998</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery is therefore not simply whether a mine or a coal fire destroyed the *Maine*. It is also a lesson in how incomplete forensic evidence, political urgency and public emotion can combine to create a historical controversy that survives long after the guns have fallen silent.
