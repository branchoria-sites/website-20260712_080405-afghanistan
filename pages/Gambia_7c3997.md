## Introduction

The evidence supports different answers in each case. The stone circles are unquestionably ancient burial monuments, although their builders’ identities and changing rituals remain uncertain. Kunta Kinte may represent a genuine strand of family memory, but Haley’s precise reconstruction cannot be independently verified. The [Ninki Nanka]({{ 'ninki-nanka/' | relative_url }}) belongs to folklore rather than zoology, yet its stories reveal real attitudes towards dangerous landscapes, spiritual power and environmental boundaries. Together, these cases show how Gambian history survives through monuments, archives, memories and stories that do not always fit neatly together.

## Who built the stone circles?

The stone circles of Senegambia form one of Africa’s largest and most remarkable monumental landscapes. More than 1,000 circles and related burial mounds extend through a band roughly 350 kilometres long and 100 kilometres wide along the River Gambia. The UNESCO World Heritage property includes four major groups: Wassu and Kerbatch in The Gambia, and Sine Ngayène and Wanar in Senegal. Within those four groups alone are 93 circles and numerous burial mounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1226/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The work established that the single burials appeared to precede in time the multiple...Read more...</span></span></span>

The monuments were made from carefully shaped blocks of laterite, an iron-rich stone common in the region. Some pillars rise to about two metres and can weigh several tonnes. Their remarkably regular cylindrical or polygonal forms show that the builders understood quarrying, stone-working and the organisation of substantial communal labour. UNESCO describes the wider landscape as the product of a prosperous and highly organised society, or succession of societies, rather than the work of an isolated or primitive population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6063/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreWassu Stone Circles Quarry SiteThe Stone Circles of the Senegambia was inscribed on the World Heritage List i...</span></span></span>

### What archaeology has established

Excavations have removed much of the basic mystery surrounding the circles’ function. They are closely associated with graves, burial mounds and funerary deposits. At some sites, archaeologists found that individual burials preceded later multiple burials, suggesting that certain monuments developed over time rather than being created in a single ceremony. The circles therefore appear to have formed part of extensive cemeteries in which communities repeatedly commemorated their dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1226/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The work established that the single burials appeared to precede in time the multiple...Read more...</span></span></span>

Dating remains complicated because the monumental tradition lasted for centuries and not every circle was built at the same time. UNESCO gives a broad range extending from the first millennium BC into the second millennium AD, while more recent archaeological work has sought to distinguish shorter regional phases within that long span. The safest conclusion is that the sites represent a changing funerary tradition rather than one moment, dynasty or migration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/122226" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDocument - Stone Circles of Senegambia (UNESCO/NHK)Description The site consists of four large groups of ston...</span></span></span>

The presence of graves does not answer every question. Archaeologists still debate exactly how bodies were selected, moved or reburied; whether particular circles belonged to families, political communities or ritual specialists; and how the monuments related to nearby settlements. The identity of the builders cannot simply be assigned to a present-day ethnic group, because the archaeological record does not preserve names and the region’s populations and identities changed over many centuries.

### Why the “African Stonehenge” idea misleads

Modern descriptions sometimes compare Wassu with Stonehenge. The resemblance is superficial: both involve upright stones arranged in circles. There is no evidence that the Gambian monuments were derived from Britain, built by wandering Europeans or designed primarily as astronomical observatories. Their consistent association with human remains and burial mounds makes a funerary interpretation much stronger than theories centred on star-watching or lost civilisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1226/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The work established that the single burials appeared to precede in time the multiple...Read more...</span></span></span>

The genuine mystery is therefore not what the circles were in the broadest sense. They were places of burial and remembrance. What remains unresolved is how their ceremonies worked, why communities invested so much labour in them and what social authority allowed the tradition to continue across such a large area.

That uncertainty matters because the circles contradict older colonial assumptions that precolonial Senegambia lacked complex political and ceremonial organisation. Even without knowing the builders’ names, the stones preserve evidence of skilled production, collective planning and long-term ideas about ancestry and sacred space.

## Was Kunta Kinte a documented person?

The best-known Gambian historical puzzle began outside the country. In *Roots*, published in 1976, the American writer Alex Haley traced his maternal family back through slavery to a young man called Kunta Kinte, whom he said had been taken from Juffureh on the River Gambia in the 1760s.

Haley’s investigation combined stories remembered by his American relatives, linguistic clues, shipping material and testimony gathered in The Gambia. He interpreted the remembered expression “Kamby Bolongo” as a reference to the River Gambia and connected the family name “Kin-tay” with the Kinte name found in the region. In Juffureh, an oral informant, Kebba Kanga Fofana, recounted a Kinte genealogy that included a young Kunta who disappeared while gathering wood “about the time the King’s soldiers came”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1977/02/14/sources" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker Sources</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker Sources</span></span></span>

Haley then identified the slave ship *Lord Ligonier*, which sailed from the River Gambia and arrived in Maryland in 1767. A contemporary advertisement confirms that the vessel brought enslaved Africans for sale at Annapolis. These records establish that the ship and voyage were real. They do not, however, include a passenger list naming Kunta Kinte.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1977/02/14/sources" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker Sources</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker Sources</span></span></span>

### Why historians challenged the identification

Historian Donald Wright examined Haley’s Gambian research and argued that the oral testimony could not support such a precise genealogy. One problem was the possibility that Fofana had already heard information about Haley’s search before giving his account. Another was that he may not have been a hereditary specialist capable of reciting an unchanged family history reaching back two centuries. Wright’s broader warning was that an “encyclopaedic informant” who seems able to answer every question may be drawing on recent conversations, public knowledge or an effort to satisfy the questioner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/uprooting-kunta-kinte-on-the-perils-of-relying-on-encyclopedic-informants/D30AC97A9019B1698E82919E69D60289" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Oral traditions are not automatically unreliable. In societies where written records were limited or controlled by colonial institutions, spoken histories can preserve valuable knowledge of families, migrations and political events. The difficulty is establishing when a particular version was formed and whether later information has entered the story. In Haley’s case, there is no securely dated record showing that the Juffureh account existed in its reported form before his investigation.

The American side of the genealogy also contains uncertainties. Haley himself described much of *Roots* as a mixture of research and novelisation, with invented dialogue and reconstructed incidents. Contemporary reviewers recognised that the historical setting was researched but that the relationship between that setting and the detailed life story of Kunta Kinte required imaginative reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1977/02/14/sources" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker Sources</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker Sources</span></span></span>

The most defensible verdict is therefore careful rather than absolute:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A ship called the *Lord Ligonier* carried enslaved people from the River Gambia to Maryland in 1767.
* Kinte was and remains a genuine family name in the region.
* Haley’s family preserved traditions of an African ancestor whose name sounded similar.
* The surviving evidence does not prove that the ancestor was the same individual described to Haley in Juffureh.
* It is also impossible to prove that no such person existed.

### Why the story still matters

The historical doubts did not erase the cultural force of *Roots*. Haley’s work transformed Juffureh and the surrounding river landscape into major places of memory for the African diaspora. Visitors travelled to The Gambia not simply to inspect ruins but to imagine a personal return across a historical rupture created by slavery. Anthropological research has shown how the *Roots* narrative became intertwined with tourism, local identity and public commemoration in Juffureh and nearby Albreda.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.openedition.org/etudesafricaines/18780" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.openedition.org">[OpenEdition Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.openedition.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on openedition.org.</span></span></span>

Kunta Kinteh Island, formerly James Island, has an independently documented history extending beyond Haley’s genealogy. Together with associated sites along the river, it preserves evidence of European-African trade, colonial competition, enslavement and later attempts to suppress the Atlantic slave trade. Its importance does not depend on proving that Kunta Kinte passed through it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark%3A/48223/pf0000371435" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesdoc.unesco.org">[UNESCO Digital Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesdoc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The enduring puzzle is therefore partly about historical method. How much certainty can be recovered when enslavement deliberately stripped people of names, families and origins? Haley may have claimed more precision than the evidence permits, but the desire that drove his investigation arose from a genuine archival absence. Millions of descendants of enslaved Africans cannot trace an individual ancestor to a named African community because the records that survive usually describe people as cargo, numbers or property.

## What was the Ninki Nanka?

The Ninki Nanka occupies a different category from the stone circles and the Kunta Kinte debate. It is not an unresolved archaeological discovery or a missing person who might one day be identified. It is a legendary being associated with rivers, swamps, creeks, forests and other places considered dangerous or spiritually powerful.

Accounts vary considerably. The creature has been described as a giant crowned serpent, a dragon-like animal, a crocodilian being or a shape combining features of several animals. Seeing it was sometimes said to cause sickness or death. Such variation is typical of living folklore: there is no single authoritative description against which every account can be measured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/AB4AAAD4DE388EDACB3308F65F63C575/9781782048701c3_p84-110_CBO.pdf/power_of_the_wild_spirits.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resolve.cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resolve.cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">power of the wild spirits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">power of the wild spirits</span></span></span>

An early written reference appears in a British colonial report from 1906, which recorded Mandinka belief in a gigantic crowned serpent living in dense bush. The report is evidence that the tradition existed by the early twentieth century, but it is not neutral testimony. Colonial officials often compressed complex local beliefs into labels such as superstition or fear, and their accounts were shaped by translation and unequal power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libsysdigi.library.illinois.edu/ilharvest/africana/books2011-05/466568/466568_1906/466568_1906_opt.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libsysdigi.library.illinois.edu">[Libsys Digi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libsysdigi.library.illinois.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">466568 1906 opt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">466568 1906 opt</span></span></span>

In 2006, visiting cryptozoological investigators attempted to collect reports of the creature. They produced no physical evidence of an unknown large animal. Later retellings sometimes present that expedition as if it brought science close to discovering a surviving dragon, but witness stories with inconsistent descriptions cannot establish the existence of a new species.

Treating the Ninki Nanka only as a failed zoological claim misses its historical significance. Beliefs about spirits inhabiting water, trees, rocks and swamps were connected with land use, danger and moral conduct across the Gambia River basin. Research reported from Gambian communities has suggested that places associated with the creature could retain unusually dense vegetation because people were reluctant to disturb them. In that sense, fear of the Ninki Nanka may sometimes have acted as an informal form of environmental protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/AB4AAAD4DE388EDACB3308F65F63C575/9781782048701c3_p84-110_CBO.pdf/power_of_the_wild_spirits.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resolve.cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resolve.cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">power of the wild spirits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">power of the wild spirits</span></span></span>

The legend has also entered modern heritage and tourism. A river route bearing the creature’s name uses the story to introduce visitors to inland communities, landscapes and traditions beyond the coastal resorts. That transformation does not make the being zoologically real; it shows how folklore can acquire new meanings as cultural heritage, environmental narrative and economic resource.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tuicarefoundation.com/projekt/tui-colourful-cultures-gambia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuicarefoundation.com">[TUI Care Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuicarefoundation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tuicarefoundation.com.</span></span></span>

## Where documented uncertainty ends

The Gambia’s best-known mysteries differ sharply in evidential strength, and treating them alike would create more confusion than insight.

The stone circles are physical monuments whose broad purpose is understood, but whose builders, chronology and detailed ceremonies remain partly unknown. Further excavation, dating and settlement research may answer some of those questions.

The Kunta Kinte case is a disputed genealogical identification. Shipping records and family traditions provide genuine historical anchors, but the link between a particular man in eighteenth-century Juffureh and Haley’s American ancestor has not been demonstrated to normal historical standards. New archival discoveries could refine the story, though a conclusive individual identification remains unlikely.

The Ninki Nanka is best understood as folklore. Its importance lies not in the possibility of capturing a hidden animal but in what its stories reveal about landscape, fear, spiritual belief and cultural memory.

Each case also warns against a different kind of mythmaking. The stone circles should not be handed to imagined outsiders simply because their builders are unnamed. A powerful family narrative should not be treated as proven genealogy merely because it answers an emotional need. A river spirit should not be stripped from its cultural setting and repackaged as an undiscovered dinosaur.

## Why these mysteries endure

All three mysteries survive because they stand where different forms of evidence meet. Archaeology records human action without preserving every name. Oral history preserves names and meanings without always preserving dates or independent verification. Colonial archives record ships, officials and commerce while often silencing the people most affected. Folklore preserves relationships with landscape through symbols that cannot be tested like ordinary eyewitness claims.

The Gambia’s unresolved past is therefore not simply a collection of unanswered riddles. It is a demonstration of how history is made from unequal remains. The stones at Wassu and Kerbatch reveal organised societies whose identities have been lost. The Kunta Kinte controversy exposes the damage done when slavery separated people from recoverable ancestry. The Ninki Nanka shows how stories can preserve social and environmental knowledge even when their supernatural surface is not taken literally.

What remains mysterious is important, but so are the limits of the mystery. The strongest understanding comes from preserving uncertainty without turning it into conspiracy, and from recognising that a story can carry profound historical meaning even when not every part of it can be proved.
