## Introduction

Some of these puzzles may eventually be solved by excavation, archival research or the rediscovery of lost objects. Others are probably beyond recovery because the decisive evidence was burned, looted or politically altered. The sensible approach is therefore to separate what contemporary records and physical remains establish from later legend. China’s historical mysteries matter not merely because they are strange, but because each exposes a larger problem: how knowledge survives political upheaval, how official history is made, and how easily an apparently familiar past can become uncertain.

## The fossils that disappeared in wartime

The disappearance of the original Peking Man fossils is among the clearest unsolved cases in the history of archaeology. Excavated at Zhoukoudian, near Beijing, during the 1920s and 1930s, the collection included skull fragments, teeth, jaws and other remains belonging to numerous early humans. Scientists regarded them as exceptionally important evidence for human evolution in eastern Asia.

By 1941, Japanese forces controlled the area around Beijing and the fossils were considered vulnerable. They were packed into two wooden footlockers, reportedly with the intention that United States Marines would carry them to the coastal city of Qinhuangdao. From there, they were meant to travel by ship to safety in the United States. The crates left the Peking Union Medical College in early December, shortly before Japan attacked American and British territories across the Pacific. They were never seen again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peking_Man" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Peking Man</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Peking Man</span></span></span>

### What is firmly established

The fossils existed, were extensively studied and were prepared for removal. Fortunately, palaeontologist Franz Weidenreich and his colleagues had produced detailed measurements, drawings and casts before the originals vanished. Those records preserved much of their scientific value, although modern techniques such as genetic analysis and advanced chemical testing cannot be applied to plaster replicas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/disappearing-pod/parking-lot-or-peking-lot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencehistory.org">[Science History Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencehistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">parking lot or peking lot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science History InstituteParking Lot or Peking Lot?6 Apr 2021 — But all that remains of Peking Man are some plaster casts and drawings. The weakest point in the story is the transfer itself. Later accounts often assume that American personnel formally received the crates, but a study of surviving archival documents found no secure evidence that the United States military ever took possession of them. That raises the possibility that the fossils disappeared before reaching the Marines, rather than during an organised coastal evacuation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://paleoanthropology.org/ojs/index.php/paleo/article/view/72" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paleoanthropology.org">[PaleoAnthropology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paleoanthropology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archival Documents and the Search for Peking Man5 Nov 2021 — The Peking Man skeletal materials, excavated in China in th...</span></span></span>

Several explanations remain plausible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **They were buried near Qinhuangdao.** A former Marine later recalled finding a box of bones there in 1947 and reburying it during fighting. Investigators identified a possible location, but subsequent construction left part of the area beneath buildings or a car park.
* **They were seized by Japanese troops.** The timing makes confiscation possible, although no reliable Japanese inventory or post-war discovery confirms it.
* **They were lost at sea.** Stories have attached them to various sunken ships, but none has been supported by a documented chain of custody.
* **They never left Beijing.** If the official handover failed, they may have been hidden, stolen or destroyed locally.

</div>

The buried-box account is more specific than most rumours, but it still depends on a memory recorded decades after the event. The most responsible conclusion is that the fossils vanished during a poorly documented movement amid military collapse. Their survival is possible, but there is no verified evidence that they remain intact.

## Did the Jianwen Emperor die in the palace fire?

In 1402, rebel forces entered the Ming capital of Nanjing after a civil war between the Jianwen Emperor and his uncle, the Prince of Yan. The imperial palace burned, Jianwen disappeared and his uncle soon took the throne as the Yongle Emperor. The official position was that Jianwen had died in the fire. Yet uncertainty over the identification of the remains allowed another story to grow: that the defeated emperor escaped, disguised himself as a Buddhist monk and lived secretly for many years.

The political setting made suspicion inevitable. Jianwen had tried to reduce the power of imperial princes, including the uncle who eventually overthrew him. Yongle’s claim to the throne therefore depended on presenting his rebellion as a restoration of legitimate government rather than a successful usurpation. He abolished many of his nephew’s measures, punished Jianwen’s supporters and reshaped the public record of the previous reign.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jianwen_Emperor" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jianwen Emperor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jianwen Emperor</span></span></span>

Accounts state that bodies found in the burned palace were identified as the emperor, his wife and their eldest son. The problem is that the victorious regime controlled both the identification and its official presentation. No independently verifiable remains survive, and contemporary documentation cannot establish beyond doubt whether Jianwen was among the dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jianwen_Emperor" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jianwen Emperor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jianwen Emperor</span></span></span>

Stories of an imperial escape became prominent in later unofficial histories. In these versions, Jianwen fled through a hidden passage, entered religious life and wandered through southern or south-western China. Some traditions even claim that Yongle’s maritime expeditions or later searches for monks were partly intended to locate him. These details are memorable but poorly evidenced. Scholars examining Jianwen’s changing image have shown how the vanished emperor became a political and literary figure whose supposed survival allowed later writers to question Yongle’s legitimacy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newhistory.org.tw/contents/2022032511341111219/165088997971265%3Bjsessionid%3D288A9B744EA840BDF9EC1ECB8C5D729A" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newhistory.org.tw">[newhistory.org.tw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newhistory.org.tw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transforming the Jianwen Emperor in Ming Dynasty ChinaThe Emperor&#x27;s Returning Ghost: Transforming the Jianwen Emperor in Ming Dynasty China...</span></span></span>

Death in the palace fire remains the simplest explanation. A defeated ruler trapped during the violent capture of his capital had little chance of arranging a successful lifelong disappearance. Yet the escape story cannot be conclusively disproved because the victor controlled the records and no securely identified body can now be tested. The enduring mystery is therefore not just where Jianwen went. It is whether a regime born from civil war can be trusted to provide the final account of the man it displaced.

## What lies inside the First Emperor’s tomb?

The terracotta warriors discovered near Xi’an in 1974 are not the tomb of China’s first emperor itself. They occupy only part of a much larger burial landscape containing hundreds of pits, tombs, buildings and other remains. At its centre stands the great mound covering the burial chamber of Qin Shi Huang, who unified China under imperial rule and died in 210 BC. That central chamber has not been opened.

An account written roughly a century after the emperor’s death describes an underground palace containing models of towers, precious objects, mechanical crossbows and rivers of mercury flowing through a miniature landscape. It also says that the ceiling represented the heavens. Such writing cannot be treated as an architectural plan, but soil investigations have detected unusually high mercury levels around the mound, giving at least one part of the ancient description some physical support.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/emperor-qin" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalgeographic.com.</span></span></span>

The wider site is already enormous. UNESCO describes nearly 200 accompanying pits and more than 600 recorded locations across a protected area of over 56 square kilometres. These include life-sized soldiers, horses, bronze chariots, weapons, burials and architectural remains. The scale suggests that the mound concealed not a simple grave but a constructed imperial world intended to preserve the emperor’s authority after death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/441/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The chamber remains sealed mainly for conservation reasons. Excavation is irreversible, and the terracotta figures provide a warning: their painted surfaces often deteriorated rapidly after exposure to air. Opening the emperor’s tomb before reliable methods exist for stabilising its contents could destroy organic materials, pigments and other evidence within hours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/magazine/article/terra-cotta-warriors" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalgeographic.com.</span></span></span>

Three questions therefore remain unanswered:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Does the chamber match the ancient written description?
* Was it entered or looted in antiquity?
* Is the emperor’s body, coffin or funerary equipment still preserved?

</div>

Claims of deadly traps or vast untouched treasure should be treated cautiously. Mechanical weapons are historically possible, but there is no direct observation of them inside the chamber. Mercury is measurable in the surrounding soil, yet that does not prove the existence of flowing rivers. This mystery survives by deliberate choice: archaeologists know approximately where the answers lie, but opening the tomb without damaging them remains the greater challenge.

## Why were Sanxingdui’s treasures burned and buried?

Sanxingdui, in Sichuan province, transformed modern understanding of Bronze Age China. Excavations revealed monumental bronze masks, gold objects, jade, ivory, ritual vessels and figures with visual forms unlike the better-known material traditions of northern China. The objects are associated with an early society in the region traditionally linked with the ancient kingdom of Shu, which left comparatively few written records of its own.

Two extraordinary pits were excavated in 1986. Six more were discovered during renewed investigations beginning in 2020. Research published after the newer excavations reports that more than 17,000 objects or fragments were recovered, including bronze, jade, gold, ivory and traces of silk. The deposits show evidence of burning, deliberate breakage and carefully organised burial. Archaeologists broadly interpret the pits as ritual rather than ordinary rubbish deposits, although the ceremonies involved are still debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/trove-of-13000-artifacts-sheds-light-on-enigmatic-chinese-civilization-180980254/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">trove of 13000 artifacts sheds light on enigmatic chinese civilization 180980254</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">trove of 13000 artifacts sheds light on enigmatic chinese civilization 180980254</span></span></span>

The central puzzle is not whether Sanxingdui possessed an advanced society; its walls, workshops and sophisticated metalwork make that clear. The mystery is why so many valuable sacred objects were damaged and deposited within a relatively short period, and why the site’s political importance subsequently declined.

Possible explanations include:

* **A major religious transformation.** Old sacred objects may have been ceremonially retired when rituals or rulers changed.
* **Political defeat or internal conflict.** A conquering group or rival faction might have destroyed objects associated with an earlier authority.
* **The relocation of the political centre.** Later cultural activity at Jinsha, near modern Chengdu, shares important features with Sanxingdui, suggesting movement and continuity rather than the disappearance of an entire people.
* **Flooding or environmental disruption.** Geological and settlement research has strengthened the argument that serious flood hazards influenced changes across the Chengdu Plain. A 2024 study based on evidence from Jinsha supported flooding as an important factor in the decline of Sanxingdui, though environmental pressure alone does not explain the ritual treatment of the objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0277379124000027" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

The popular description of Sanxingdui as a civilisation that suddenly “vanished” is therefore misleading. Its political centre may have shifted, and its population and traditions may have continued elsewhere. The burned treasures still require explanation, but there is no need to invoke visitors from outside history, much less the extraterrestrial claims sometimes attached to the elongated eyes and unfamiliar faces of the bronzes. Their unusual appearance demonstrates cultural diversity within ancient China, not evidence of a non-human origin.

Sanxingdui remains mysterious because the material record is rich while the written record is sparse. Each new excavation adds thousands of details, but objects cannot directly tell historians who ordered their burial, what words accompanied the ceremonies or whether the participants believed they were ending an era.

## How did most of the Yongle Encyclopedia disappear?

Completed in 1408, the Yongle Encyclopedia was one of the largest scholarly compilations produced in the premodern world. Created by thousands of scholars under the Yongle Emperor, it assembled extracts from earlier works covering history, literature, science, religion, medicine and many other subjects. The finished manuscript comprised 11,095 volumes and was copied by hand rather than printed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/international-collections/2023/04/yongle-encyclopedia-volumes-fully-digitized/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">yongle encyclopedia volumes fully digitized</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">yongle encyclopedia volumes fully digitized</span></span></span>

Only a small percentage survives. The Library of Congress and other institutions preserve scattered volumes, while substantial holdings remain in Chinese collections. Many of the original books from which the encyclopedia quoted have themselves disappeared, meaning every surviving volume can contain fragments of otherwise lost works.

The mystery is not simply that volumes were destroyed. Books of this scale were exceptionally vulnerable to fire, damp, neglect and political violence. The more difficult question is what happened to the main original set. A second manuscript copy was made in the sixteenth century, but the earlier set subsequently vanished from clear view.

One theory holds that it was destroyed in a palace fire during the late Ming period. Another proposes that it was placed in the tomb complex of the Jiajing Emperor, who ordered the production of the duplicate. Searches and historical investigations have not produced decisive proof for either explanation. The surviving copy was itself gradually depleted through theft, official neglect and warfare. Further losses occurred during the nineteenth century, including the violence surrounding foreign military interventions and the Boxer uprising.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongle_Encyclopedia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Yongle Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yongle Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

The dispersed books occasionally reappear in private collections or auctions, creating the possibility that more volumes survive unnoticed. Digitisation projects have made known examples easier to study and compare, but they cannot reconstruct the missing majority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/international-collections/2023/04/yongle-encyclopedia-volumes-fully-digitized/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">yongle encyclopedia volumes fully digitized</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">yongle encyclopedia volumes fully digitized</span></span></span>

Unlike a lost treasure made from gold, the encyclopedia’s greatest value lies in the texts it preserved. Its disappearance may represent the loss of thousands of writings for which no other copy existed. The unresolved fate of the original set also illustrates how a state can command the creation of an immense monument to knowledge without ensuring its long-term survival.

## The disputed final flight of Lin Biao

Lin Biao was one of the most powerful figures in the People’s Republic of China and was formally presented as Mao Zedong’s successor during the Cultural Revolution. On 13 September 1971, an aircraft carrying Lin, his wife, his son and several others crashed in Mongolia. Everyone aboard was killed. Chinese authorities later alleged that Lin had been involved in a plot against Mao and had fled after its failure.

The crash itself is not seriously in doubt. Foreign governments received information confirming that Lin had died in an aircraft accident in Mongolia, and photographs and investigations documented the wreckage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v17/d157" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

What remains disputed is the chain of events that put him on the aircraft. Questions include whether Lin personally approved an assassination plan, whether the supposed plot was primarily the work of his son, whether the family intended to reach the Soviet Union, and whether the departure was a planned escape or a panicked flight during an internal confrontation.

The official account emerged through a political system in which documents were restricted, witnesses were vulnerable and Lin was rapidly transformed from designated successor into condemned traitor. That does not make every allegation false, but it complicates efforts to distinguish evidence from the political campaign that followed his death. Studies of the incident have consequently focused as much on the culture of power around Mao as on the final hours of the flight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/40542338_The_Culture_of_Power_The_Lin_Biao_Incident_in_the_Cultural_Revolution" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate The Lin Biao Incident in the Cultural Revolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate The Lin Biao Incident in the Cultural Revolution</span></span></span>

Speculation that the aircraft was deliberately shot down has not acquired convincing public evidence. Mechanical failure, inadequate fuel, confusion in the cockpit and the pressures of an abrupt departure are more conventional possibilities. The most secure conclusion is that Lin’s political relationship with Mao had broken down and that he died while leaving China. Precisely what he knew, intended or authorised remains obscured by secrecy, conflicting testimony and the political uses made of his death.

## Why these mysteries endure

China’s unresolved historical cases share several recurring features. They often arose at moments when institutions were breaking down: a wartime evacuation, the capture of a capital, a dynastic transition or a struggle within the state. The uncertainty was then amplified by lost documents, controlled archives or later storytelling.

They also show that different kinds of mystery require different standards of judgement. Peking Man is a missing-object case that might be solved by physical recovery. The Jianwen Emperor’s fate depends on compromised historical testimony and may never be settled. The First Emperor’s tomb contains evidence that remains inaccessible for conservation reasons. Sanxingdui is an interpretive archaeological puzzle in which new discoveries continually revise the question. The Yongle Encyclopedia combines destruction with the possibility of future rediscovery, one volume at a time.

None benefits from exaggeration. The strongest explanations are generally grounded in familiar historical forces: war, political rivalry, environmental disruption, ritual change, theft and decay. Yet explaining the circumstances does not erase the mystery. In each case, something decisive is still absent—a body, a crate, an archive, an inscription or an unopened room. That gap is what keeps these episodes alive in China’s public memory, while reminding readers that history is not simply the past itself, but the incomplete evidence that happens to survive.
