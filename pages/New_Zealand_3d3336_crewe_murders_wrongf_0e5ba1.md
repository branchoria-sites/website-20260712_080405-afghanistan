## Introduction

The Crewe murders are one of [New Zealand]({{ 'which-new-zealand-mysteries-still-defy/' | relative_url }})'s most significant unresolved criminal cases because they combine two enduring mysteries in a single story. The first is the unsolved killing of Waikato farming couple Harvey and Jeannette Crewe in 1970. The second is how the investigation led to the wrongful conviction of their neighbour, Arthur Allan Thomas, after police fabricated crucial evidence. Thomas was convicted twice, spent nine years in prison, received a free pardon in 1979, and was later vindicated by a Royal Commission of Inquiry that concluded detectives had planted the cartridge case central to the prosecution. The result is a case in which New Zealand has official confirmation of a major miscarriage of justice, yet no one has ever been convicted of the murders themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/page/crewe-murders" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">crewe murders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crewe murders, 1970 &#124; NZ History...</span></span></span>

## How did the murders lead to a wrongful conviction?

Harvey and Jeannette Crewe lived on a farm near Pukekawa, south of Auckland. On 22 June 1970, relatives discovered that the couple had disappeared while their young daughter, Rochelle, had been left alive in her cot. The discovery immediately suggested that someone had returned to the property after the killings to feed the child, adding another disturbing feature to the case.

Over the following months, Harvey Crewe's body and then Jeannette Crewe's body were recovered from the Waikato River. Both had been shot with a.22 calibre rifle and weighted before being thrown into the water. Despite an extensive investigation, police struggled to establish a clear motive or identify a convincing suspect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/page/crewe-murders" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">crewe murders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crewe murders, 1970 &#124; NZ History...</span></span></span>

Attention eventually focused on neighbouring farmer Arthur Allan Thomas. Prosecutors argued that Thomas had both motive and opportunity, while also relying on forensic evidence said to connect his rifle with the crime scene. Thomas was convicted in 1971, successfully appealed, and was retried in 1973. Despite growing concerns about the police investigation, the second jury also convicted him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/page/arthur-allan-thomas-convicted-crewe-murders-second-time" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Arthur Allan Thomas convicted of Crewe murders – again &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NZHistory Arthur Allan Thomas convicted of Crewe murders – again &#124; NZ History</span></span></span>

Even during the trials, however, critics questioned whether the evidence against Thomas was reliable. Investigative journalists, forensic specialists and defence supporters argued that the prosecution's case contained serious weaknesses. Books by journalists Pat Booth and later David Yallop helped transform what had seemed a settled murder conviction into one of New Zealand's most controversial criminal cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://teara.govt.nz/en/document/26498/the-fate-of-arthur-thomas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: teara.govt.nz">[Te Ara Encyclopedia of New Zealand]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">teara.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Ara Encyclopedia of New ZealandThe fate of Arthur Thomas &#124; Violent crime &#124; Te Ara Encyclopedia of New ZealandMarch 22, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 22, 2011</span></span></span>

## The planted cartridge case

The turning point came with a single spent.22 cartridge case known during the trials as Exhibit 350.

Police claimed the cartridge had been found in the Crewes' garden months after the murders and that microscopic examination showed it had been fired from Thomas's rifle. Because the murder weapon itself was never recovered, the cartridge became one of the strongest pieces of physical evidence linking Thomas to the killings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gg.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2021-06/RC%20135%20Arthur%20Allan%20Thomas.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gg.govt.nz">[Governor-General of New Zealand]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gg.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">RC 135 Arthur Allan Thomas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Governor-General of New ZealandOCT 1981H. 6Report of the Royal Commission...</span></span></span>

The problem was that many observers found the discovery highly unusual. The cartridge had supposedly remained unnoticed through repeated searches before suddenly appearing months later in an area already examined. Questions also arose over its condition and handling.

These doubts eventually led to a formal inquiry. The 1980 Royal Commission reached devastating conclusions. It found that senior detectives had fabricated evidence by firing Thomas's rifle using ammunition taken from his farm, producing the cartridge case and planting it at the Crewe property to implicate him. The Commission described the conduct as "an unspeakable outrage" and rejected the prosecution's suggested motive against Thomas. It also criticised aspects of the second trial that undermined confidence in the conviction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gg.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2021-06/RC%20135%20Arthur%20Allan%20Thomas.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gg.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gg.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">RC 135 Arthur Allan Thomas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Governor-General of New ZealandOCT 1981H. 6Report of the Royal Commission...</span></span></span>

The Commission's findings transformed the case. What had once been viewed as disputed allegations against investigators became an official conclusion that crucial forensic evidence had been deliberately manufactured.

## Pardon, police reviews and the unanswered question

Before the Royal Commission reported, Prime Minister Robert Muldoon had already ordered an independent review by Queen's Counsel Robert Adams-Smith. That review concluded the conviction was unsafe, leading to Thomas receiving a free pardon in December 1979 after spending approximately nine years in prison. He later received financial compensation for the wrongful conviction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gg.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2021-06/RC%20135%20Arthur%20Allan%20Thomas.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gg.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gg.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">RC 135 Arthur Allan Thomas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Governor-General of New ZealandOCT 1981H. 6Report of the Royal Commission...</span></span></span>

The pardon did not solve the murders. Instead, it reopened them.

Decades later, New Zealand Police conducted another comprehensive review. Published in 2014, it accepted that the planted cartridge case was probably the product of police misconduct and acknowledged the conclusions of the Royal Commission. Investigators re-examined alternative suspects, witness statements and forensic material, but concluded there was insufficient evidence to prosecute anyone else. The review accepted that the murders remain officially unsolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.nzherald.co.nz/webcontent/document/pdf/201431/crewe-review-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.nzherald.co.nz">[NZ Herald]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.nzherald.co.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">crewe review final report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New Zealand Police...</span></span></span>

Because of the compromised investigation, later detectives faced a fundamental difficulty. Once key evidence had been fabricated and the original inquiry had focused heavily on the wrong suspect, it became far harder to reconstruct what had actually happened. Witness memories had faded, potential evidence had been lost, and some people of interest had died before renewed investigation became possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.nzherald.co.nz/webcontent/document/pdf/201431/crewe-review-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.nzherald.co.nz">[NZ Herald]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.nzherald.co.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">crewe review final report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New Zealand Police...</span></span></span>

## Why the case remains debated

Unlike many historical mysteries, the principal uncertainty is not whether official wrongdoing occurred. That question has largely been settled by the Royal Commission.

The unresolved issues instead include:

* **Who killed Harvey and Jeannette Crewe?** No alternative suspect has ever been proved responsible beyond reasonable doubt.
* **What was the motive?** Various possibilities involving family relationships, personal disputes and financial matters have been examined over the decades, but none has been conclusively established.
* **Could the murders still be solved?** While periodic reviews have considered advances in forensic science, the passage of time and the compromised original investigation mean the prospects of a definitive prosecution are widely regarded as limited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.nzherald.co.nz/webcontent/document/pdf/201431/crewe-review-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.nzherald.co.nz">[nzherald.co.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.nzherald.co.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">crewe review final report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New Zealand Police...</span></span></span>

The case has therefore become an example of how an investigation can fail in two ways at once: innocent people may be convicted while the true offender remains unidentified.

## Why the Crewe case still matters

The Crewe murders occupy a unique place in New Zealand's legal history because they changed public attitudes towards police accountability and wrongful convictions.

The exposure of fabricated forensic evidence demonstrated that miscarriages of justice can arise not merely from honest mistakes but from deliberate misconduct. The case encouraged greater scrutiny of police investigations, forensic procedures and disclosure obligations in criminal trials. It also became a touchstone in later debates about criminal appeals and the importance of independent review when convictions appear unsafe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://teara.govt.nz/en/document/26498/the-fate-of-arthur-thomas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: teara.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">teara.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Ara Encyclopedia of New ZealandThe fate of Arthur Thomas &#124; Violent crime &#124; Te Ara Encyclopedia of New ZealandMarch 22, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 22, 2011</span></span></span>

Yet the original mystery remains unresolved. Harvey and Jeannette Crewe were murdered, Arthur Allan Thomas was officially cleared, and the identity of the killer has never been established. That combination of an acknowledged miscarriage of justice and an unsolved double homicide is what continues to make the Crewe case one of the most consequential and enduring historical mysteries in New Zealand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/page/crewe-murders" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">crewe murders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crewe murders, 1970 &#124; NZ History...</span></span></span>
