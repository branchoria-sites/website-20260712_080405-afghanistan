## Introduction

Badrulchau is one of [Palau]({{ 'what-do-palaus-greatest-mysteries-reveal/' | relative_url }})'s most important archaeological sites, yet the central question surrounding it remains unresolved: what were its giant stone monuments actually built for? The surviving basalt monoliths on northern Babeldaob clearly represent a major communal building project undertaken by organised Palauan societies. What is uncertain is whether the site primarily functioned as a political meeting place, a ceremonial centre, a symbol of chiefly authority, or some combination of all three. Archaeology has ruled out ideas that the monuments were created by a lost civilisation, but it has not produced enough direct evidence to identify their precise role. Instead, Badrulchau remains an example of a genuine historical mystery in which the surviving stones, oral traditions and archaeological evidence point towards several plausible interpretations rather than a single definitive answer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/3849" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[publications.dainst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ions &#124; Journal of Global ArchaeologyMay 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2022</span></span></span>

## What survives at Badrulchau

Badrulchau lies in the modern state of Ngarchelong on Babeldaob, Palau's largest island. Today the site consists of rows of upright basalt monoliths, several weighing several tonnes, together with associated earthworks and terraces. Some of the stones bear carved human faces, making them among the most distinctive prehistoric monuments in Micronesia. The monument is part of a much larger cultural landscape of engineered hillsides, terraces and earthworks that transformed large areas of Babeldaob during Palau's prehistoric Earthwork Era.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/3849" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[dainst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ions &#124; Journal of Global ArchaeologyMay 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2022</span></span></span>

The site visible today is only a fragment of its original form. The timber structures that may once have stood above the stones have long disappeared in the tropical climate, leaving archaeologists to interpret foundations without the buildings they once supported. This absence of wooden architecture is one of the main reasons the site's purpose remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/3849" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[publications.dainst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ions &#124; Journal of Global ArchaeologyMay 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2022</span></span></span>

Radiocarbon samples recovered beneath parts of the monument have often been cited as dating the site to around AD 161. Archaeologists are more cautious than many popular accounts. The charcoal establishes that activity beneath the monument occurred before the stones were erected, but it does not prove that every monolith was placed at exactly that date. The site could have developed over generations through multiple construction phases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267698045_An_Assessment_of_Radiocarbon_Dates_from_Palau_Western_Micronesia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) An Assessment of Radiocarbon Dates from Palau, Western MicronesiaJanuary 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2005</span></span></span>

## Why do many researchers think it supported a meeting house?

The most widely accepted interpretation is that the monoliths formed the stone supports for an exceptionally large *bai*, the traditional Palauan meeting house used for political decision-making, ceremonies and community gatherings.

This theory is based on several strands of evidence rather than on a single discovery.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* Stone foundations supporting timber meeting houses are known from other traditional Palauan settlements.
* The arrangement of the monoliths resembles the type of structural supports needed for a large timber building.
* Nearby archaeological sites preserve stone platforms associated with historic meeting houses.
* Oral traditions consistently connect Badrulchau with an enormous meeting house unlike any surviving example.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iarii.org/research/megalithic-earthworks-in-palau/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iarii.org">[iarii.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iarii.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on iarii.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

If this interpretation is correct, Badrulchau would represent the largest known ceremonial or political meeting house in ancient Palau. Such a building would have required coordinated labour, specialist planning and considerable authority to organise construction, indicating the presence of complex chiefly leadership long before European contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/3849" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[publications.dainst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ions &#124; Journal of Global ArchaeologyMay 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2022</span></span></span>

However, no preserved roof, wall posts or flooring survive to confirm the exact dimensions of the structure. The meeting-house reconstruction therefore remains a well-supported hypothesis rather than an established fact.

### The role of oral tradition

Palauan oral traditions add another layer to the mystery. One famous story says the meeting house was built overnight by supernatural beings or gods, but daylight arrived before the work was finished, leaving only the stone supports behind. Other versions describe an enormous building capable of holding thousands of people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://palau.co/discover-palau/culture/stone-monoliths/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: palau.co">[Discover Palau]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">palau.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Palau Stone Monoliths &#124; Babeldaob &#124; Discover the Heart of Palau</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Palau Stone Monoliths &#124; Babeldaob &#124; Discover the Heart of Palau</span></span></span>

Modern historians do not treat these stories as literal accounts of construction. Instead, they see them as cultural memories expressing the extraordinary scale and prestige of the monument. Across many societies, unusually ancient structures become associated with divine builders because later generations no longer remember the original human construction. In this sense, the legend reinforces rather than replaces archaeological evidence that Badrulchau was regarded as exceptional.

## Could the site have served more than one purpose?

One reason the mystery has endured is that archaeologists increasingly recognise that monumental sites rarely had only a single function.

Evidence from Babeldaob's wider landscape suggests prehistoric earthworks fulfilled several overlapping roles, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* political authority;
* ceremonial activity;
* elite residence;
* defence;
* burial;
* agricultural management; and
* displays of communal power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/3849" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[dainst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ions &#124; Journal of Global ArchaeologyMay 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2022</span></span></span>

</div>

Badrulchau may therefore have operated as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a ceremonial gathering place for major rituals;
* the seat of powerful chiefs;
* a regional political assembly;
* a monument demonstrating the ability of leaders to organise labour; or
* different combinations of these functions at different periods.

</div>

Rather than choosing one explanation, many researchers now favour a broader interpretation in which architecture, ritual and political authority were closely connected.

## What archaeology can and cannot prove

Modern archaeological work has substantially narrowed the mystery, but important questions remain unanswered.

Researchers can say with confidence that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the monument was built by prehistoric Palauan communities rather than by an unknown civilisation;
* moving and erecting the stones required organised labour and sophisticated planning;
* Badrulchau formed part of the wider monumental landscape that characterises ancient Babeldaob; and
* the site belongs within Palau's long sequence of earthwork construction rather than standing as an isolated monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/3849" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[dainst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ions &#124; Journal of Global ArchaeologyMay 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2022</span></span></span>

</div>

Archaeology cannot yet demonstrate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the exact date every stone was erected;
* whether all of the monoliths belong to a single construction phase;
* the appearance or dimensions of the timber structure, if one existed;
* whether ceremonial, political or residential functions dominated the site's use; or
* how long the monument remained in active use before being abandoned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/3849" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[dainst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ions &#124; Journal of Global ArchaeologyMay 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2022</span></span></span>

</div>

The missing evidence is largely a consequence of preservation. Tropical climates rapidly destroy timber, roofing and organic materials, leaving only the stone foundations and earthworks.

## Why Badrulchau still matters

Badrulchau occupies a central place in understanding prehistoric Palau because it demonstrates that the island supported societies capable of organising large-scale engineering projects centuries before European arrival. The monument also shows how archaeology and oral tradition can complement each other without necessarily providing identical answers.

Its enduring mystery is not about whether people built the monument—they certainly did—but about how ancient Palauan communities combined architecture, leadership, ceremony and collective identity within this remarkable place. As new mapping techniques, geoarchaeological studies and landscape surveys continue across Babeldaob, Badrulchau remains one of the strongest pieces of evidence that Palau's monumental earthwork culture was both technically sophisticated and socially complex, even if the precise purpose of its giant stones cannot yet be proved beyond debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.dainst.org/journals/joga/article/view/3849" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.dainst.org">[dainst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.dainst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ions &#124; Journal of Global ArchaeologyMay 17, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2022</span></span></span>
