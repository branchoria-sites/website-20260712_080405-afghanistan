## Introduction

One of the least resolved chapters in [Eswatini]({{ 'what-remains-unsolved-in-eswatinis-past/' | relative_url }})'s modern history concerns the covert killings carried out during the final decades of apartheid. From the late 1970s into the late 1980s, South African security forces repeatedly crossed into the then Kingdom of Swaziland to monitor, abduct and kill members of the African National Congress (ANC), its armed wing Umkhonto we Sizwe (MK), and suspected supporters. The broad pattern is no longer in doubt. South Africa's own Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC), amnesty hearings and later archival releases established that cross-border operations were authorised and systematically conducted. What remains uncertain is who authorised particular killings, which victims were deliberately targeted, and how much evidence disappeared with missing or destroyed security records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ocj.gov.za/trc/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ocj.gov.za">[ocj.gov.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ocj.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Chief Justice Truth and Reconciliation Commission</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Chief Justice Truth and Reconciliation Commission</span></span></span>

The mystery is therefore not whether covert violence occurred. It lies in the incomplete documentary trail. Some former operatives confessed in detail, while others denied involvement or never applied for amnesty. Important intelligence files were destroyed before South Africa's democratic transition or were never located, leaving investigators to reconstruct events from fragmentary testimony, surviving operational documents and witness accounts.

## How South African security operations crossed into Eswatini

Eswatini occupied a strategically important position during the apartheid years. It bordered eastern South Africa and became a route through which ANC members, couriers and recruits moved between exile and underground networks. This made the country a priority target for South African military intelligence, Security Branch police and special forces.

Operations inside Eswatini took several forms:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Surveillance of ANC safe houses and suspected supporters.
* Recruitment of informers and double agents.
* Cross-border kidnappings and forced returns to South Africa.
* Targeted shootings and bomb attacks.
* Ambushes against guerrillas attempting to cross the frontier.

</div>

The violence was often designed to appear deniable. Killers frequently operated in civilian clothing, crossed the border secretly and withdrew before local authorities could respond. Some attacks occurred inside Eswatini itself, while others involved luring activists towards the border before arresting or killing them. The TRC later concluded that these operations formed part of a broader regional campaign of destabilisation extending across neighbouring states.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sabctrc.saha.org.za/reports/volume2/chapter2/subsection36.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sabctrc.saha.org.za">[saha.org.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sabctrc.saha.org.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Truth Commission</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Report - TRC Final Report - Volume 2, Section 1, Chapter...</span></span></span>

A notable example occurred on 4 June 1980, when bomb attacks on houses in Manzini killed MK member Patrick Makau and seven-year-old Patrick Nkosi, the son of an ANC activist. The attacks demonstrated that civilian homes within Eswatini had become operational targets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sabctrc.saha.org.za/reports/volume2/chapter2/subsection25.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sabctrc.saha.org.za">[sabctrc.saha.org.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sabctrc.saha.org.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Truth Commission</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Report - TRC Final Report - Volume 2, Section 1, Chapter...</span></span></span>

## What later confessions and investigations established

The end of apartheid revealed far more than had previously been known, but not everything.

The South African TRC heard extensive evidence about operations in Swaziland. Former members of the Security Branch, Vlakplaas counter-insurgency unit and military structures described cross-border raids, assassination plans and covert infiltration. Amnesty applications acknowledged participation in several killings and ambushes, particularly those intended to stop MK fighters entering South Africa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/media/1999/9907/p990729b.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TRUTH AND RECONCILIATION COMMISSIONJuly 29, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 29, 1999</span></span></span>

The Commission also found that some operations had been authorised at the highest levels of the apartheid state. In one important finding concerning a December 1986 operation in Swaziland, it concluded that the unlawful killings and abductions had been approved by senior government authorities and carried out by South African Defence Force Special Forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/report/finalreport/volume%202.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Justice VOLUME TWO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truth and Reconciliation Commission of...</span></span></span>

The South African History Archive has since assembled large collections of TRC material relating specifically to Swaziland. These records include amnesty applications, testimony from perpetrators, victim statements and official correspondence, confirming that cross-border assassinations formed part of an organised security strategy rather than isolated incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.saha.org.za/collections/AL2878/a02205.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saha.org.za">[saha.org.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saha.org.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">South African History Archive - Cross-border Activities of the Apartheid Regime: Swaziland...</span></span></span>

Yet even these admissions were incomplete. Amnesty depended upon full disclosure, but many suspected participants never applied, while others denied knowledge or claimed poor memory. Some applications covered only particular operations, leaving investigators unable to connect individual attacks into a complete chain of command.

## Why orders and chains of command remain uncertain

The central unresolved question is not whether killings occurred but who ordered each specific operation.

Several factors continue to frustrate historians and investigators.

**Destroyed records.** During South Africa's democratic transition, significant quantities of security and intelligence files were deliberately destroyed. The TRC repeatedly complained that missing documentation prevented investigators from reconstructing operational planning and identifying commanders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/report/execsum.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It is important to note that, once the Amnesty Committee finishes its work, an additional volume will report on the work of that...</span></span></span>

**Fragmentary testimony.** Many surviving accounts come from former operatives seeking amnesty. Their evidence can be highly detailed but is necessarily selective, since applicants generally focused on incidents relevant to their own legal position rather than providing comprehensive histories of regional operations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/media/1999/9907/p990729b.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TRUTH AND RECONCILIATION COMMISSIONJuly 29, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 29, 1999</span></span></span>

**Multiple security structures.** Responsibility was divided among the Security Branch, Military Intelligence, Special Forces, Vlakplaas and covert informant networks. Even when a particular unit carried out an attack, responsibility for planning, political approval and intelligence gathering may have rested elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/report/finalreport/volume%202.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Justice VOLUME TWO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truth and Reconciliation Commission of...</span></span></span>

**Operations designed for deniability.** False identities, unmarked vehicles, intermediaries and recruited informers were standard features of clandestine operations. These methods succeeded in frustrating later reconstruction almost as effectively as they concealed responsibility at the time.

The result is that some murders can be linked confidently to particular operatives, while others remain attributable only to unnamed elements of the apartheid security apparatus.

## Victims whose stories remain incomplete

The uncertainty affects more than institutional history. It also affects individual families.

TRC hearings referred to periods during which Swaziland became what witnesses described as a "killing ground" for anti-apartheid activists. Testimony discussed victims including Mildred Msomi, Tutu Nkwanyane and Theophilis Dlodlo, while journalists and later researchers connected several of these attacks to Vlakplaas-linked death squads. Even so, definitive legal findings identifying every participant were often impossible because corroborating documentation was absent or witnesses disagreed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/hrvtrans/methodis/dlodlo.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TRUTH AND RECONCILIATION COMMISSIONMay 3, 1996...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 3, 1996</span></span></span>

Other cases involve disappearances, unsuccessful assassination attempts or killings attributed to unnamed covert units. In these instances, historians can often establish that South African forces were active in the area and pursuing specific targets, yet cannot fully reconstruct the operational orders behind the attack.

## Why the missing records still matter

The missing files are not merely an archival problem. They shape modern understanding of responsibility.

Without complete operational records it is difficult to determine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* which killings were centrally authorised and which were initiated locally;
* how intelligence about targets was collected;
* whether Eswatini's own officials knowingly assisted particular operations;
* how extensive informer networks became inside the kingdom; and
* whether additional victims have never been formally identified.

</div>

The ANC itself highlighted many of these unanswered questions in submissions to the TRC, calling for disclosure of who authorised cross-border assassinations, who carried them out and what happened to missing activists. Many of those questions remain only partially answered decades later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://justice.gov.za/trc/hrvtrans/submit/anctruth.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Justice ANC Submission to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ANC Submission to the Truth and Reconciliation CommissionAugust 19, 1996...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 1996</span></span></span>

## What is established and what remains unresolved

The strongest available evidence allows several firm conclusions.

Established facts include that South African apartheid security forces repeatedly conducted covert operations inside Eswatini; that these included kidnappings, bombings, ambushes and assassinations; that senior officials authorised at least some operations; and that TRC investigations confirmed a pattern of unlawful cross-border violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/report/finalreport/volume%202.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov.za">[justice.gov.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Justice VOLUME TWO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truth and Reconciliation Commission of...</span></span></span>

What remains unresolved is narrower but deeply important. Historians still cannot identify every victim with certainty, reconstruct every chain of command or determine precisely who issued orders in numerous individual cases. The destruction and disappearance of security records, combined with incomplete confessions and the passage of time, mean that some of Eswatini's apartheid-era killings remain permanently clouded by missing evidence.

Rather than a conspiracy built on speculation, this is an archival mystery rooted in documented absence: enough evidence survives to prove the campaign existed, but not enough to explain every death or assign responsibility in every case.
