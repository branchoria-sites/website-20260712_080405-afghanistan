## Introduction

The mystery of the Banyoles Man is not about whether he existed, but about who he really was. For more than 80 years, the preserved body of an unidentified African man was displayed in the Darder Museum in Banyoles, Spain, under the name "El Negro" ("The Black Man"). After an international campaign against the display of human remains, his body was removed, returned to [Botswana]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-botswanas/' | relative_url }}) in 2000, and buried with state honours in Gaborone. Yet the central question remains unresolved: what was his real name, where exactly did he come from, and was Botswana truly his homeland? Those unanswered questions have turned a successful repatriation into one of Botswana's most unusual historical mysteries, raising wider issues about colonial collecting, museum ethics and the difficulty of restoring identity after historical records have been lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/eln0.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro Index Page</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 11, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 11, 2003</span></span></span>

## How his body entered European collections

The man's story appears to begin in southern Africa around 1830. According to nineteenth-century accounts left by the French naturalists Jules and Édouard Verreaux, they removed the body of a recently deceased African man from a burial site during one of their collecting expeditions. The body was then prepared using taxidermy techniques, transported to France and exhibited alongside animal specimens as an ethnographic curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/banyol.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro&quot; of Banyoles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 20, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 1999</span></span></span>

During the nineteenth century, the preserved body changed hands several times before being purchased by the Catalan naturalist Francesc Darder. It became part of the museum that later bore Darder's name in Banyoles, where it remained on public display from 1916 until 1997. Visitors knew it simply as "El Negro", a label that erased any personal identity and encouraged viewers to see the body as representing an anonymous African rather than an individual with a family and community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/eln0.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro Index Page</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 11, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 11, 2003</span></span></span>

International criticism intensified after Haitian physician Alphonse Arcelin challenged the display during the period leading up to the 1992 Barcelona Olympic Games. After years of political debate, the museum agreed to return the remains to Africa. Before repatriation, conservators removed the artificial materials added during taxidermy, leaving mainly the skeleton for burial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elpais.com/diario/2000/02/09/catalunya/950062062_850215.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elpais.com">[elpais.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elpais.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El PaísEl Ayuntamiento de Banyoles repatriará a Botsuana al bosquimano disecado &#124; Noticias de Cataluña &#124; EL PAÍSFebruary 9, 2000...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 9, 2000</span></span></span>

## Why his identity became confused

The greatest mystery is that almost every description of the man contradicted another.

Early museum records variously described him as a "Bushman", a "Bechuana", a "Kalahari warrior", a "chief", or simply "El Negro". These labels reflected nineteenth-century European assumptions rather than reliable documentation. Even the supposed place where his body had been taken changed repeatedly in museum catalogues and newspaper reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/banyol.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro&quot; of Banyoles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 20, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 1999</span></span></span>

Research undertaken before the repatriation challenged many long-held assumptions. Historian Neil Parsons and colleagues at the University of Botswana examined surviving documents from the Verreaux brothers, museum catalogues and early newspaper reports. Their work suggested that the man was probably not a San man from the Kalahari, as had often been claimed, but more likely belonged to the Batlhaping, a Tswana-speaking community living near the confluence of the Orange and Vaal rivers in what is now South Africa. They estimated that he was about 27 years old when he died around 1830.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/banyol3.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El Negro: update April 2000...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 2000</span></span></span>

Even these conclusions remain provisional. The surviving documents are incomplete, sometimes contradictory and filtered through European collectors whose main interest was acquiring specimens rather than accurately recording personal histories. No reliable record preserves the man's own name, family or precise birthplace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/banyol3.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El Negro: update April 2000...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 2000</span></span></span>

## Why Botswana received the remains

One of the unusual aspects of the case is that Botswana accepted the remains despite uncertainty over whether the man was actually from territory within its present-day borders.

The decision reflected both diplomacy and symbolism. The Organisation of African Unity supported returning the remains to Africa, while Botswana agreed to receive them because the historical records repeatedly referred to the individual as a "Bechuana" and because no other state could establish a stronger claim. In October 2000 the remains were flown to Gaborone, where thousands of people paid their respects before a state burial in Tsholofelo Park. The grave was later recognised as a national monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/eln06.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro: arrival and burial</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 5, 2000...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 5, 2000</span></span></span>

The ceremony restored dignity to a man whose body had been exhibited as an object. However, it did not resolve the question of whether he was buried in the place he would himself have considered home. If the Batlhaping hypothesis is correct, his original community may have lived in what is now South Africa rather than Botswana.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/eln0.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro Index Page</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 11, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 11, 2003</span></span></span>

## The unresolved questions after repatriation

Although the repatriation closed one chapter, several historical questions remain unanswered.

The most significant include:

* **What was his real name?** No contemporary African record identifying him has survived.
* **Exactly where was he buried originally?** Documentary evidence points broadly towards the Orange–Vaal region but not to a specific grave.
* **Which community did he belong to?** The Batlhaping interpretation is widely regarded as the strongest documentary explanation, but some alternative interpretations continue to appear in later publications.
* **Should he have been buried in Botswana?** Botswana's government accepted the remains in good faith, yet historians acknowledge that uncertainty about his origins means another location might arguably have been appropriate if stronger evidence ever emerged.
* **What became of associated objects?** Questions also surrounded the display items—such as clothing, weapons and ornaments—that had accompanied the taxidermied body and whether all culturally significant materials should also have been returned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/eln0.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Negro Index Page</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 11, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 11, 2003</span></span></span>

Because no known descendants have been identified and no definitive archival discovery has emerged since the repatriation, these questions remain open.

## Why the case still matters

The Banyoles Man occupies a distinctive place in Botswana's historical memory because the mystery is inseparable from questions of governance and human dignity.

Unlike many historical mysteries that revolve around hidden treasure or unexplained deaths, this case concerns the recovery of identity after colonial collecting practices stripped an individual of his name and history. Botswana's role was not to solve every historical uncertainty but to ensure that the remains were no longer treated as a museum curiosity. The repatriation became an important statement that human remains deserve respect regardless of whether every detail of their biography can be reconstructed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://d.lib.msu.edu/pula/342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: d.lib.msu.edu">[msu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">d.lib.msu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Missing persons, stolen bodies and issues of patrimony: the El Negro story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Missing persons, stolen bodies and issues of patrimony: the El Negro story</span></span></span>

For historians, the case also demonstrates the limits of archival reconstruction. Careful research has narrowed the possibilities considerably, making the Batlhaping explanation more persuasive than earlier claims that the man came from the Kalahari. Yet the evidence has never reached the point where his identity can be stated with certainty. His real name, family and rightful resting place remain among Botswana's most enduring unresolved historical questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thuto.org/ubh/afhist/elnegro/banyol3.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thuto.org">[thuto.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thuto.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El Negro: update April 2000...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 2000</span></span></span>
