## Introduction

The mystery of who first settled the Fijian Islands is not about whether people arrived, but exactly how they did so. Archaeologists agree that the first permanent settlers reached Fiji around 3,000 years ago as part of the remarkable Lapita expansion across the western Pacific. Yet important questions remain unresolved. Did the first colonists arrive in a single wave or several? Which islands did they depart from? Was Bourewa truly Fiji's earliest settlement, or have later discoveries changed the picture? These debates matter because Fiji sits at a pivotal point in one of humanity's greatest maritime migrations. Understanding its earliest communities helps explain how people first crossed vast stretches of open ocean and eventually populated Remote Oceania.

## What Lapita pottery reveals

The strongest evidence for Fiji's first settlers comes not from written records but from pottery. Distinctive Lapita ceramics, decorated with finely stamped geometric patterns, appear across a vast area stretching from the Bismarck Archipelago through Vanuatu and New Caledonia to Fiji, Tonga and Samoa. Because these vessels share manufacturing techniques and decorative styles, they provide archaeologists with a reliable marker for tracking the movement of people rather than simply the exchange of goods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e Peninsula, Viti Levu Island &#124; Journal of Pacific ArchaeologyAugust 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2013</span></span></span>

In Fiji, Lapita pottery is found alongside evidence of permanent settlement, including shell middens, stone tools, hearths and the remains of transported plants and animals. The settlers were not isolated explorers surviving by chance. They deliberately carried crops such as taro and yam, domesticated animals and the knowledge needed to establish new communities on previously uninhabited islands. Microfossil studies from Bourewa have identified evidence consistent with introduced food crops, reinforcing the view that colonisation involved planned migration rather than accidental drift.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440306001555" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evidence for introduced taro (Colocasia esculenta) and lesser yam (Dioscorea esculenta) in Lapita-era (c. 3050–2500 cal. yr...</span></span></span>

The pottery also reveals cultural links rather than identical societies. While Lapita ceramics across the Pacific share recognisable features, local differences emerged rapidly after settlement. This suggests that Fiji became both a destination and a centre of cultural development, rather than merely a stopping point on a longer journey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e Peninsula, Viti Levu Island &#124; Journal of Pacific ArchaeologyAugust 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2013</span></span></span>

## Why Bourewa became central to the puzzle

For many years, Bourewa on the southwest coast of Viti Levu was celebrated as Fiji's oldest known settlement. Excavations beginning in the early 2000s uncovered an extensive Lapita site containing decorated pottery, shell deposits and radiocarbon samples suggesting occupation around 1200 BC. The discovery transformed understanding of Fiji's earliest history because it demonstrated that sophisticated seafarers had reached the islands much earlier than previously documented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://repository.usp.ac.fj/id/eprint/3116/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.usp.ac.fj">[repository.usp.ac.fj]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.usp.ac.fj</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Early Lapita settlement site at Bourewa, southwest Viti Levu Island, Fiji</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Early Lapita settlement site at Bourewa, southwest VitiLevu Island, Fiji - USP Electronic Research Repository...</span></span></span>

However, archaeology rarely provides simple answers. Dating ancient settlements depends on interpreting radiocarbon samples correctly, and not every sample reflects the exact moment people first arrived.

### The dating problem

The principal uncertainty concerns what materials should be trusted. Charcoal can be misleading because it may come from the heartwood of old trees that were already centuries old when burned. Marine shells present another challenge because ocean chemistry can distort radiocarbon ages unless carefully corrected.

A major Bayesian re-analysis of more than 80 radiocarbon dates from Bourewa and neighbouring sites concluded that many charcoal dates were artificially old. After accounting for these problems, researchers estimated initial occupation at approximately 2,814 years before present (around the ninth century BC), somewhat later than some of the earliest published estimates. The same study argued that another Lapita site, Matanamuani on Naigani Island, may now represent Fiji's earliest securely dated settlement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e Peninsula, Viti Levu Island &#124; Journal of Pacific ArchaeologyAugust 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2013</span></span></span>

This illustrates an important archaeological principle: the mystery is not created by disagreement over the existence of early settlers, but by improving scientific methods that continually refine when those settlers first arrived.

## Competing migration models

Although scholars broadly agree that Fiji was settled by Lapita peoples moving eastwards through the Pacific, several questions remain open.

**A rapid expansion model** proposes that highly skilled navigators spread quickly through Vanuatu, New Caledonia, Fiji, Tonga and Samoa within only a few generations. Similar pottery traditions and closely clustered radiocarbon dates support the idea of remarkably rapid colonisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e Peninsula, Viti Levu Island &#124; Journal of Pacific ArchaeologyAugust 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2013</span></span></span>

**A staged migration model** suggests a more gradual process in which explorers established pioneering settlements before later groups expanded and strengthened the population. Some archaeological evidence points to satellite communities appearing after the first occupation of major sites such as Bourewa, indicating that settlement unfolded over time rather than through a single migration event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://repository.usp.ac.fj/id/eprint/3116/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.usp.ac.fj">[repository.usp.ac.fj]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.usp.ac.fj</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Early Lapita settlement site at Bourewa, southwest Viti Levu Island, Fiji</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Early Lapita settlement site at Bourewa, southwest VitiLevu Island, Fiji - USP Electronic Research Repository...</span></span></span>

**Interaction rather than replacement** has become another important perspective. Instead of imagining Lapita settlers moving through an empty cultural landscape, many researchers argue that migration involved continuing contacts with communities in Near Oceania before and during expansion into Fiji. Exchange networks, shared technologies and evolving cultural practices may have shaped the migration itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e Peninsula, Viti Levu Island &#124; Journal of Pacific ArchaeologyAugust 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2013</span></span></span>

None of these models disputes that Lapita peoples were Fiji's earliest known settlers. The debate centres on the pace, organisation and complexity of the migration.

## What remains uncertain

Several enduring questions continue to drive research.

First, archaeologists have not identified the definitive "first landing place" in Fiji. Bourewa remains one of the most significant sites, but newer analyses show that other settlements may be equally old or even slightly earlier. Future discoveries could again alter the picture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e Peninsula, Viti Levu Island &#124; Journal of Pacific ArchaeologyAugust 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2013</span></span></span>

Second, the exact migration routes remain debated. Most evidence favours movement through Vanuatu and New Caledonia before reaching Fiji, but it is difficult to reconstruct individual voyages undertaken three millennia ago. Pottery styles, stone sourcing and environmental evidence all provide clues, yet none offers a complete map of the first journeys.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e Peninsula, Viti Levu Island &#124; Journal of Pacific ArchaeologyAugust 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2013</span></span></span>

Third, researchers continue to investigate how many migration events occurred. The archaeological record could represent one substantial colonising population followed by local expansion, or several related groups arriving over successive generations. The available evidence does not yet distinguish these possibilities conclusively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e Peninsula, Viti Levu Island &#124; Journal of Pacific ArchaeologyAugust 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2013</span></span></span>

## Why the Lapita puzzle still matters

The mystery surrounding Fiji's first settlers is unusual because it concerns the refinement of knowledge rather than the absence of evidence. Archaeologists possess abundant traces of the earliest communities, yet every improvement in dating techniques, excavation methods and scientific analysis slightly reshapes the chronology.

Fiji occupies a crucial position in the story of Pacific exploration. It marks the point where the Lapita expansion entered Remote Oceania before later populations developed the cultures that would eventually spread across Polynesia. Solving the remaining questions about Fiji's earliest settlements therefore illuminates not only the history of one island nation but also one of humanity's greatest achievements: the deliberate settlement of the world's largest ocean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pacificarchaeology.org/index.php/journal/article/view/NunnPetchey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pacificarchaeology.org">[pacificarchaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pacificarchaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e Peninsula, Viti Levu Island &#124; Journal of Pacific ArchaeologyAugust 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2013</span></span></span>
