## Introduction

Five cases stand out: the lost Great Bell of Dhammazedi, the assassination of independence leader [Aung San]({{ 'aung-san/' | relative_url }}), the missing royal ruby and other treasures of King Thibaw, the supposed burial of wartime Spitfires, and the transformation of the ancient Pyu cities. Together they show why unresolved history in Myanmar is unusually difficult to investigate. Colonial conquest scattered records and artefacts; war destroyed sites and memories; later governments restricted archives and archaeology; and political upheaval repeatedly interrupted research.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Myanmar_928b7c-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Which Myanmar Mysteries Still Defy a Clear..." loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The best approach is therefore not to ask whether each story is “true” in a simple sense. It is to separate well-supported events from uncertain details, later folklore and claims that have failed serious testing.

## Is the Great Bell of Dhammazedi still beneath Yangon’s rivers?

The Great Bell of Dhammazedi is Myanmar’s most famous lost treasure. According to the traditional account, King Dhammazedi commissioned an enormous bronze bell in the late fifteenth century and presented it to the Shwedagon Pagoda. The Venetian merchant Gasparo Balbi, who visited Lower Myanmar in the 1580s, described seeing a very large bell covered in writing he could not read. His testimony is important because it appears to confirm that a remarkable bell existed before its disappearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Bell_of_Dhammazedi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Great Bell of Dhammazedi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 29, 2008 — The Great Bell of Dhammazedi was a bronze bell, believed to be the largest bell ever cast. It was cast on 5 February 1484...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 29, 2008</span></span></span>

The standard story says that the Portuguese adventurer Filipe de Brito seized the bell in 1608. He intended to take it across the river to his base at Thanlyin, melt it down and use the metal for cannon. The overloaded raft supposedly broke apart near the meeting of the Yangon and Bago rivers, taking the bell and perhaps part of de Brito’s vessel to the bottom. The bell has never been recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Bell_of_Dhammazedi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Great Bell of Dhammazedi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 29, 2008 — The Great Bell of Dhammazedi was a bronze bell, believed to be the largest bell ever cast. It was cast on 5 February 1484...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 29, 2008</span></span></span>

What remains uncertain is almost everything needed for a successful search: the bell’s exact size, the route by which it was moved, the place where the raft failed and the degree to which river channels have shifted since the seventeenth century. Claims that the bell weighed roughly 300 tonnes depend on traditional measurements whose interpretation is disputed. A bell of that weight would also have presented immense transport problems, even before it reached the water.

There is a deeper documentary difficulty. A Myanmar historian interviewed during a 2014 search noted that several major chronicles of the period do not mention the bell, even though Dhammazedi’s other religious donations were recorded. Balbi’s description remains the strongest early evidence, but it does not prove the later account of the theft and sinking in every detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.irrawaddy.com/news/burma/spirits-prayers-mark-hunt-burmas-lost-bell.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irrawaddy.com">[The Irrawaddy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irrawaddy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">spirits prayers mark hunt burmas lost bell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The IrrawaddySpirits, Prayers Mark Hunt for Burma&#x27;s Lost Bell26 Aug 2014 — The only record Win has found that mentions the bell was writt...</span></span></span>

Repeated searches have used divers, sonar and local knowledge. Muddy water, heavy sediment, shipwreck debris and centuries of changing river geography make identification exceptionally difficult. In 2014, a search team announced that it had located the bell, while also invoking supernatural guardians as an explanation for delays. No independently verified recovery followed, and the claim was not accepted as proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.irrawaddy.com/news/burma/dragons-add-mythical-twist-search-dhammazedi-bell.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irrawaddy.com">[The Irrawaddy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irrawaddy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Irrawaddy Dragons Add Mythical Twist to Search for Dhammazedi Bell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The IrrawaddyDragons Add Mythical Twist to Search for Dhammazedi BellAugust 26, 2014 — 26 Aug 2014 — A salvage team says it has found the...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 26, 2014</span></span></span>

The most reasonable conclusion is that a large bell almost certainly existed, but the familiar sinking story contains details that cannot yet be securely checked. The bell may remain buried beneath deep sediment, may lie somewhere other than the commonly searched area, or may have broken apart long ago. It is also possible that later tradition combined several events into one memorable narrative.

Its cultural importance is larger than its material value. The story turns an attempted act of plunder into a tale of frustrated conquest: the sacred object is stolen, but the thief never succeeds in turning it into a weapon. That moral shape helps explain why the bell has remained vivid in public memory even when searches have produced no physical result.

## Who was really behind Aung San’s assassination?

On 19 July 1947, only months before Burma became independent, gunmen entered the Secretariat in Rangoon, now Yangon, and opened fire on a meeting of the Executive Council. Aung San and several senior colleagues were killed. The murders removed much of the leadership expected to guide the new state through independence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historytoday.com/archive/feature/who-killed-aung-san" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historytoday.com">[History Today]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historytoday.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History Today Who Killed Aung San?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History TodayWho Killed Aung San?May 19, 2026 — 6 Jun 2026 — In 1947, as independence loomed, Burma&#x27;s prime minister-in-waiting, Aung San...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 19, 2026</span></span></span>

The central legal question appeared to be settled quickly. Investigators connected the gunmen to U Saw, a former prime minister and political rival of Aung San. U Saw and several accomplices were tried, convicted and sentenced to death. The surviving court record and contemporary evidence make it difficult to argue that U Saw had no involvement at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.networkmyanmar.org/ESW/Files/Death-of-Aung-San-rev3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: networkmyanmar.org">[networkmyanmar.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">networkmyanmar.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Death of Aung San rev3</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Death of Aung San in 1947 - An Important ClarificationNovember 26, 2022 — The assassination of General Aung San and six members of th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 26, 2022</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery is whether he acted alone. Weapons associated with U Saw had passed through British military hands, and British officers were implicated in illicit arms dealing. A British officer, David Vivian, was later imprisoned for supplying weapons to U Saw. This created obvious grounds for suspicion at a moment when colonial officials, soldiers, intelligence personnel and Burmese political groups were operating in a poorly controlled post-war environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aung_San" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aung San</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aung San</span></span></span>

That evidence supports several different interpretations:

* **U Saw organised the murders for political advantage.** This remains the explanation most closely supported by the trial and the direct links between his circle, the weapons and the attackers.
* **Individual British officers assisted him without official authority.** The illegal arms trade makes this plausible, but does not by itself establish a wider state conspiracy.
* **A faction within the colonial establishment encouraged or protected the plot.** This theory has persisted because some officials distrusted Aung San, important records were once restricted, and the weapons trail involved British personnel.
* **The British government ordered the assassination.** No conclusive documentary proof has emerged for this much stronger claim. British policy under Clement Attlee had moved towards transferring power, and contemporary British officials also regarded Aung San as the most capable leader available to manage independence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.networkmyanmar.org/ESW/Files/Death-of-Aung-San-rev3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: networkmyanmar.org">[networkmyanmar.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">networkmyanmar.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Death of Aung San rev3</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Death of Aung San in 1947 - An Important ClarificationNovember 26, 2022 — The assassination of General Aung San and six members of th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 26, 2022</span></span></span>

Later archival releases have added detail but have not produced a decisive order, payment record or intelligence document proving that the British government directed the attack. Historical investigations have therefore tended to distinguish between documented British negligence or criminal arms dealing and the unproven claim of an authorised colonial assassination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.networkmyanmar.org/ESW/Files/Death-of-Aung-San-rev3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: networkmyanmar.org">[networkmyanmar.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">networkmyanmar.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Death of Aung San rev3</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Death of Aung San in 1947 - An Important ClarificationNovember 26, 2022 — The assassination of General Aung San and six members of th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 26, 2022</span></span></span>

The case remains powerful partly because the political consequences cannot be measured. Aung San had been negotiating relations among the central government and ethnic nationalities while also preparing constitutional independence. His death did not single-handedly cause Myanmar’s later civil wars or military rule, but it removed a uniquely influential figure at an exceptionally fragile moment.

Public memory has consequently expanded the question from “Who supplied the gunmen?” to “What kind of country might have emerged had Aung San lived?” The first question is historical and evidential. The second is an unanswerable counterfactual, but it has made the assassination a continuing national wound rather than merely a solved criminal case.

## What happened to King Thibaw’s missing royal treasures?

When British forces occupied Mandalay in November 1885, they ended the Konbaung monarchy and sent King Thibaw and Queen Supayalat into exile in India. The palace contained royal regalia, gemstones, ceremonial objects and precious metalwork. Some items were inventoried and transferred into British custody; others were dispersed, presented to institutions or returned decades later. The British Museum records that parts of the last king’s regalia were seized in 1885 and that important pieces were returned to Myanmar in 1964.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2023-11/Burma_to_Myanmar_large_print_guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Museum Burma to Myanmar: large print exhibition text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museum Burma to Myanmar: large print exhibition text</span></span></span>

The most famous missing object is the Nga Mauk ruby, traditionally described as an exceptionally large and valuable stone belonging to the crown. According to the royal family’s account, Thibaw entrusted the ruby and other gems to Colonel Edward Sladen during the surrender and expected them to be restored. After the king reached exile, he repeatedly sought the return of missing valuables. British officials replied that they could not locate a ruby recorded under that name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/burma-royal-ruby-nagamauk-story/81583262" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slideshare.net">[www.slideshare.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slideshare.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">burma royal ruby nagamauk story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">burma royal ruby nagamauk story</span></span></span>

This is a genuine archival mystery, but the popular version often outruns the evidence. Descriptions of the ruby’s extraordinary size, perfect colour or magical qualities come partly from royal tradition and later retellings. No securely identified photograph, scientific description or surviving inventory entry establishes exactly what the stone looked like. It may have been known under another name, confused with another gem or described more magnificently over time.

The possibilities are narrower than the legends suggest:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* It may have been privately stolen during the confusion of the palace occupation.
* It may have entered an official collection under an incomplete or altered description.
* It may have been broken up, recut or sold, making later identification impossible.
* The object remembered as one unique ruby may represent a more complicated group of royal stones and stories.

</div>

Claims that the Nga Mauk ruby entered the British Crown Jewels have never been demonstrated by reliable gemological or archival evidence. Similar appearance is not enough: large rubies cannot be identified securely from colour, legend or royal association alone.

Other palace treasures also disappeared. A British Library-related investigation cited reports of a jewelled crown, large quantities of stones, a golden calf and a ruby taken from a major Buddha image. Yet palace inventories, eyewitness testimony and later family recollections do not always agree, leaving historians unsure how many objects were formally seized, privately looted, concealed or exaggerated in later accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeofam.com/journal/2020/6/17/the-treasures-of-king-thibaw-of-burma-a-british-library-investigation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeofam.com">[ArchaeoFam]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeofam.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archaeo Fam The Treasures of King Thibaw of Burma</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archaeo Fam The Treasures of King Thibaw of Burma</span></span></span>

The mystery matters because it concerns more than the location of a gemstone. The missing treasures represent the disorder and unequal power of annexation. British authorities controlled the records, transport and distribution of the captured court’s property, while the deposed family had limited means to challenge official answers. Even a complete modern search of museum collections could not easily reconstruct what happened during the poorly documented first days of occupation.

## Were dozens of Spitfires buried after the Second World War?

The buried-Spitfires story claimed that British forces packed unused fighter aircraft into crates and buried them near airfields in Burma at the end of the Second World War. Versions of the account placed aircraft at Mingaladon, Meiktila or Myitkyina and suggested that dozens of nearly pristine machines were waiting underground. In 2013, an internationally publicised expedition began excavations near Yangon airport.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/trans-trans-regional-and-national-studies-of-southeast-asia/article/spitfires-sprouting-in-the-burmese-spring-the-reallife-quest-for-historic-fantasy-aircraft-in-contemporary-myanmar/FAB50A064B15CFCE43A7954F19A0303A" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Unlike many lost-treasure stories, this one was subjected to organised archival, archaeological and geophysical testing. Researchers examined wartime records, witness accounts, aerial photographs and suspected burial sites. Excavation found no aircraft, no crates and no convincing evidence of a large concealment operation. The project’s historical report concluded that the traditional account was extremely difficult to sustain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/11790075/ASSUMED_MISSING_REPORTED_BURIED_the_search_for_the_lost_Spitfires_of_Burma_A_REPORT_INTO_HISTORICAL_RESEARCH_AND_SUBSEQUENT_FIELDWORK_AT_YANGON_INTERNATIONAL_AIRPORT_UNDERTAKEN_BETWEEN_6_AND_22_JANUARY_2013" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

The practical objections were substantial. Burying complete aircraft would have required labour, machinery, fuel and documentation at a time when military units were accounting for equipment and withdrawing from the region. Aircraft could be destroyed, abandoned, cannibalised for parts or flown elsewhere more easily than they could be secretly buried in large numbers. Wartime metal, runway surfaces, trenches and later construction could also produce misleading signals during geophysical surveys.

Researchers did find evidence of the wartime landscape, including structures and disturbed ground. That discovery helped explain why earlier survey results and local memories might have seemed promising without confirming the presence of aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.cision.com/wyg/r/legend-of-the-buried-spitfires-unearthed-%2Cc9436406" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.cision.com">[Cision News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.cision.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">News Legend of the buried spitfires unearthed?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">News Legend of the buried spitfires unearthed?</span></span></span>

The strongest assessment is therefore that the mass burial did not happen in the form usually described. Isolated aircraft parts or wreckage may remain around former wartime sites, but this is very different from squadrons of factory-packed Spitfires hidden underground.

The case is useful because it shows how a modern historical legend develops. A witness remembers crates or earthworks. The story gains a famous object—the Spitfire—and the supposed number grows as it is retold. Media attention makes the claim appear more established than it is. Finally, physical investigation is interpreted not as a test of the story but as another dramatic chapter in the search.

In this instance, archaeology did not solve a mystery by finding treasure. It solved much of it by showing that the expected evidence was absent.

## Did the Pyu civilisation suddenly disappear?

The ancient Pyu cities of Halin, Beikthano and Sri Ksetra were among the earliest major urban centres in mainland Southeast Asia. Their walled settlements, religious monuments and irrigation systems developed across much of the first millennium. UNESCO dates the wider Pyu urban tradition from roughly 200 BC to AD 900, although individual sites had different histories and periods of importance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1444/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Older summaries often describe a dramatic ending. Chinese records report attacks by the kingdom of Nanzhao and the capture of Pyu people in the ninth century. This encouraged a simple narrative in which invasion destroyed the cities, the population vanished and a new Bamar civilisation replaced it.

Archaeology has made that picture less certain. Radiocarbon dating indicates that the major Pyu centres were functioning by the early centuries AD, but researchers have also found continuity between late prehistoric settlements, Pyu urbanism and later regional development. The evidence does not fit neatly with the idea that an entire people disappeared at one moment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/0F0C574F1E15EAF91616517928428FBF/9789814786652c6_p88-121_CBO.pdf/thousand_years_before_bagan_radiocarbon_dates_and_myanmars_ancient_pyu_cities.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resolve.cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resolve.cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Several processes may have overlapped: warfare, shifts in trade routes, changing river and irrigation conditions, political reorganisation, migration and the growth of Bagan. Some cities declined while cultural practices, religious traditions and populations continued elsewhere. Pyu influence survived in writing, art, Buddhist institutions and forms of urban organisation.

The mystery is therefore not “Where did everyone go?” but how political identity changed when the cities lost power. Written sources are limited, archaeology remains incomplete, and the labels used by later historians may make communities appear more separate than they were. It is often impossible to determine whether a change in pottery, inscriptions or political centres represents population replacement, cultural blending or a new ruling elite.

This is an important correction to the popular “lost civilisation” story. The Pyu were not a vanished race leaving inexplicable ruins. They were communities whose cities rose, changed and declined over many centuries. The unresolved task is to reconstruct that long transition from uneven excavation, scattered inscriptions and external records.

## Why Myanmar’s mysteries remain difficult to settle

These cases survive for different reasons, but several obstacles recur.

**Records are unevenly distributed.** Documents concerning annexation, military intelligence and royal property were often produced or retained by foreign authorities. Myanmar chronicles, oral histories and colonial files may describe the same event from incompatible positions.

**Landscapes have changed.** Rivers shift, tropical conditions damage materials, cities expand over older sites and war repeatedly disturbs the ground. Even a real object may no longer remain where historical testimony places it.

**Political instability limits investigation.** Archaeology, archival access and heritage conservation require long-term institutions. Myanmar’s coups, armed conflicts and periods of isolation have repeatedly restricted that continuity.

**National memory adds moral meaning.** The Dhammazedi Bell represents sacred endurance; the royal ruby represents colonial dispossession; Aung San’s death represents a lost political future. Such meanings are historically significant, but they can also make sceptical examination appear disrespectful or politically motivated.

**Repetition can harden speculation into apparent fact.** An uncertain estimate becomes a precise weight; an allegation becomes a documented conspiracy; a remembered wartime excavation becomes a buried squadron. The more vivid version is usually the one that survives.

The most persuasive explanations are consequently modest. U Saw’s conspiracy is well supported, while an official British order is not. A great bell probably existed, but its exact resting place and dimensions remain unknown. [Royal treasures]({{ 'royal-treasures-999d98/' | relative_url }}) were undoubtedly dispersed, although the identity and later fate of the Nga Mauk ruby cannot be proved. The mass-burial story of the Spitfires has largely failed physical and archival testing. The Pyu cities declined, but their inhabitants and culture did not simply vanish.

Myanmar’s enduring historical enigmas matter because they expose the limits of the surviving record. They reveal where conquest, violence, institutional failure and collective memory have left gaps—and why those gaps continue to attract both careful investigation and imaginative mythmaking.
