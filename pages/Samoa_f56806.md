## Introduction

These cases differ sharply. The *Joyita* remains an authentic disappearance, although mechanical failure probably explains why the voyage went wrong. Pulemelei is an archaeological puzzle whose broad social role is becoming clearer, even though its precise ceremonies and builders are unknown. [Black Saturday]({{ 'black-saturday/' | relative_url }}) is not mysterious in the sense that responsibility is unknowable: colonial police fired on Samoans. The narrower mystery is why the fatal shots were never properly attributed and why no individual was prosecuted. Together, the cases show how genuine uncertainty can arise from ocean conditions, lost traditions and institutions that failed to preserve—or chose not to pursue—the full truth.

## The empty ship that left Apia

On 3 October 1955, the *Joyita* departed Apia for the Tokelau Islands on what should have been a journey of roughly two days. It carried 25 passengers and crew, along with timber, food and medical supplies. When it failed to arrive, aircraft searched a vast area of ocean without finding it. More than five weeks later, on 10 November, another vessel encountered the *Joyita* far to the west of its expected route, listing heavily and partly flooded. Every person aboard had disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://natlib.govt.nz/records/22387490" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: natlib.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">natlib.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The incident was the subject of a Royal Commission of Enquiry in Western Samoa in 1956. Source...Read more...</span></span></span>

The discovery created an enduring maritime mystery because the vessel itself had survived. The *Joyita* had unusual buoyancy, partly because its wooden hull was lined with cork, so even severe flooding was unlikely to send it straight to the seabed. Yet its people, life-saving equipment, navigation instruments, logbook and much of its cargo were missing. Its radio was set to the international distress frequency, while later inspection revealed serious defects, including corroded pipework and inadequate radio range.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Joyita" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MV JoyitaThe ship was in very poor condition, with corroded pipes and a radio which... ### What probably went wrong

The strongest modern explanation does not require piracy, mutiny or Cold War intrigue. A corroded pipe in the engine-cooling system appears to have allowed seawater into the vessel. Because the leak was difficult to locate and the pumping arrangements were poor, water could have accumulated before the danger was fully understood. Engine failure and unreliable communications would then have made the situation appear worse than it was.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://explorersweb.com/exploration-mysteries-mv-joyita/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: explorersweb.com">[explorersweb.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">explorersweb.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">exploration mysteries mv joyita</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exploration Mysteries: MV Joyita10 Nov 2022 — The life rafts and cargo were missing, along with the captain&#x27;s logbooks, navig...</span></span></span>

The crucial mistake was probably abandoning ship. Those aboard may have believed that the badly listing *Joyita* was about to sink, without appreciating how much buoyancy the cork-filled hull provided. They may have transferred to rafts or a small boat, taking navigation equipment and supplies with them. Once separated from the ship, they would have been extremely vulnerable to rough seas, thirst and exposure.

This theory explains several important facts:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The flooding:** damaged pipework provided a credible route for seawater to enter.
* **The missing rafts and equipment:** these could have been taken during an organised evacuation.
* **The absent cargo:** some goods may have been unloaded to lighten the vessel or placed in the evacuation craft.
* **The ship’s survival:** the crew may have underestimated its ability to remain afloat.
* **The lack of bodies or wreckage:** a small raft could vanish almost completely in the central Pacific.

</div>

It does not establish exactly when the passengers left, whether everyone entered the same craft, or whether an injury or death accelerated the decision. Reported bloodstained bandages have encouraged more dramatic theories, but medical supplies and a doctor were aboard, so the bandages alone are not evidence of violence. The official inquiry criticised the vessel’s condition but could not determine the fate of its occupants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://natlib.govt.nz/records/22387490" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: natlib.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">natlib.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The incident was the subject of a Royal Commission of Enquiry in Western Samoa in 1956. ### Why the mystery persists

The *Joyita* case survives because the likely cause of the emergency is not the same as proof of what happened to the people. Mechanical evidence can reconstruct a leak and loss of power, but no survivor described the evacuation. The missing logbook removed the best possible record of the vessel’s final hours.

The case also remains a human issue rather than merely a “ghost ship” story. Families in Samoa and Tokelau were left without bodies, confirmed places of death or a final account of their relatives’ decisions. Community representatives have continued to seek formal recognition and closure decades later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.matauala.org.nz/joyita-unsolved-mystery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: matauala.org.nz">[Matauala Hub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">matauala.org.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Matauala HubJoyita - Unsolved Mystery &#124; Atafu Tokelau...The MV Joyita departed for Tokelau in 1955, but only 6 of the 23 people on board...</span></span></span>

The fairest conclusion is that the *Joyita* probably suffered a manageable mechanical emergency that appeared catastrophic to those aboard. The disappearance itself remains unsolved because the evacuation craft and all 25 people were never found.

## What was Pulemelei built to do?

On the island of Savai‘i stands the Pulemelei mound, a vast stepped structure made from basalt stone. Measuring roughly 60 metres across at its base and reaching about 12 metres at its highest side, it is generally described as the largest known ancient monumental structure in Polynesia. Its scale is not in doubt. Its precise function is.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wmf.org/monuments/pulemelei-mound" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wmf.org">[wmf.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wmf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wmf.org.</span></span></span>

Earlier popular descriptions sometimes treated Pulemelei as an isolated “pyramid” hidden in vegetation. Archaeological work has produced a more informative picture. The mound stood within a substantial inland settlement containing house platforms, pathways, stone boundaries, cooking areas and other structures. Excavations found evidence of occupation beneath the monument stretching back around two millennia, while the mound itself was enlarged in stages during the later prehistoric period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/40387368" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

That sequence matters. Pulemelei was not necessarily constructed in one campaign according to a single master plan. It developed over generations as the surrounding community changed. Radiocarbon evidence indicates an initial monumental platform followed by later additions, including a higher upper section and associated walkways. Researchers have interpreted it as an important central or ceremonial place connected with the growth of social hierarchy in Samoa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/40387368" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

### The plausible interpretations

No surviving inscription identifies the monument’s founder or states its purpose. Archaeologists therefore work from location, construction phases and comparison with other Samoan sites.

The leading possibilities overlap rather than exclude one another:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A chiefly centre:** its immense size may have displayed the authority and labour network of a powerful ruling family.
* **A ceremonial platform:** the summit and adjoining areas may have hosted gatherings, rites or high-status activities.
* **A political landmark:** the mound may have helped establish control over a large inland settlement and its surrounding productive land.
* **A monument repeatedly reworked:** later communities may have added new meanings to a structure inherited from earlier generations.

</div>

Evidence from Pulemelei and more recently documented large mounds elsewhere in Samoa supports the idea that monumental construction accompanied the emergence of increasingly unequal and organised communities. Large-scale building required leadership, food production and the mobilisation of many workers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2024/06/21/rare-samoan-discovery-offers-clues-to-the-origins-of-inequality.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: auckland.ac.nz">[auckland.ac.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">auckland.ac.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">rare samoan discovery offers clues to the origins of inequality</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">rare samoan discovery offers clues to the origins of inequality</span></span></span>

The claim that Pulemelei was an astronomical observatory, tomb-filled pyramid or work of an unknown foreign civilisation is not supported by the best evidence. Its materials, associated settlement and wider archaeological setting place it firmly within Samoa’s own history. The mystery concerns its exact use and changing meaning, not the cultural identity of the society that built it.

### Why archaeology cannot supply every answer

Stone survives better than speech, performance and political custom. Archaeologists can date charcoal, map foundations and identify cooking remains, but ceremonies may leave little permanent material. Later settlement changes, vegetation and limited excavation further reduce what can be reconstructed.

There is also a broader heritage problem. Samoan archaeology has historically received less sustained investigation than some neighbouring regions. Researchers working at Pulemelei described Savai‘i as comparatively underexplored, while recent scholarship has stressed the importance of combining archaeological research with local authority, oral history and heritage management.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/772723/Archaeological_investigations_at_the_Pulemelei_mound_Savaii_Samoa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[academia.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archaeological investigations at the Pulemelei mound Savaii Samoa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archaeological investigations at the Pulemelei mound Savaii Samoa</span></span></span>

Pulemelei is therefore a genuine archaeological enigma, but not a blank slate. It was almost certainly a major centre of Samoan communal, political and ceremonial life. What remains uncertain is which activities occurred there, who directed its successive enlargements and why the surrounding settlement was eventually abandoned.

## Who shot Tupua Tamasese?

On 28 December 1929, a procession organised by Samoa’s independence movement passed through Apia to welcome home supporters returning from exile. New Zealand colonial police attempted to make arrests. A clash followed, and police retreated towards their station before opening fire with revolvers, rifles and a Lewis machine gun. At least eight Samoans and one New Zealand constable were killed; others later gave higher totals. Among the Samoan dead was Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III, one of the movement’s most important leaders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">black saturday</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">black saturday</span></span></span>

The broad responsibility for Black Saturday is well established. Armed colonial police fired into a crowd during an operation for which they had prepared firearms and a mounted machine gun. The uncertainty lies in the detailed sequence of shots and, most significantly, the identity of the person who fired the bullet that mortally wounded Tupua Tamasese.

Accounts agree that he was trying to restrain or calm the crowd when he was hit. He died the following day. A subsequent inquest reportedly found that the rifle fire which killed him had been unnecessary, yet no officer was prosecuted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.govt.nz/discover-our-stories/the-rise-of-the-mau-and-the-fall-of-colonial-rule-in-samoa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govt.nz.</span></span></span>

### An unresolved killing, not an unknowable event

Describing Black Saturday as a mystery can be misleading if it suggests that all versions are equally uncertain. They are not. The colonial authorities’ armed response, the deaths and the political suppression surrounding the event are extensively documented. The narrower unresolved questions concern individual action and official accountability:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which officer fired the fatal bullet?
* Were commands given to use rifles and the machine gun, or did firing become uncontrolled?
* Why did the investigation not identify the shooter?
* Why was no one held criminally responsible after the inquest criticised the gunfire?
* To what extent did official reports minimise the conduct of the police?

</div>

Different sources give varying casualty totals, partly because some victims died later and because records distinguish differently between those killed immediately, fatally wounded or otherwise injured. The variations should not be used to cast doubt on the central facts. Official New Zealand historical and archival accounts now describe the firing as panicked and acknowledge Black Saturday as the worst episode in New Zealand’s relationship with Samoa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">black saturday</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">black saturday</span></span></span>

The absence of an identified shooter may reflect more than ordinary confusion. Police were firing from several positions, witnesses faced chaos, and ballistic investigation was limited. Yet the colonial administration also had little incentive to direct an aggressive inquiry against its own officers while it was attempting to weaken the independence movement. The failure to establish individual responsibility is therefore both an archival gap and a question about institutional power.

### How Black Saturday entered national memory

Tupua Tamasese’s death became a symbol of disciplined resistance. The independence movement continued despite arrests, exile and pressure from the administration, with women assuming increasingly visible organisational roles when many male activists were forced into hiding. Samoa eventually became independent in 1962.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nvdatabase.swarthmore.edu/content/mau-opposition-new-zealand-rule-samoa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nvdatabase.swarthmore.edu">[swarthmore.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nvdatabase.swarthmore.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mau opposition new zealand rule samoa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">mau opposition new zealand rule samoa</span></span></span>

The unresolved identity of the shooter matters because it represents a wider absence of justice. Samoa knows who controlled the police, who armed them and whose political system they were defending. What remains missing is the final step of accountability: the naming and prosecution of the individual responsible for the fatal shot.

## Why warships stayed in Apia’s doomed harbour

In March 1889, American and German warships were confronting one another in Apia during an international struggle for influence over Samoa. A British vessel was also present. As weather conditions deteriorated, the commanders faced a dangerous choice: leave the harbour for open sea or remain anchored in an exposed and increasingly violent anchorage. Most stayed. On 15 and 16 March, a cyclone wrecked or disabled nearly the entire fleet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/research/library/online-reading-room/title-list-alphabetically/t/typhoons-and-hurricanes-the-storm-at-apia-samoa-15-16-march-1889.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[navy.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">typhoons and hurricanes the storm at apia samoa 15 16 march 1889</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">typhoons and hurricanes the storm at apia samoa 15 16 march 1889</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 1889</span></span></span>

There is no mystery about the storm itself. Contemporary naval records and later histories describe the falling pressure, extreme wind, heavy seas and destruction in detail. The puzzle is psychological and political: why did experienced captains remain in a harbour known to become deadly during severe weather?

The usual explanation is national pride. Neither the German nor American squadron wished to be the first to withdraw during a tense military stand-off, because departure might appear to concede control of Apia. That interpretation is plausible, but it can become too neat. Commanders also faced uncertain forecasts, crowded conditions, difficult manoeuvring and the danger of attempting to leave too late. Decisions that appear obviously reckless after a disaster may have seemed less clear as the storm developed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/research/library/online-reading-room/title-list-alphabetically/t/typhoons-and-hurricanes-the-storm-at-apia-samoa-15-16-march-1889.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">typhoons and hurricanes the storm at apia samoa 15 16 march 1889</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">typhoons and hurricanes the storm at apia samoa 15 16 march 1889</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 1889</span></span></span>

The British cruiser *Calliope* eventually forced its way out to sea, narrowly escaping while other vessels were driven onto reefs or beaches. Its survival became the most celebrated episode of the disaster, but superior engines and a late, desperate decision both played roles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/research/library/online-reading-room/title-list-alphabetically/t/typhoons-and-hurricanes-the-storm-at-apia-samoa-15-16-march-1889.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">typhoons and hurricanes the storm at apia samoa 15 16 march 1889</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">typhoons and hurricanes the storm at apia samoa 15 16 march 1889</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: march 1889</span></span></span>

The cyclone has sometimes been romanticised as nature intervening to prevent an imperial battle. That is a memorable story, not an explanation. The storm did not end foreign competition over Samoa; the islands were later divided between colonial powers. The more useful lesson is that imperial rivalry distorted risk judgement. What remains debatable is how much each captain’s choice resulted from pride, operational limitation or simple miscalculation.

## The Samoa story that divided anthropology

A different kind of historical puzzle began when American anthropologist Margaret Mead conducted research in the Samoan archipelago during the 1920s. Her 1928 book *Coming of Age in Samoa* argued that adolescence was shaped strongly by culture and that young women in the community she studied experienced less of the turmoil commonly associated with adolescence in the United States.

Decades later, anthropologist Derek Freeman claimed that Mead had fundamentally misunderstood Samoan society and had been misled by young informants who treated her questions as a joke. The resulting dispute became one of anthropology’s most famous controversies and was often presented as a dramatic contest between culture and biology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781118924396.wbiea1361" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiley.com.</span></span></span>

This controversy belongs only partly to Samoa’s national history because Mead’s principal fieldwork took place on Ta‘ū in what is now American Samoa. It remains relevant because outsiders repeatedly used “Samoa” as evidence in arguments about human nature, sexuality and adolescence, sometimes reducing complex island societies to a laboratory for Western debates.

Later scholarly assessments have generally rejected Freeman’s simple “hoax” narrative. Critics found that he selectively represented Mead’s claims, relied heavily on retrospective testimony and treated differences between communities or historical periods as direct disproof. At the same time, Mead’s brief fieldwork, small sample and broad generalisations have also been questioned. One academic reassessment concluded that she sometimes generalised too widely from a limited locality, but that Freeman’s counter-evidence against her central argument was weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781118924396.wbiea1361" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiley.com.</span></span></span>

The best interpretation is therefore not that Mead was either completely vindicated or exposed as the victim of a decisive deception. Her work captured some genuine cultural differences but compressed a complicated society into an accessible argument for an overseas audience. Freeman identified legitimate questions about method, yet overstated the evidence for deliberate trickery.

The enduring mystery is less “What were Samoan teenagers really like?” than “How did such limited evidence acquire global authority?” The controversy demonstrates how Samoa’s history could be filtered through the ambitions and disagreements of visiting researchers, with Samoan voices too often treated as data rather than as participants in interpreting their own society.

## What is genuinely unresolved?

Samoa’s major historical enigmas do not all deserve the same verdict.

**The fate of the *Joyita*’s 25 occupants remains unresolved.** A leak, failed machinery and a mistaken evacuation form the most economical explanation, but no direct evidence shows where or how the passengers died.

**Pulemelei’s general importance is clearer than its exact purpose.** It was a large indigenous centre embedded in a substantial settlement, probably associated with authority, ceremony and developing social hierarchy. Claims of unknown foreign builders or exotic lost civilisations are unnecessary.

**Black Saturday’s institutional responsibility is established.** The unresolved element is individual accountability for Tupua Tamasese’s fatal shooting and the failure of colonial authorities to identify or prosecute the shooter.

**The 1889 cyclone was not supernatural intervention.** The remaining debate concerns the commanders’ judgement and the extent to which imperial rivalry kept vulnerable ships inside Apia harbour.

**The Mead controversy cannot be solved by declaring one anthropologist entirely truthful and the other wholly wrong.** It is a dispute over evidence, generalisation and the power of outsiders to define Samoa for international readers.

The common thread is missing testimony. The *Joyita* carried its witnesses away. Pulemelei’s ceremonies were not recorded in surviving written form. Black Saturday’s investigation stopped short of naming the fatal shooter. Naval officers left records shaped by professional and national reputation, while foreign anthropologists framed Samoan life through their own intellectual battles.

These gaps should invite careful investigation rather than conspiracy. Samoa’s historical mysteries are most compelling when the surviving evidence is allowed to set the limits: enough to reject fantasy, but not always enough to recover every decision, voice or final moment.
