## Introduction

Enforced disappearance is one of [South Sudan]({{ 'what-still-haunts-south-sudans-history/' | relative_url }})'s most painful and unresolved historical and political mysteries because it deliberately removes both a person and the evidence of what happened to them. Families are left without confirmed deaths, burial places, official records or legal remedies. In many cases, the central question is not whether someone was arrested, but what happened after they entered state custody and who ultimately bears responsibility. Since the civil war began in December 2013, United Nations investigators, human rights organisations and survivors have documented repeated patterns of arbitrary arrest, secret detention and disappearance carried out by state security agencies, while many individual cases remain unresolved years later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/afr65/5970/2017/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[amnesty.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalSouth Sudan: Open Letter from Secretary-General Salil Shetty to President Salva Kiir on prolonged detentions, enforc...</span></span></span>

Unlike a conventional missing-person case, enforced disappearance is itself recognised under international law as a continuing violation because authorities conceal a person's fate or whereabouts. As long as that concealment continues, the crime has not ended. This legal principle explains why disappearances remain active historical and human rights questions long after the initial arrest.

## Who made South Sudan's missing people vanish?

South Sudan's disappearances are closely associated with periods of civil war, political rivalry and expanding security powers. Numerous reports identify the country's National Security Service (NSS) and, in some instances, military intelligence as the agencies most frequently accused of carrying out arrests that were followed by prolonged incommunicado detention or complete disappearance. Government authorities have often denied responsibility or declined to disclose detainees' locations, making independent verification extremely difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/report/2020/12/14/what-crime-was-i-paying/abuses-south-sudans-national-security-service" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[hrw.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrw.org.</span></span></span>

For relatives, the uncertainty itself becomes a form of punishment. Without official confirmation of death, families frequently cannot recover remains, arrange burials, settle estates or obtain legal recognition of what happened. Witnesses are often reluctant to speak because of fears of retaliation, while many detention sites remain inaccessible to independent investigators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/report/2020/12/14/what-crime-was-i-paying/abuses-south-sudans-national-security-service" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrw.org.</span></span></span>

This lack of transparency has transformed many disappearances into enduring historical mysteries. The uncertainty rarely stems from complete ignorance that an arrest occurred; instead, it results from missing documentation, contradictory official statements and the absence of credible investigations.

## Disappearances during war and state repression

The outbreak of conflict in December 2013 created conditions in which arrests linked to perceived political opposition became widespread. Amnesty International documented hundreds of arbitrary detentions, prolonged incommunicado imprisonment and enforced disappearances, particularly involving individuals accused of supporting opposition groups or criticising the government. Former detainees described being held without judicial oversight, denied access to lawyers or relatives, and subjected to torture or other ill-treatment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/afr65/5970/2017/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty InternationalSouth Sudan: Open Letter from Secretary-General Salil Shetty to President Salva Kiir on prolonged detentions, enforc...</span></span></span>

These practices created a recurring pattern rather than isolated incidents. Human Rights Watch concluded that the NSS operated with broad powers, weak judicial oversight and extensive legal immunities, allowing abuses to occur with little prospect of accountability. The organisation also documented how fear of secret detention became an effective tool for silencing journalists, activists, politicians and ordinary citizens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/report/2020/12/14/what-crime-was-i-paying/abuses-south-sudans-national-security-service" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrw.org.</span></span></span>

The UN Commission on Human Rights in South Sudan reached similar conclusions. Investigators found reasonable grounds to believe that many detainees had effectively been subjected to enforced disappearance because authorities failed to acknowledge detention, kept inadequate custody records, prevented contact with families and, in some instances, disposed of bodies without proper documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.right-docs.org/doc/a-hrc-40-crp.1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: right-docs.org">[RightDocs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">right-docs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RightDocs40/CRP.1 Report of the Commission on Human Rights in South Sudan / RightDocs - Where human rights resolutions count...</span></span></span>

## The cases of Dong Samuel Luak and Aggrey Idri

No disappearance better illustrates the unresolved questions surrounding state responsibility than those of Dong Samuel Luak and Aggrey Idri.

Dong Samuel Luak was a South Sudanese lawyer, human rights advocate and recognised refugee living in Kenya. Aggrey Idri was an opposition official associated with the movement led by Riek Machar. Both disappeared separately from the streets of Nairobi on 23 and 24 January 2017 after being seized by unidentified men. Almost immediately, concerns emerged that the operation had crossed international borders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2017/04/24/three-months-still-no-word-disappeared-south-sudanese-men" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights WatchThree Months, Still No Word on Disappeared South Sudanese Men &#124; Human Rights WatchApril 23, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2017</span></span></span>

Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International reported credible evidence that both men were subsequently transferred illegally to Juba and held inside NSS facilities. Witnesses reportedly saw them in detention during the final week of January 2017 before they disappeared again. Kenyan courts had already ruled against their deportation, making their reported transfer especially controversial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2018/05/02/south-sudan-reveal-fate-and-whereabouts-2-men" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[hrw.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights WatchSouth Sudan: Reveal Fate and Whereabouts of 2 Men &#124; Human Rights WatchMay 2, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2, 2018</span></span></span>

For more than two years, neither government publicly acknowledged what had happened. Families continued searching while officials denied knowledge of the men's whereabouts. The absence of records, combined with conflicting official accounts, turned the case into one of East Africa's most prominent unresolved disappearances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2019/02/12/kenya-south-sudan-investigate-critics-disappearance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrw.org.</span></span></span>

In 2019, the UN Commission on Human Rights in South Sudan concluded that it was "highly probable" the two men had been unlawfully executed after being held by state security officials. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International called for independent criminal investigations, but no comprehensive public accounting has resolved the case or identified those responsible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2019/04/30/south-sudan-investigate-apparent-2017-killing-activists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">south sudan investigate apparent 2017 killing activists</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">south sudan investigate apparent 2017 killing activists</span></span></span>

The mystery therefore shifted over time. The original question—where are Dong Samuel Luak and Aggrey Idri?—became a broader question of who authorised their transfer, detention and probable killing, and why no transparent investigation has followed.

## Why responsibility remains hidden

One reason these disappearances remain unresolved is the difficulty of tracing responsibility through security institutions.

International investigators have repeatedly pointed to several obstacles:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* detainees were often not entered into reliable custody registers;
* detention facilities operated with limited independent oversight;
* relatives were frequently denied information about prisoners' locations;
* witnesses feared retaliation for providing testimony;
* legal immunities protected many security officials from prosecution without executive approval.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/report/2020/12/14/what-crime-was-i-paying/abuses-south-sudans-national-security-service" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[hrw.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrw.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

These conditions make it difficult to distinguish between direct perpetrators, local commanders and senior officials responsible through chains of command. International criminal law recognises that responsibility may extend beyond those carrying out an arrest if commanders knew, or should have known, about unlawful conduct and failed to prevent or punish it. Determining those command relationships, however, requires documentary evidence and witness testimony that remain largely unavailable.

The resulting uncertainty has encouraged competing narratives. Government officials have frequently denied involvement, while victims' families, rights organisations and UN investigators have argued that the available evidence points towards state responsibility. The absence of transparent judicial proceedings has prevented these competing claims from being fully tested in court.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2019/02/12/kenya-south-sudan-investigate-critics-disappearance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[hrw.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrw.org.</span></span></span>

## Why missing records matter as much as missing people

Historical mysteries often survive because documents disappear alongside individuals. South Sudan's disappearance cases demonstrate this pattern vividly.

The absence of arrest warrants, custody logs, transfer orders and burial records prevents investigators from reconstructing events with certainty. Even when eyewitnesses exist, their testimony may be challenged because official documentation is incomplete or contradictory. Families therefore confront two simultaneous losses: the disappearance of a loved one and the disappearance of the administrative record that could explain what happened.

The UN Commission noted that inconsistent registration practices and the absence of reliable detention records significantly complicated efforts to establish responsibility. In some allegations, investigators also raised concerns that bodies may have been buried anonymously or disposed of in ways that eliminated forensic evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.right-docs.org/doc/a-hrc-40-crp.1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: right-docs.org">[RightDocs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">right-docs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RightDocs40/CRP.1 Report of the Commission on Human Rights in South Sudan / RightDocs - Where human rights resolutions count...</span></span></span>

## The unresolved legacy of Disappearances
South Sudan's enforced disappearances remain unresolved not because nothing is known, but because critical pieces of evidence remain concealed or inaccessible. Independent investigations have identified recurring patterns, credible witnesses and probable perpetrators in some cases, yet public accountability has remained limited.

For families, the mystery is deeply personal: confirmation of death, recovery of remains and acknowledgement of responsibility are still absent. For historians, the disappearances illustrate how modern conflicts can create gaps in the historical record that persist long after fighting subsides. And for South Sudan itself, these unresolved cases continue to symbolise the unfinished struggle to establish transparent institutions capable of preserving records, investigating abuses and providing justice for victims.
