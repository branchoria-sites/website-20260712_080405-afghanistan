## Introduction

Two of Trinidad's most important archaeological sites, Caurita and Banwari Trace, preserve rare clues to the island's Indigenous past, yet neither offers straightforward answers. The Caurita petroglyphs are Trinidad's only confirmed pre-Columbian rock carvings, while Banwari Trace has produced the oldest known human remains and one of the earliest archaeological settlements in the Caribbean. Together they reveal that people lived, travelled, buried their dead and created symbolic art on the island thousands of years before European colonisation. What remains uncertain is not whether these communities existed, but how they understood the images they carved, why particular burial practices were chosen, and how their beliefs and identities changed over millennia. The greatest mystery is therefore one of lost knowledge: the archaeological evidence survives, but much of the cultural tradition that once explained it was broken by conquest, disease and colonial displacement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5644/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Banwari Trace Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreBanwari Trace Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreAugust 17, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 2011</span></span></span>

## What archaeologists have securely established

Caurita and Banwari Trace preserve very different kinds of evidence, but both are regarded as exceptional archaeological sites.

At Caurita, in the Maracas Valley, a large boulder bears deliberately carved geometric and curvilinear figures. Archaeologists identify the carvings as a pre-Columbian petroglyph rather than a natural rock formation. Although rock art is known elsewhere in the Caribbean and northern South America, confirmed examples are extremely rare in Trinidad, making Caurita unique within the island's archaeological record. The carvings demonstrate that symbolic or ceremonial expression formed part of Indigenous life, even though their precise purpose cannot now be recovered with confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sta.uwi.edu/fhe/history/archaeology-centre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sta.uwi.edu">[The University of the West Indies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sta.uwi.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The University of the West Indies The Archaeology Centre &#124; Department of History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The University of the West Indies The Archaeology Centre &#124; Department of History</span></span></span>

Banwari Trace, by contrast, preserves an ancient settlement rather than rock art. Excavations beginning in 1969 uncovered shell middens, stone and bone tools, fishing equipment, grinding stones and evidence of repeated occupation over thousands of years. Radiocarbon dating places the earliest occupation around 7000 years before present (roughly 5000 BC), making it the oldest known pre-Columbian archaeological site in the Caribbean. The site's changing shell deposits also record environmental change as the nearby Oropuche Lagoon gradually shifted from freshwater conditions towards mangrove and marine habitats following post-glacial sea-level rise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5644/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Banwari Trace Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreBanwari Trace Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreAugust 17, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 2011</span></span></span>

Among Banwari Trace's most significant discoveries is a crouched human burial found only about 20 centimetres below the surface. The individual lay on the left side with very few grave goods—a rounded pebble near the skull and a pointed implement near the hip. Stratigraphic evidence places the burial towards the later phase of occupation, approximately 3400 BC rather than at the site's earliest settlement. This distinction is important because it shows that the famous burial does not necessarily represent the first inhabitants of the site, but a community already established there for many centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5644/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Banwari Trace Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreBanwari Trace Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreAugust 17, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 2011</span></span></span>

## Why the carvings and burial remain open to interpretation

Neither Caurita nor Banwari Trace presents a mystery because the evidence is doubtful. Instead, the uncertainty lies in interpretation.

### The Caurita carvings

Researchers broadly agree that the petroglyphs were intentionally created, but there is no surviving Indigenous explanation for their meaning.

Several interpretations have been proposed over the years:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* They may have marked a ceremonial or sacred place.
* They could have expressed spiritual beliefs through symbolic imagery.
* Some researchers have suggested they indicated territorial identity or gathering places.
* Others have explored links with wider Caribbean and northern South American rock-art traditions.

</div>

None of these explanations has gained universal acceptance because there is no accompanying written tradition and no secure way to translate the images as though they formed a script. Similar-looking designs elsewhere do not necessarily share identical meanings. Claims that the carvings represent astronomical maps, hidden treasure or a lost civilisation have no convincing archaeological support and extend well beyond the available evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sta.uwi.edu/fhe/history/archaeology-centre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sta.uwi.edu">[The University of the West Indies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sta.uwi.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The University of the West Indies The Archaeology Centre &#124; Department of History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The University of the West Indies The Archaeology Centre &#124; Department of History</span></span></span>

### The Banwari burial

The Banwari burial raises different questions. The crouched position is widely recognised as a deliberate funerary practice rather than an accidental posture, but archaeologists continue to debate what it symbolised.

Interpretations include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a customary burial position shared with other early Indigenous societies;
* a ritual connected with beliefs about death or rebirth;
* practical traditions passed through generations rather than symbolic ones.

</div>

The two associated objects—a pebble and a pointed implement—are intriguing because they appear intentional, yet they are too few to reveal a complete burial ritual. They may have held symbolic value, served practical purposes, or simply reflected local customs that varied between communities. Without comparable written accounts, archaeologists cannot determine which explanation is correct.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5644/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Banwari Trace Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreBanwari Trace Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreAugust 17, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 2011</span></span></span>

An additional point of discussion concerns the individual themselves. Popular references long used the nickname "Banwari Man", but later work has questioned whether the remains may instead belong to a woman. The uncertainty illustrates how early archaeological labels sometimes become embedded in public memory even after later research encourages greater caution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uwispace.sta.uwi.edu/bitstreams/2eca4c89-383f-4926-a0af-28cc2e8fe4e3/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwispace.sta.uwi.edu">[uwispace.sta.uwi.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwispace.sta.uwi.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The University of the West Indies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The University of the West Indies</span></span></span>

## How colonisation disrupted the knowledge needed to interpret them

The greatest obstacle to interpreting both sites is historical rather than archaeological.

When Europeans reached Trinidad, centuries of disease, forced labour, displacement and cultural disruption profoundly affected Indigenous communities. Languages disappeared, oral traditions were interrupted and ceremonial practices changed or were suppressed. Even where descendants of Trinidad's First Peoples preserved aspects of their heritage, much specialised knowledge about ancient sacred places and symbolic traditions had already been lost before systematic archaeology began.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sta.uwi.edu/fhe/history/archaeology-centre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sta.uwi.edu">[The University of the West Indies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sta.uwi.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The University of the West Indies The Archaeology Centre &#124; Department of History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The University of the West Indies The Archaeology Centre &#124; Department of History</span></span></span>

This means archaeologists must rely primarily on physical evidence—stone tools, burials, settlement layers and carved rock—rather than on explanations handed down directly from the people who created them. Comparisons with neighbouring regions in South America and the wider Caribbean provide useful context, especially because Trinidad was connected to the South American mainland during the earliest phases of settlement. However, similarities cannot prove identical beliefs or customs. Every comparison remains an informed inference rather than a definitive translation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5644/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Banwari Trace Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreBanwari Trace Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreAugust 17, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 2011</span></span></span>

The absence of written testimony also explains why many apparently simple questions remain unanswered. Archaeologists can often determine when an object was made or how it was deposited, but not necessarily what it meant to the people who created it.

## Why these sites still matter

Caurita and Banwari Trace are sometimes grouped together because both preserve Indigenous clues, yet they illuminate different aspects of Trinidad's earliest history.

Banwari Trace demonstrates extraordinary antiquity. It confirms that people occupied what is now Trinidad thousands of years before pottery-making societies emerged and provides evidence for early migration routes from mainland South America into the Caribbean. The site's environmental record also helps reconstruct changing coastlines and ancient ways of life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5644/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Banwari Trace Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreBanwari Trace Archaeological Site - UNESCO World Heritage CentreAugust 17, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 2011</span></span></span>

Caurita contributes something equally valuable but less tangible. It preserves one of the few surviving examples of Indigenous symbolic expression on the island. Even if the carvings can never be translated with certainty, they remind modern observers that Trinidad's earliest inhabitants possessed complex traditions that extended beyond everyday survival into ritual, identity and artistic expression.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sta.uwi.edu/fhe/history/archaeology-centre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sta.uwi.edu">[The University of the West Indies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sta.uwi.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The University of the West Indies The Archaeology Centre &#124; Department of History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The University of the West Indies The Archaeology Centre &#124; Department of History</span></span></span>

Taken together, the two sites show that the enduring mystery is not the existence of Trinidad's earliest communities but the partial silence surrounding their ideas. Archaeology continues to recover objects, dates and environmental evidence, yet the stories that once gave those clues their full meaning remain only partly recoverable. That combination of remarkable preservation and irreversible cultural loss is what makes Caurita and Banwari Trace among [Trinidad and Tobago]({{ 'which-trinidad-and-tobago-mysteries/' | relative_url }})'s most significant historical enigmas.
