## El Dorado: a ritual transformed into a golden city

El Dorado is often imagined as a lost city hidden somewhere in the South American interior. The earliest Colombian strand of the story was different. Accounts recorded after the Spanish conquest described an investiture ceremony associated with the Muisca people and Lake Guatavita, north-east of present-day Bogotá. A new ruler was said to travel on a raft, make offerings and enter the water after being covered in gold dust. Over time, Europeans turned the “gilded man” into a kingdom, then a city, and finally an entire landscape supposedly overflowing with precious metal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://colombia.travel/en/encanto/guatavita-real-lake-and-legend-el-dorado" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colombia.travel">[Colombia Travel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colombia.travel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guatavita: the real lake and the legend of El DoradoThe legend of El Dorado is that the Muisca chief would arrive at the lake on a wooden...</span></span></span>

There is real evidence behind the legend. The Muisca were highly skilled metalworkers who deposited gold-alloy objects in caves, lakes and other sacred places. The famous Muisca raft, found near Pasca in 1969 and now held by Bogotá’s Gold Museum, depicts an elaborate ceremony involving a central figure and attendants aboard a raft. Its imagery is compatible with traditions of chiefly ritual and votive offering, although scholars caution that it does not necessarily illustrate one particular event at Guatavita.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstreams/f908fef3-39a1-43bf-8605-c2423881c0d3/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.cam.ac.uk">[Cambridge University Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">bridge University RepositoryThe Muisca Raft: Context, Materiality and TechnologyFebruary 2, 2022 — by MA Uribe Villegas · 2021 · Cited...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 2, 2022</span></span></span>

Recent archaeological work around Lake Guatavita supports its importance as a Muisca ritual landscape. It does not, however, confirm the enormous treasure imagined by conquistadors and later adventurers. The surviving evidence is more consistent with repeated sacred offerings than with a royal vault or submerged city. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century chroniclers also wrote after Indigenous society had been violently disrupted, so their descriptions cannot simply be treated as eyewitness transcripts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373351164_El_Dorado_Offerings_in_Lake_Guatavita_A_Muisca_Ritual_Archaeological_Site" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate El Dorado Offerings in Lake Guatavita: A Muisca Ritual</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El Dorado Offerings in Lake Guatavita: A Muisca Ritual...August 23, 2023 — 3 Sept 2023 — The fame of Lake Guatavita as the m...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 23, 2023</span></span></span>

The mystery is therefore not whether a golden metropolis awaits discovery. Almost certainly it does not. The harder questions concern how the ceremonies actually worked, how often they occurred and how much Spanish accounts preserved rather than reshaped. El Dorado remains important because it shows how an Indigenous religious practice was converted into a European treasure fantasy—one that encouraged invasion, forced exploration and repeated attempts to drain Guatavita.

## What do the statues of San Agustín mean?

In the Upper Magdalena region of southern Colombia, hundreds of stone sculptures stand near burial mounds, tombs, terraces and ceremonial waterworks. Some combine human bodies with fangs, animal features or doubled figures. Others appear to represent guardians, ancestors, ritual specialists or supernatural beings. UNESCO describes San Agustín as the largest surviving group of megalithic funerary monuments and religious sculpture in South America.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/744/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSan Agustín Archaeological ParkThe tombs contain an elaborate funerary architecture of stone corridors, colum...</span></span></span>

The sculptures were produced by societies whose history cannot be reconstructed from a surviving written record. Archaeology shows that people occupied the region for thousands of years and that monument-building developed within a long sequence of farming communities. The best-known statues and monumental tombs belong broadly to the first millennium AD, but San Agustín was not a single city created at one moment, nor necessarily the work of one unified “lost civilisation”. It was a changing sacred landscape used by communities spread across the Upper Magdalena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icanh.gov.co/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/sanagustin_en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icanh.gov.co">[icanh.gov.co]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icanh.gov.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">San Agustín</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Magdalena region. (González, 2007). Page 16. San Agustín / National Archaeological Park / Guidebook &#124; 15 hunter-gatherer...Read more...</span></span></span>

This matters because popular accounts sometimes present the figures as products of an inexplicable vanished race. The archaeological problem is subtler. Researchers can analyse their placement, carving styles, associated pottery, tomb architecture and evidence of social hierarchy. They can infer that the monuments connected death, ancestry, political authority and supernatural power. They cannot translate each image as though it belonged to a known written mythology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/744/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSan Agustín Archaeological ParkThe tombs contain an elaborate funerary architecture of stone corridors, colum...</span></span></span>

The most striking figures may therefore remain permanently open to interpretation. Fanged mouths could refer to transformation, predatory animals, ritual masks or several ideas at once. Double beings may represent spiritual companions, altered states or relations between living rulers and ancestors. Claims that the sculptures depict visitors from other continents—or beings from outer space—add no explanatory value and are unsupported by the excavation record.

San Agustín is a genuine mystery because much of its symbolic language is inaccessible, not because its makers were unknowable. The monuments reveal local engineering, artistic traditions and organised ceremonial life. What is missing is the oral and ritual context that once made their meanings obvious.

## Was Ciudad Perdida really a lost city?

Ciudad Perdida, in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, is frequently described as Colombia’s answer to a legendary vanished city. It was built by ancestors of the region’s present-day Indigenous peoples and contains terraces, roads, stairways, drainage works and circular building platforms adapted to steep, wet mountain terrain. UNESCO documentation records 169 terraces and an extensive system of stone-built infrastructure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/documents/publi_wh_papers_07_en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">publi wh papers 07 en</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">publi wh papers 07 en</span></span></span>

Its modern name encourages a misleading picture. The settlement disappeared from the knowledge of the Colombian state and wider archaeological world, but the Sierra Nevada was never an empty wilderness without historical memory. Indigenous communities continued to regard the landscape as sacred. The site became widely known to outsiders after grave robbers reached it in the 1970s; archaeological work followed, and written accounts commonly date its official recognition to 1976.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark%3A/48223/pf0000131634" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesdoc.unesco.org">[UNESCO Digital Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesdoc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The real questions concern the settlement’s role within a wider network. It was not an isolated wonder but one of several complex centres connected by paths and shared architectural traditions. Archaeologists continue to investigate how political authority, exchange, agriculture and ritual were organised across the Sierra Nevada before Spanish colonisation and demographic collapse disrupted the region.

Calling Ciudad Perdida “lost” is therefore reasonable only from the viewpoint of national institutions that had ceased to recognise it. The label becomes misleading when it erases Indigenous continuity or suggests that the settlement’s builders vanished without descendants.

## The San José: shipwreck, grave and disputed inheritance

On 8 June 1708, during the War of the Spanish Succession, the Spanish galleon San José encountered a British squadron near Cartagena. The ship exploded or suffered catastrophic damage during the battle and sank rapidly. Hundreds of sailors, soldiers and passengers died. Because it was carrying gold, silver, emeralds and other colonial cargo, the vessel became one of the world’s most sought-after wrecks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2024/04/01/off-the-coast-of-colombia-the-san-jose-shipwreck-and-its-coveted-gold_6667025_4.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Le Monde.fr Off the coast of Colombia, the San José shipwreck and its coveted gold</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le Monde.fr Off the coast of Colombia, the San José shipwreck and its coveted gold</span></span></span>

Colombia announced in 2015 that it had located the wreck at a depth of roughly 600 metres. Photographs of distinctive cannons and other objects supported its identification. Yet the precise location remains restricted, partly to protect the site from looting. A United States-linked salvage company has maintained that its predecessors identified the wreck area decades earlier, while Spain has invoked the status of a state vessel. Indigenous representatives from Bolivia have argued that wealth extracted from their ancestors’ territories and labour cannot ethically be treated simply as Spanish or Colombian property.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2024/04/01/off-the-coast-of-colombia-the-san-jose-shipwreck-and-its-coveted-gold_6667025_4.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[lemonde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Le Monde.fr Off the coast of Colombia, the San José shipwreck and its coveted gold</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le Monde.fr Off the coast of Colombia, the San José shipwreck and its coveted gold</span></span></span>

The vast valuations attached to the cargo should be treated cautiously. They depend on incomplete manifests, uncertain survival rates and hypothetical auction prices. More importantly, reducing the wreck to a sum of money obscures both the people who died and the colonial system that produced the cargo.

Colombia designated the location a protected archaeological area in 2024 and presented its programme as scientific research rather than commercial salvage. An initial non-invasive expedition mapped and photographed the site. In 2025, researchers recovered a cannon, three coins and a porcelain cup for conservation and analysis, while critics questioned whether removing material was necessary or compatible with the principle that underwater heritage should normally be preserved in place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/cb956b79763fc6a69969a6695794e228" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

Several mysteries now overlap:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **How exactly did the vessel sink?** An explosion is the leading explanation, but researchers are examining whether damage to the hull or another sequence of failures contributed.
* **What remains aboard?** Images reveal scattered cargo and equipment, but no full archaeological inventory exists.
* **Who may decide its future?** Colombia, Spain, private claimants and communities linked to the extracted wealth approach ownership from incompatible legal and moral positions.
* **Should it be raised at all?** Recovery can generate knowledge, but exposure to oxygen and handling can destroy objects preserved for three centuries underwater.

</div>

The San José is no longer a lost ship in the ordinary sense. Its enduring mystery lies in what the site contains, what happened during its final minutes and whether it should be treated primarily as treasure, scientific evidence, a naval grave or the material record of colonial exploitation.

## Who killed Jorge Eliécer Gaitán?

On 9 April 1948, Liberal leader Jorge Eliécer Gaitán was shot after leaving his office in central Bogotá. A crowd seized the suspected gunman, Juan Roa Sierra, and killed him before he could be properly interrogated or tried. The assassination triggered the uprising and destruction known as the Bogotazo and became a defining threshold in Colombia’s twentieth-century political violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d330" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

The basic sequence is well established: Gaitán was attacked in public, Roa Sierra was identified at the scene and the suspect died in the immediate chaos. What remains unresolved is motive and possible sponsorship. Roa Sierra’s death eliminated the person best placed to explain whether he acted alone, was manipulated or belonged to a wider plot.

Theories have accused Conservative interests, rival Liberals, communists, foreign intelligence services and combinations of political or police actors. The timing—during a major inter-American conference in Bogotá—and Gaitán’s popularity helped international conspiracy theories flourish. Yet no surviving documentary trail has established that the CIA, Soviet agents or a Colombian political faction ordered the murder. A later United States intelligence assessment judged a private, irrational act by Roa Sierra to be very probable, although such an assessment cannot itself close the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/The-Bogotazo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bogotazo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Bogotazo</span></span></span>

The lone-gunman explanation is plausible because Roa Sierra appears to have been unstable, economically desperate and preoccupied with Gaitán. But plausible is not the same as proven. Witness accounts were gathered amid riots, the suspect’s movements were never fully clarified and the investigation became entangled with the political struggle over responsibility for the Bogotazo.

The assassination became more than a criminal case because many Colombians saw Gaitán as a possible route towards peaceful social reform. The unanswered question—whether his death was the act of one disturbed man or the removal of a threatening national leader—became a vessel for wider distrust of the political system. That public meaning explains why the case still generates fiction, historical debate and conspiracy claims even when new evidence is scarce.

## The disappeared of the Palace of Justice

On 6 November 1985, members of the M-19 guerrilla movement seized Colombia’s Palace of Justice in Bogotá. The armed forces retook the building in a destructive operation that ended the following day. Judges, employees, visitors, combatants and soldiers were among the dead. In the aftermath, relatives reported that several people—particularly cafeteria workers—had survived the building and then vanished in state custody.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cejil.org/en/press-releases/colombia-should-be-held-liable-for-violating-human-rights-in-the-case-of-missing-persons-during-the-palace-of-justice-siege-in-colombia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cejil.org">[Cejil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cejil.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cejil.org.</span></span></span>

This is not a mystery that can responsibly be framed as paranormal or romantic. It is an unresolved human-rights case involving missing people, contradictory official accounts and the long struggle of their families. Film footage and testimony showed some survivors leaving the palace and being taken to a nearby military-controlled location. Colombia’s Truth Commission concluded that several of the missing had survived the retaking, been detained and subsequently disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cejil.org/en/press-releases/colombia-should-be-held-liable-for-violating-human-rights-in-the-case-of-missing-persons-during-the-palace-of-justice-siege-in-colombia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cejil.org">[Cejil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cejil.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cejil.org.</span></span></span>

In 2014, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights found Colombia internationally responsible for forced disappearances and other violations connected to the operation. The ruling established state responsibility in important parts of the case, but it did not answer every question about each person’s fate, the full chain of command or the handling and identification of remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.corteidh.or.cr/docs/casos/articulos/seriec_287_ing.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: corteidh.or.cr">[corteidh.or.cr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">corteidh.or.cr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on or.cr.</span></span></span>

Confusion was deepened by fire, damaged evidence, misidentified bodies, disputed forensic work and years of institutional resistance. Later identifications corrected some earlier assumptions, showing that not every person initially listed as disappeared had shared the same fate. That does not erase the established disappearances; it demonstrates why each victim’s case requires careful individual evidence rather than one sweeping narrative.

The Palace of Justice remains one of Colombia’s most painful archival mysteries because the uncertainty was not produced only by battlefield chaos. It was compounded by secrecy, unreliable records and delayed investigation. The unresolved issue is less whether abuses occurred than whether the country will ever recover a complete account of who was taken, who gave the orders and where all the victims were left.

## Why these mysteries endure

Colombia’s historical enigmas persist for different reasons, and placing them in one sensational category can hide more than it reveals.

**Colonial distortion** transformed Muisca offerings into the fantasy of El Dorado. European writers preserved useful clues while filtering Indigenous ritual through expectations of limitless gold.

**Loss of cultural interpretation** makes San Agustín difficult to read. Archaeology can date monuments and reconstruct social practices, but it cannot fully replace the spoken traditions that explained their symbols.

**Distance and preservation** protected the San José while preventing direct study. Its depth helped keep the wreck intact, but it also allowed legal claims and imagined valuations to outrun the archaeological evidence.

**Destroyed witnesses and compromised investigations** sustain political mysteries. Roa Sierra’s lynching made a conclusive account of Gaitán’s murder far harder. At the Palace of Justice, damaged records and institutional obstruction deepened uncertainty for victims’ families.

**Public memory reshapes every case.** El Dorado became a symbol of both wonder and colonial greed. San Agustín became evidence of an impressive pre-Columbian past. Gaitán’s death came to represent a national future violently interrupted. The Palace of Justice became a test of whether the state could acknowledge crimes committed during its response to insurgency.

The best-supported explanations are rarely the most extravagant. El Dorado was rooted in real ritual but not a golden city. Ciudad Perdida was unknown to national archaeology, not abandoned by all human memory. San Agustín’s figures are difficult to interpret, not evidence of an alien civilisation. The San José is an archaeological site and mass grave, not merely a treasure chest. Gaitán may have been killed by a lone attacker, but the failures of investigation prevent certainty. The Palace of Justice disappearances are not speculative folklore: substantial judicial and testimonial evidence shows that state agents detained people who were never safely returned.

Taken together, these cases reveal why mystery matters in Colombian history. The gaps are not empty spaces waiting for fantasy. They show where conquest silenced knowledge, where conflict destroyed evidence, where institutions failed victims and where ownership of the past remains unsettled.
