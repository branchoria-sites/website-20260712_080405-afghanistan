## Was Zorats Karer really an ancient observatory?

On a high plateau near Sisian in southern Armenia stands a striking complex of upright basalt stones. Often promoted as the “Armenian Stonehenge”, Zorats Karer consists of stone alignments, a central enclosure, burial structures and numerous blocks pierced by carefully made holes. Its age, changing uses and unusual construction have encouraged claims that it was one of the world’s earliest astronomical observatories.

The astronomical interpretation developed partly from attempts to match the holes and stone alignments with the Sun, Moon and prominent stars. Some Armenian researchers proposed that prehistoric observers looked through the openings to mark solstices, equinoxes or celestial events. The possibility is not absurd: ancient societies elsewhere demonstrably incorporated astronomical alignments into ritual architecture. Yet an alignment identified today is not automatically proof of deliberate observation. With many stones and possible sight lines, coincidental matches become increasingly likely.

Archaeologists have generally offered a less spectacular but better-supported explanation. Investigations have identified large stone tombs and material associated with a cemetery used over several periods from the Bronze Age into the Iron Age. Pavel Avetisyan of Armenia’s National Academy of Sciences has described the monument as a multilayered, multipurpose archaeological landscape rather than a structure with one simple function. The holes may have served practical purposes connected with transport, securing ropes or attaching wooden elements; their original role has not been conclusively established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://novoscriptorium.com/2019/03/11/megalithic-complex-zorats-karer-carahunge-in-armenia-a-bronze-age-iron-age-necropolis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: novoscriptorium.com">[Novo Scriptorium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">novoscriptorium.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Novo Scriptorium Megalithic complex &#x27;Zorats Karer&#x27; (Carahunge) in Armenia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Novo ScriptoriumMegalithic complex &#x27;Zorats Karer&#x27; (Carahunge) in ArmeniaMarch 11, 2019 — 11 Mar 2019 — Zorats Karer was investigated in 2...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2019</span></span></span>

The best current assessment is therefore cautious. Zorats Karer was certainly a major prehistoric ceremonial and funerary site. Some astronomical awareness may have influenced its construction, but claims that it was a precisely engineered observatory thousands of years older than better-documented examples have not been demonstrated. A critical review of the proposed stellar alignments found the dating methods and astronomical conclusions unsustainable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Karahunge-the-Armenian-Stonehenge_fig1_253521460" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Karahunge, the “Armenian Stonehenge”.Carahunge is a megalithic monument in southern Armenia that has often been acclaimed as...</span></span></span>

The continuing argument reveals how national memory can shape archaeological interpretation. The “observatory” story presents ancient Armenia as a centre of advanced scientific knowledge, while the cemetery interpretation sounds less exceptional to a general audience. In reality, a monumental burial landscape constructed and reused by prehistoric communities is no less historically important. The genuine mystery is not whether the stones conceal a lost scientific civilisation, but how funerary ritual, territorial identity and perhaps observation of the sky were combined at the site.

## What happened inside Areni-1 Cave?

[Areni-1]({{ 'areni-1/' | relative_url }}) Cave in the Vayots Dzor region preserves an unusually intimate record of life more than 5,000 years ago. Its dry, stable environment protected organic materials that normally decay, including plant remains, textiles and a remarkably complete leather shoe dated to approximately 3600–3500 BC. Researchers also found installations associated with wine production dating to around 4100–4000 BC: a pressing basin, fermentation or collection vessels, storage jars, grape remains and chemical traces consistent with red wine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6967/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreAreni-1 (Birds&#x27;) Cave: Prehistoric Life and Winemaking in...The cave preserves the earliest known archaeolog...</span></span></span>

These discoveries are well established. What remains uncertain is why wine was being produced in the cave and how the different activities found there fitted together.

The wine installation stood near burials and vessels containing human skulls. Drinking cups and other deposits suggest that wine may have played a part in ceremonies for the dead. Archaeological research describes Areni-1 not merely as a workshop but as a place used for storage, keeping animals, food preparation, habitation and ritual practices at different times.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259334407_Areni-1_Cave_Armenia_A_Chalcolithic-Early_Bronze_Age_settlement_and_ritual_site_in_the_southern_Caucasus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) Areni-1 Cave, Armenia: A Chalcolithic–Early Bronze</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">onwards, people used Areni-1 Cave for habitation and for keeping goats, storing plant foods, and ritual purposes;...Read more...</span></span></span>

That combination makes simple explanations difficult. The cave could have served a community that periodically gathered for funerals or commemorative feasts. Wine might have been offered to the dead, consumed by participants or used in ceremonies marking social status. It is also possible that practical production and sacred activity occupied neighbouring areas without belonging to one unified ritual system.

The famous shoe adds another unresolved detail. It was found carefully positioned in a plastered pit beneath an overturned vessel, with goat horns and pottery nearby. The shoe may simply have been stored and forgotten, but its setting leaves open the possibility of deliberate deposition. Archaeologists cannot determine whether it belonged to an ordinary individual, was placed as an offering or became part of a ceremony whose meaning is now unrecoverable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areni-1_shoe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Areni-1 shoe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Areni-1 shoe</span></span></span>

Areni-1 is sometimes presented as though it merely settled a competition over the “world’s oldest winery”. Its deeper importance is that it preserves a social puzzle. The technology of winemaking can be reconstructed from residues and equipment; the beliefs surrounding it cannot. The evidence strongly suggests that wine, death and communal ritual were connected, but no surviving text explains how.

## Why did prehistoric Armenians raise “dragon stones”?

Across Armenia’s mountains and adjoining parts of the South Caucasus, archaeologists have documented tall basalt monuments carved with images of fish, cattle hides or flowing forms. Later tradition associated these stones with dragons, but their builders left no written explanation of what they represented.

Many stand in high-altitude pastures near springs, streams and early water-management systems. Their remote locations made their construction an enormous undertaking: communities had to quarry, shape, transport and erect multi-ton blocks during the short season when mountain routes were accessible. Their placement was therefore unlikely to have been casual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01998-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

For years, dating was one of the central difficulties. Some stones had been moved or reused, destroying their original archaeological setting. One monument at Lchashen was found above a burial from the sixteenth century BC, but researchers concluded that the stone itself had a longer and more complicated history. Such reuse shows why an associated grave does not necessarily reveal when a monument was first carved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X24002293" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

A major statistical study published in 2025 analysed 115 examples, comparing their dimensions, altitude and relationship to water sources. Its results strengthened the interpretation that the stones belonged to a prehistoric cult centred on water, fertility and seasonal use of mountain landscapes. The concentrations at particular elevations also suggest deliberate choices rather than random survival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01998-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

This does not mean the mystery has been completely “solved”. Statistical patterns can reveal where communities placed monuments and how much effort they invested, but they cannot supply a lost mythology. Fish imagery may have represented water, divine power, abundance or transformation. Cattle-hide forms may have referred to sacrifice, pastoral wealth or a ritual connection between animals and springs.

The dragon stones are therefore a good example of a mystery becoming narrower rather than disappearing. Archaeology increasingly supports a relationship with water and highland ritual. What remains unknown is the identity of the beings honoured there, the ceremonies performed and the stories through which prehistoric communities understood the monuments.

## How were the Zeytun Gospels torn apart?

The Zeytun Gospels were produced in 1256 and illuminated by Toros Roslin, one of the most celebrated artists of medieval Armenian manuscript culture. For centuries, the book was kept by the Armenian community of Zeytun, in what is now Turkey. During the violence and mass displacement of the Armenian Genocide, the manuscript began a journey through hiding places, refugee routes and diaspora communities.

At some point, eight richly illuminated pages known as the Canon Tables were separated from the rest of the volume. The exact circumstances are unknown. The separation appears to have occurred during or shortly after the period of persecution and displacement between 1915 and 1923, but no secure account identifies who removed the pages, whether the act was theft, emergency protection or a mixture of both.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://plone.unige.ch/art-adr/cases-affaires/canon-tables-2013-western-prelacy-of-the-armenian-apostolic-church-of-america-and-j-paul-getty-museum/case-note-2013-canon-tables-2013-western-prelacy-of-the-armenian-apostolic-church-of-america-and-j-paul-getty-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: plone.unige.ch">[Plone UNIGE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">plone.unige.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Plone UNIGECanon Tables – Western Prelacy of the Armenian Apostolic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Plone UNIGECanon Tables – Western Prelacy of the Armenian Apostolic</span></span></span>

The main body of the manuscript eventually reached the Armenian church’s collection in Yerevan. The missing pages followed another route and were later acquired by the J. Paul Getty Museum in Los Angeles. In 2010, the Western Prelacy of the Armenian Apostolic Church sued the museum, maintaining that the leaves had been wrongfully separated during the genocide and that the Church remained their owner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eurasianet.org/armenia-church-sues-getty-museum-to-reclaim-armenian-cultural-heritage" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eurasianet.org">[Eurasianet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eurasianet.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">armenia church sues getty museum to reclaim armenian cultural heritage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">armenia church sues getty museum to reclaim armenian cultural heritage</span></span></span>

A settlement in 2015 allowed the Getty to retain the pages while recognising the Church’s ownership. The agreement resolved the immediate legal dispute without reconstructing every stage of the leaves’ passage through the twentieth-century art market.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anca.org/getty-western-prelacy-reach-settlement-in-art-restitution-case/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anca.org">[Armenian National Committee of America]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anca.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on anca.org.</span></span></span>

The mystery matters because provenance—the documented chain of an object’s ownership—is especially fragile when communities are destroyed or expelled. Receipts and sales records may create the appearance of a legitimate transaction decades later, while concealing an original seizure that occurred amid persecution. Conversely, objects were sometimes separated or sold by survivors attempting to preserve them or finance escape. The absence of documentation prevents easy moral categories.

The Zeytun pages are not a mythical lost treasure. They are surviving fragments whose missing history reflects the fragmentation of the community that protected them. Their story connects Armenia’s manuscript tradition with broader questions about museum collecting, genocide-era property and whether legal possession can ever fully settle cultural ownership.

## Who killed Catholicos Khoren I?

During the 1930s, the Soviet authorities intensified their assault on religious institutions. Churches in Soviet Armenia were closed or confiscated, clergy were arrested, and the Armenian Apostolic Church came under severe political and financial pressure. Its leader, Catholicos Khoren I, died suddenly at Holy Etchmiadzin during the night of 5–6 April 1938.

The official explanation was a heart attack. The circumstances immediately aroused suspicion. His burial was hurried, public mourning was restricted, and Soviet accounts avoided a clear discussion of how he died. Later church histories and many scholars concluded that he was killed by officers or agents of the NKVD, the Soviet secret police. A study of the Armenian Church under Soviet rule described the death as mysterious but “undoubtedly” caused by the NKVD, while a UCLA account of the period similarly refers to the 1938 killing as an NKVD murder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scispace.com/pdf/the-armenian-church-under-the-soviet-regime-part-1-the-ht9r9gook4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scispace.com">[SciSpace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scispace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the armenian church under the soviet regime part 1 the ht9r9gook4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the armenian church under the soviet regime part 1 the ht9r9gook4</span></span></span>

The broad political context strongly supports foul play. Stalin’s Great Purge was then at its height, and thousands of people in Soviet Armenia were arrested or executed. A regional study of the terror reports that 4,639 people were executed in Armenia during the repression of 1930–1938, almost all of them in 1937–1938. It also records reports that secret-police agents strangled Khoren I.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://css.ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/gess/cis/center-for-securities-studies/pdfs/CAD-22-9-12.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: css.ethz.ch">[Center for Security Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">css.ethz.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for Security Studies No. 22: Stalinist Terror in the South Caucasus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for Security Studies No. 22: Stalinist Terror in the South Caucasus</span></span></span>

The exact chain of command and manner of death are less certain. Accounts variously describe strangulation, a fatal assault during an attempt to seize church property, or an operation authorised by senior Soviet officials. Later testimony attributed ultimate responsibility to Lavrentiy Beria, one of Stalin’s most powerful security chiefs, but the surviving evidence does not establish every participant beyond dispute.

Recent scholarship has also questioned whether the authorities originally intended to kill Khoren. The state wanted control over church finances and access to diaspora resources, while eliminating the Catholicos risked provoking Armenians abroad and weakening Soviet influence over them. On this interpretation, his death may have resulted from violent coercion rather than a carefully planned execution. The hurried burial would then have been intended to suppress public anger and conceal what had happened.

The heart-attack explanation is difficult to reconcile with the wider evidence, and historians generally regard state involvement as highly probable. The unresolved part concerns responsibility: who gave the order, what the attackers were trying to obtain and whether murder was the objective from the beginning.

## Is there a comet on the crown of Tigranes the Great?

Coins issued under Tigranes II, who ruled from 95 to 55 BC, show the Armenian king wearing a distinctive pointed crown decorated with stars and birds. On certain examples, the star-like emblem appears to have an extended or curved tail. Researchers have suggested that this represents Halley’s Comet, which passed close to Earth in 87 BC, early in Tigranes’ reign.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0405073" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Halley&#x27;s comet of 87 BC on the coins of Armenian king Tigranes?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Halley&#x27;s comet of 87 BC on the coins of Armenian king Tigranes?</span></span></span>

The proposed identification is attractive because comets were widely interpreted as signs of royal destiny, war or political transformation. Tigranes was building a large regional empire at the time, and a striking celestial event could have been adopted as a symbol of divine favour. If the theory is correct, the coins would contain one of the earliest known visual representations of Halley’s Comet.

Yet the design is too stylised to provide certainty. Stars, solar emblems and divine symbols commonly appeared on ancient royal dress. Variations between dies may reflect artistic choices rather than an astronomical event. The curved “tail” is not present in the same form on every coin, and no surviving Armenian inscription explicitly states that Tigranes adopted a comet as his emblem. The researchers who proposed the connection therefore framed it as a possibility, not a settled identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0405073" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

This small numismatic puzzle demonstrates how historians work when written sources are scarce. The date of a comet, the chronology of coin issues and the form of an emblem can be compared, producing a plausible interpretation. What they cannot do is recover the king’s intention without additional evidence. The symbol may be Halley’s Comet, another celestial body or an established mark of Armenian kingship.

## What Armenia’s mysteries actually reveal

Armenia’s unresolved past is shaped less by inexplicable events than by interrupted evidence. Prehistoric builders left monuments without texts. Ancient rulers used symbols whose political language is now obscure. Manuscripts were scattered when their guardians were killed or displaced. Soviet officials suppressed records concerning deaths for which the state itself may have been responsible.

The most plausible conclusions are correspondingly restrained:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Zorats Karer was principally a prehistoric funerary and ceremonial complex; its use as a formal observatory remains unproven.
* Areni-1 preserves clear evidence of early wine production, but the relationship between winemaking, burial and ritual is still being reconstructed.
* The dragon stones were probably connected with water, highland pastoral life and organised religious activity, although their mythology is lost.
* The Zeytun Gospel pages were separated during an era of persecution and displacement, but their complete route into the art market cannot be recovered.
* Khoren I was probably killed through Soviet state violence, though the immediate circumstances and chain of responsibility remain disputed.
* The emblem on Tigranes’ crown may represent Halley’s Comet, but the identification remains an informed hypothesis.

</div>

These cases endure because they lie at the boundary between recovery and loss. Modern science can date leather, analyse wine residues, map monuments and compare coin designs. Courts and historians can trace surviving ownership records and political testimony. But no technique can automatically replace destroyed archives or restore the voices of communities that were silenced. Armenia’s historical mysteries are therefore not empty gaps waiting for imaginative theories. They are reminders of how much can be learned from fragments—and how carefully those fragments must be interpreted.
