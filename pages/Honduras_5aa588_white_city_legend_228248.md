## Introduction

The legend of [Honduras]({{ 'honduras/' | relative_url }})'s White City, often called *La Ciudad Blanca*, has fascinated explorers for centuries. The short answer to the question is that **archaeologists have confirmed the existence of major ancient settlements beneath the forests of eastern Honduras, but they have not proved that a single legendary "White City" ever existed as described in folklore or adventure stories.** Modern discoveries have transformed the debate from "Is there anything there?" to "What kind of civilisation occupied the Mosquitia rainforest, and how many large settlements did it contain?"<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159890" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Ancient Settlement Patterns through LiDAR in the Mosquitia Region of Honduras &#124; PLOS OneAugust 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2016</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Honduras_5aa588_white_city_legend_228248-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="White City illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Honduras_5aa588_white_city_legend_228248-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Honduras_5aa588_white_city_legend_228248-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

What makes the mystery enduring is that several different traditions became merged into one story. Indigenous memories of abandoned places, Spanish reports of prosperous inland regions, twentieth-century exploration claims and modern media accounts all contributed to the idea of a single lost city hidden beneath the jungle. Scientific evidence has confirmed extensive archaeology in the region, but it has also shown that reality is more complex than the legend.

## Origins of the legend

The White City legend did not begin with one eyewitness account. Instead, it developed over centuries from several separate strands.

One of the earliest written references came in 1526, when the Spanish conquistador Hernán Cortés wrote that he had heard reports of a rich province deep within the Honduran interior, with numerous towns and considerable wealth. He never reached the area himself, and his description was based on second-hand information rather than direct observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/05/06/the-el-dorado-machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span></span></span>

Equally important were Indigenous traditions. Archaeologist Chris Begley and other researchers have argued that stories told by Pech and Tawahka communities about ancestral places and abandoned settlements were later blended with Spanish accounts. Rather than describing one specific city, these traditions often referred to sacred ancestral landscapes whose significance was cultural as much as geographical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/05/06/the-el-dorado-machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span></span></span>

During the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, explorers increasingly searched the remote Mosquitia rainforest for spectacular ruins. As reports accumulated, separate rumours gradually merged into the idea of one magnificent "White City" built of pale stone. The nickname "City of the Monkey God" emerged even later and reflects twentieth-century adventure literature more than any established Indigenous tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/05/06/the-el-dorado-machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span></span></span>

## Theodore Morde and the mystery of the missing location

The White City entered popular culture largely because of the American explorer Theodore Morde.

In 1940, after an expedition into the Mosquitia, Morde announced that he had found the legendary city. He described walls, plazas and ceremonial structures and claimed local traditions associated the site with a monkey deity. Newspapers around the world reported the apparent breakthrough, and Morde returned with numerous artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/05/06/the-el-dorado-machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span></span></span>

The crucial problem was that he never revealed the location.

Morde promised to organise a proper archaeological excavation but never returned to the site. His death in 1954 meant that no maps, coordinates or convincing archaeological documentation were ever produced. This absence created one of the legend's greatest mysteries.

Several explanations have been proposed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* He genuinely found an important archaeological site but exaggerated its significance.
* He deliberately concealed the location to prevent looting.
* He combined several different ruins into a single dramatic narrative.
* He misunderstood what he had found.

</div>

No surviving evidence conclusively supports any one explanation. Archaeologists generally regard Morde as someone who almost certainly visited genuine ancient settlements but whose published accounts cannot be accepted as proof of a unique lost city.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/05/06/the-el-dorado-machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker The El Dorado Machine &#124; The New Yorker</span></span></span>

## Lidar discoveries and what they proved

The biggest change in the debate came not through another jungle expedition but through airborne laser mapping.

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) uses laser pulses from an aircraft to penetrate gaps in the forest canopy and generate detailed models of the ground surface. In the early 2010s, surveys of valleys within the Mosquitia revealed an extensive network of artificial features invisible from the air or on ordinary satellite photographs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159890" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Ancient Settlement Patterns through LiDAR in the Mosquitia Region of Honduras &#124; PLOS OneAugust 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2016</span></span></span>

Subsequent field investigations confirmed that these were genuine archaeological remains rather than natural formations. Researchers identified:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* plazas and ceremonial spaces;
* earthen pyramids and platforms;
* terraces and landscape engineering;
* roadways and earthworks;
* canals and water-management systems;
* multiple settlements organised into a regional hierarchy.

</div>

The peer-reviewed archaeological study concluded that one surveyed valley contained **nineteen settlements organised around a dominant urban centre**, demonstrating that what now appears to be untouched rainforest had once supported a densely populated and carefully engineered landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159890" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Ancient Settlement Patterns through LiDAR in the Mosquitia Region of Honduras &#124; PLOS OneAugust 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2016</span></span></span>

Ground investigations also uncovered an extraordinary undisturbed cache of carved stone objects, including decorated metates, bowls and sculptures depicting jaguars, vultures and other symbolic figures. Because the deposit had not been looted, it provided rare evidence of ritual activity in the region shortly before the settlements were abandoned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159890" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Ancient Settlement Patterns through LiDAR in the Mosquitia Region of Honduras &#124; PLOS OneAugust 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2016</span></span></span>

## Why the discoveries did not prove the White City legend

The LiDAR discoveries were genuine archaeological breakthroughs, but they did **not** demonstrate that explorers had located the legendary White City exactly as described in folklore.

Instead, they changed the historical question.

The evidence suggests that eastern Honduras supported a network of substantial settlements belonging to a previously poorly understood civilisation. The peer-reviewed research deliberately avoided identifying the excavated city with the legendary White City. Instead, researchers referred to one principal settlement as the "City of the Jaguar" within the Valle de la Fortaleza study area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159890" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Ancient Settlement Patterns through LiDAR in the Mosquitia Region of Honduras &#124; PLOS OneAugust 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2016</span></span></span>

Several reasons explain this caution:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The White City legend has no single consistent historical description.
* Different traditions probably referred to different places.
* The archaeological evidence indicates multiple large settlements rather than one isolated metropolis.
* No inscription or historical document identifies any excavated site as "the" White City.

</div>

As archaeologist Chris Fisher observed, the archaeological discoveries reveal a sophisticated civilisation, but they do not settle centuries of legend in one dramatic moment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159890" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Ancient Settlement Patterns through LiDAR in the Mosquitia Region of Honduras &#124; PLOS OneAugust 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2016</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery remains

The White City continues to be debated because archaeology has answered some questions while raising others.

Researchers now know that the Mosquitia was far more densely populated before European contact than earlier generations believed. Large communities reshaped the landscape through construction, agriculture and ceremonial architecture. This overturned the old assumption that the region had always been sparsely inhabited rainforest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159890" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Ancient Settlement Patterns through LiDAR in the Mosquitia Region of Honduras &#124; PLOS OneAugust 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2016</span></span></span>

Yet several important uncertainties remain.

First, much of the region has still never been systematically surveyed. Additional valleys may contain comparable archaeological landscapes.

Second, the ancient civilisation responsible for many of these settlements remains imperfectly understood. Unlike the better-known Maya cities farther west, the Mosquitia communities developed distinctive architectural styles and material culture that are still being investigated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159890" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Ancient Settlement Patterns through LiDAR in the Mosquitia Region of Honduras &#124; PLOS OneAugust 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2016</span></span></span>

Third, the reasons for abandonment are still debated. Archaeologists suspect that epidemic diseases introduced after European arrival spread through trade networks before Spaniards themselves entered these remote valleys, contributing to widespread demographic collapse. ## A legend transformed by evidence

The enduring fascination of the White City lies in the fact that the legend was neither completely true nor completely false.

The romantic image of a single gleaming lost city hidden beneath the jungle has never been demonstrated. At the same time, sceptics who once dismissed the stories as pure fantasy were also mistaken. Modern archaeology has shown that the forests of eastern Honduras conceal an extensive cultural landscape of ancient settlements, many unknown to science until LiDAR made them visible.

Rather than ending the mystery, these discoveries have reframed it. The question is no longer whether there were remarkable cities in the Mosquitia, but how many existed, how they were connected, what societies built them and how their history became transformed into one of the Americas' most enduring legends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159890" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identifying Ancient Settlement Patterns through LiDAR in the Mosquitia Region of Honduras &#124; PLOS OneAugust 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2016</span></span></span>
