## Introduction

The strongest evidence does not support sweeping theories about lost superweapons, secret empires or all-powerful conspirators. Instead, these cases remain compelling because crucial information was never created, was poorly preserved, was deliberately obscured or is simply too limited to support a final answer. Together they reveal something important about Pakistan’s history: mysteries survive not only because events are strange, but because archaeology, political power, institutional weakness and public memory determine which questions can still be answered.

## Why the Indus script remains unread

The oldest major mystery associated with present-day Pakistan is the sign system used by the Indus civilisation. Thousands of short inscriptions appear on seals, tablets, pottery and other objects recovered from sites including [Mohenjo-daro]({{ 'mohenjo-daro/' | relative_url }}) and Harappa. Yet nobody has produced a decipherment accepted by specialists.

The obstacle is not a lack of imaginative proposals. Suggested underlying languages have included early Dravidian, Indo-Aryan and several lost language families. The real problem is the evidence. Most inscriptions are extremely short, there is no known bilingual text comparable to the Rosetta Stone, and scholars cannot securely identify the language spoken by the civilisation. Statistical studies show that the signs occur in structured sequences, with recurring opening and closing patterns, but this does not reveal what the symbols mean. It does not even settle beyond dispute whether they represent complete spoken language, abbreviated administrative information or a more limited system of symbols.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.3017" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Statistical analysis of the Indus script using $n$-grams</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistical analysis of the Indus script using $n$-gramsJanuary 20, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 20, 2009</span></span></span>

Two broad positions dominate the debate. Many researchers treat the signs as writing and look for grammatical or economic patterns. Computational work has found an ordered structure resembling formal communication, while one interpretation suggests that some tablets may have recorded rations, labour or repeated transactions. Other scholars argue that the texts are too short, repetitive and restricted to demonstrate full literacy, and may instead have functioned like religious, political or ownership emblems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://safarmer.com/fsw2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: safarmer.com">[safarmer.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">safarmer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">22 which is often invoked to support Hindu nationalist (Hindutva) ideas, is the claim that Indus inscriptions embodied Hindu...Read more...</span></span></span>

Claims that the script has already been “solved” should therefore be treated cautiously. A convincing decipherment would need to explain a large proportion of the known inscriptions consistently, fit the archaeological context and make predictions that other scholars could test. Selecting a few symbols that resemble later letters, religious images or modern words is not enough.

The mystery matters because decipherment could transform knowledge of Indus government, trade, belief and social organisation. Mohenjo-daro’s planned streets, wells, public structures and drainage demonstrate sophisticated civic administration, but its inhabitants left no readable royal inscriptions, law codes or historical accounts. Their cities are visible; their own explanation of those cities remains silent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/138/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Archaeological Ruins at Moenjodaro</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreArchaeological Ruins at Moenjodaro - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## Did Mohenjo-daro fall to massacre, flood or climate change?

Mohenjo-daro, in Sindh, was one of the largest cities of the Indus civilisation. Its orderly streets, baked-brick buildings and sanitation systems helped establish the civilisation as a counterpart to ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia. Large-scale urban life declined after about 1900 BC, but the older popular image of a city destroyed in one catastrophic invasion is no longer supported by the best archaeological evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/138/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Archaeological Ruins at Moenjodaro</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreArchaeological Ruins at Moenjodaro - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Early excavators found several groups of human remains in apparently irregular positions. These skeletons were later woven into a dramatic account in which invading warriors massacred the city’s inhabitants. Archaeologist Mortimer Wheeler connected the scene with ancient hymns describing a god who destroyed forts, producing the memorable accusation that the invader’s deity “stood accused”.

George Dales re-examined the evidence and showed why this interpretation was unsafe. Nine seasons of excavation in a city several miles across produced only around 37 skeletons securely associated with the Indus period. They came from different locations and archaeological levels, rather than from a single destruction layer. The remains therefore did not demonstrate a city-wide massacre or one decisive invasion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/the-mythical-massacre-at-mohenjo-daro/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: penn.museum">[Penn Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">penn.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museum Expedition Magazine &#124; The Mythical Massacre at Mohenjo-Daro</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Expedition Magazine &#124; The Mythical Massacre at Mohenjo-Daro...</span></span></span>

Modern explanations favour a long process of urban decline rather than an overnight disappearance. Changing river systems, weakening monsoons, repeated droughts, flooding, agricultural pressures, altered trade and migration probably affected different settlements in different ways. Climate evidence supports increasing environmental stress, but researchers still debate its timing and how directly it caused social change. Environmental disruption may explain why large cities became harder to sustain; it does not prove that the entire civilisation suddenly vanished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.whoi.edu/press-room/news-release/climate-change-likely-caused-migration-demise-of-ancient-indus-valley-civilization/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whoi.edu">[whoi.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whoi.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Woods Hole Oceanographic InstitutionAncient Indus Valley Civilization &amp; Climate Change&#x27;s...13 Nov 2018 — Climate Change Likely Caused Mi...</span></span></span>

The people themselves did not necessarily disappear. Settlement patterns suggest movement away from some major urban centres and the transformation of Indus traditions into smaller, more regionally varied communities. The better question is therefore not “Who destroyed Mohenjo-daro?” but “Why did a highly connected urban system cease to function in its earlier form?”

Modern myths have complicated that question. Internet stories claim that Mohenjo-daro contains radioactive skeletons, vitrified buildings or evidence of an ancient nuclear explosion. No reputable archaeological investigation supports these claims. They combine distorted descriptions of the skeletons with invented measurements and speculation detached from the site’s excavation record. The genuine mystery is a difficult problem of climate, rivers, economy and social adaptation—not evidence of prehistoric superweapons.

## Who was behind Liaquat Ali Khan’s assassination?

On 16 October 1951, Pakistan’s first prime minister, Liaquat Ali Khan, was shot while addressing a public meeting at Company Bagh in Rawalpindi, later renamed Liaquat Bagh. The identified gunman, Said Akbar, was immediately shot dead by police. That act eliminated the person most able to explain whether he acted alone, what his motive was and whether anybody had assisted him.

The basic event is well established; the wider responsibility is not. The United Nations’ later inquiry into Benazir Bhutto’s assassination noted that the gunman in Liaquat’s case was killed at the scene and that those who may have stood behind the murder were never established. Contemporary United States diplomatic records show that foreign officials were deeply concerned about the political consequences, particularly for Pakistan’s leadership, relations with India and the Kashmir dispute, but they do not demonstrate who ordered the killing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/681517/files/S_2010_191-EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library System Etpu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library System Etpu</span></span></span>

Several theories have persisted. Said Akbar was an Afghan national connected to the frontier politics of the period, leading to suggestions that the killing arose from tensions between Afghanistan and Pakistan. Other accounts point towards domestic rivals, Cold War intelligence interests or disagreements over foreign policy. Declassified documents are sometimes presented as proof of foreign sponsorship, but evidence that other governments had motives or closely monitored the political situation is not evidence that they organised the assassination.

The original investigation failed to produce a persuasive, publicly accessible account that connected the gunman to a sponsor. Later recollections added allegations about missing papers, unexplored financial payments and the loss of investigative material, but these claims vary in reliability. The central gap remains simple: no surviving evidence securely establishes Said Akbar’s motive or a chain of command above him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dawn.com/news/1213461" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dawn.com">[Dawn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dawn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The mystery that shrouds Liaquat Ali Khan&#x27;s murder</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The mystery that shrouds Liaquat Ali Khan&#x27;s murder</span></span></span>

Liaquat’s death became part of public memory because it occurred during Pakistan’s fragile first years. He had served as prime minister since independence and was a central figure in the creation of the new state. His assassination intensified concerns that major political killings could alter national direction without ever being fully explained.

## What brought down General Zia’s aircraft?

On 17 August 1988, a Pakistan Air Force C-130 carrying President General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq crashed shortly after leaving Bahawalpur. Everyone aboard was killed, including senior Pakistani officers, the United States ambassador Arnold Raphel and the head of the American military assistance mission.

The circumstances naturally generated suspicion. Zia had ruled Pakistan since the 1977 military coup and had made the country a central partner in the campaign against Soviet forces in Afghanistan. He had domestic enemies, foreign adversaries and strained relationships within the political and military establishment. Any unexplained fatal accident involving such a figure was likely to produce competing assassination theories.

Pakistan’s official investigation reportedly rejected ordinary mechanical failure, pilot error, a missile and an external explosion. It concluded that a criminal act of sabotage was the most probable cause, possibly involving a substance that incapacitated the crew. However, investigators did not identify the method conclusively or name the perpetrators. Contemporary reporting therefore treated sabotage as the official assessment, not as a solved crime.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.upi.com/Archives/1988/10/16/An-official-report-of-an-investigation-into-the-plane/2836592977600/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upi.com">[upi.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">An official report of an investigation into the plane</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An official report of an investigation into the plane</span></span></span>

The list of proposed suspects has included Soviet intelligence, Afghan operatives, hostile military insiders, foreign governments and Zia’s domestic opponents. Stories about poison gas, explosives, contaminated cargo or manipulated controls have all circulated. None has been established by publicly available physical evidence.

The United Nations inquiry into Benazir Bhutto’s death later observed that Pakistani and American investigations into Zia’s crash reached conflicting conclusions and that the case remained surrounded by speculation. That disagreement is one reason the mystery survives: the public record offers grounds to doubt a simple accident but not enough evidence to identify a saboteur.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/681517/files/S_2010_191-EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library System Etpu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library System Etpu</span></span></span>

The crash also changed Pakistan immediately. It ended eleven years of military rule under Zia and reopened the path to elections. Because the political consequences were so large, later accounts often treat the result of his death as evidence of who caused it. That reasoning is unreliable. Many actors may have benefited from the removal of Zia; benefit after an event does not prove responsibility for it.

## Why Benazir Bhutto’s murder is still unresolved

Benazir Bhutto was assassinated on 27 December 2007 after an election rally at Liaquat Bagh—the same Rawalpindi park where Liaquat Ali Khan had been killed in 1951. An attacker fired towards her vehicle and then detonated an explosive device. The attack killed Bhutto and numerous bystanders.

Militant involvement is strongly plausible. Bhutto faced threats from Al-Qaeda, the Taliban and Pakistani extremist groups, and the government publicly blamed a network associated with militant commander Baitullah Mehsud. Yet identifying a likely militant source did not answer every question about planning, facilitation, security failures or possible support from other actors.

A United Nations commission found that Bhutto had received inadequate protection despite known threats. It also documented serious investigative failures. The crime scene was washed down soon after the attack, damaging the opportunity for a thorough forensic examination. Investigators concentrated heavily on lower-level suspects linked to Mehsud while doing little to pursue alternative lines of inquiry, including possible assistance, wider organisational responsibility or involvement by elements of the state establishment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/681517/files/S_2010_191-EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library System Etpu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library System Etpu</span></span></span>

The commission did not claim to have identified the mastermind. Its mandate was to establish facts and circumstances, not conduct a criminal prosecution. It concluded that the police investigation lacked independence, effectiveness and the political will to follow evidence wherever it led. It also found that intelligence bodies played an extensive and poorly disclosed role during crucial stages of the investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/681517/files/S_2010_191-EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library System Etpu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library System Etpu</span></span></span>

This distinction is essential. There is evidence supporting militant responsibility for the immediate attack, but the complete chain of planning and assistance was not established. Claims that the case conclusively proves a particular state conspiracy go beyond the available findings. Equally, claims that the public explanation settled every question ignore documented failures in security, evidence preservation and investigative independence.

Bhutto’s assassination endures as a national mystery because uncertainty was created not merely by a clever attacker but by institutional actions after the event. When a crime scene is compromised and major leads are not pursued, the resulting doubt cannot easily be repaired years later.

## How the “Persian princess” became a modern murder puzzle

In October 2000, Pakistani police recovered an elaborately presented mummy in Balochistan. Its coffin and ornaments appeared to identify the body as an ancient Persian princess, supposedly a daughter of the Achaemenid king Xerxes. The find attracted international attention and competing cultural claims before scientific examination overturned the story.

Asma Ibrahim of the National Museum of Pakistan led the investigation. Specialists identified errors in the inscriptions and inconsistencies in the supposed mummification method. Medical imaging showed that the body and its treatment did not match the ancient identity being claimed. Radiocarbon testing of human tissue and accompanying materials indicated a modern date; research placed the woman’s death in the mid-1990s rather than the fifth century BC. United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime+2ScienceDirect<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unodc.org/cld/case-law-doc/traffickingculturalpropertycrimetype/pak/2001/the_persian_mummy_case.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unodc.org">[unodc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unodc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unodc.org.</span></span></span>

The artefact was therefore not an ancient royal burial but a manufactured antiquities fraud. The body of a recently deceased young woman had been altered, dried and placed inside an artificially aged display intended for sale. Investigators found injuries suggesting that she may have died violently, although the precise circumstances and her identity were never conclusively established in the public record. United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unodc.org/cld/case-law-doc/traffickingculturalpropertycrimetype/pak/2001/the_persian_mummy_case.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unodc.org">[unodc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unodc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unodc.org.</span></span></span>

This makes the case unusual among archaeological hoaxes. Scientific testing successfully solved the question of authenticity but exposed a more troubling mystery: who was the woman, how did she die, and who obtained and transformed her body? The false royal biography briefly attracted more attention than the real unidentified victim.

The case also demonstrates how national prestige and the international antiquities market can encourage premature conclusions. Before the tests were complete, the mummy was treated as a discovery of major historical importance. Once the claim collapsed, public interest faded, although the unresolved modern death was arguably the more serious matter.

## What these mysteries reveal about Pakistan’s past

Pakistan’s strongest historical mysteries fall into three different categories, and keeping them separate helps prevent exaggeration.

**Evidence-limited mysteries** arise because the surviving material is incomplete. The Indus signs are real and highly structured, but the inscriptions are too short and lack a bilingual key. The decline of Mohenjo-daro can be reconstructed only through archaeology, environmental records and settlement patterns, none of which provides a single written explanation.

**Investigation-damaged mysteries** survive because institutions failed to preserve or pursue evidence. The deaths of Liaquat Ali Khan, Zia-ul-Haq and Benazir Bhutto all generated official inquiries, but none produced an uncontested account identifying every responsible actor. Dead suspects, restricted records, conflicting findings and compromised crime scenes created space in which both reasonable doubt and unsupported conspiracy could grow.

**Manufactured mysteries** begin with deception. The Persian princess was not ancient, but the hoax was sufficiently elaborate to exploit expectations about lost royalty, smuggling and spectacular discovery. Scientific investigation dismantled the historical claim while revealing a genuine human tragedy beneath it.

These cases remain culturally powerful because each concerns an absence: unread words, abandoned cities, missing motives, unidentified planners or an unnamed victim. Their lesson is not that every gap conceals a grand conspiracy. It is that uncertainty has a history of its own. Some questions remain open because ancient evidence is scarce; others because modern authorities failed to protect the truth when it was still recoverable.
