## Who killed Olof Palme?

Swedish prime minister Olof Palme was shot in the back shortly before midnight on 28 February 1986 while walking home from a cinema with his wife, Lisbet, in central Stockholm. The couple had no bodyguards with them. The gunman escaped on foot, leaving behind two bullets but no weapon and no indisputable forensic link to a suspect. Early mistakes permanently weakened the inquiry. The crime scene was not secured effectively, witness accounts diverged and important lines of investigation were pursued unevenly. Christer Pettersson, a man with a history of violent crime, was convicted in 1989 after Lisbet Palme identified him, but the conviction was overturned on appeal because the supporting evidence was insufficient. No murder weapon was ever tied to him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/news/2019/may/16/olof-palme-sweden-prime-minister-unsolved-murder-new-evidence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Who killed the prime minister?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The unsolved murder that still haunts SwedenMay 16, 2019 — On February 28, 1986, Swedish Prime Minister Olof Palme was assassinated in ce...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 16, 2019</span></span></span>

In June 2020, chief prosecutor Krister Petersson closed the investigation and said that Stig Engström, a graphic designer who had worked in an office beside the murder scene, was the most likely killer. Engström had placed himself at the scene as a witness, wore clothing resembling some descriptions of the fleeing man and gave changing accounts of his actions. However, he died in 2000 and could neither be questioned nor tried. No gun, DNA evidence or direct eyewitness identification connected him conclusively to the shooting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aklagare.se/globalassets/dokument/ovriga-dokument/amr-8123-25-18dec-2025-translation-of-a-decision-in-a-review-case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aklagare.se">[Åklagarmyndigheten]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aklagare.se</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Åklagarmyndigheten Translation of a Decision in a Review Case</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ÅklagarmyndighetenTranslation of a Decision in a Review CaseDecember 18, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 18, 2025</span></span></span>

That apparent official solution did not last. Following a review, Sweden’s prosecution authority decided in December 2025 that the available evidence was not sufficient to justify identifying Engström as the murderer. * **A Swedish extremist connection:** Palme attracted fierce hostility from parts of the radical right, yet no operational conspiracy has been proved.

## What happened to Raoul Wallenberg?

Raoul [Wallenberg]({{ 'wallenberg/' | relative_url }}) was a Swedish diplomat sent to Budapest in 1944, when Hungary’s Jewish population faced deportation and murder. Working through the Swedish legation, he distributed protective documents, established protected houses and intervened during deportations and forced marches. The exact number of people personally saved by him is debated, but his rescue work unquestionably protected thousands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yadvashem.org/righteous/stories/wallenberg.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yadvashem.org">[Yad Vashem]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yadvashem.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yad VashemRaoul Wallenberg, the Righteous Among the NationsAfter the occupation of Hungary on March 19, 1944, the Swedish degation launch...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 19, 1944</span></span></span>

On 17 January 1945, after Soviet forces entered Budapest, Wallenberg and his driver, Vilmos Langfelder, were taken into Soviet custody. Wallenberg apparently intended to discuss relief and reconstruction work with Soviet commanders. Instead, he was transported into the Soviet prison system and disappeared. The reason for his arrest has never been established. Soviet suspicions that he was involved in espionage, his American connections and his contacts with German and Hungarian officials may all have contributed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rwi.lu.se/about/about-raoul-wallenberg/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rwi.lu.se">[rwi.lu.se]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rwi.lu.se</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About Raoul WallenbergRaoul Wallenberg was a man of outstanding individual courage, humanity and decisiveness. In 1957, it produced a memorandum stating that a prisoner named Wallenberg had died in Moscow’s Lubyanka prison on 17 July 1947, supposedly from a heart attack. The account has remained doubtful because it emerged late, lacked supporting records and was inconsistent with some subsequent prison testimony. Other evidence has encouraged the possibility that a prisoner interrogated after the reported death date was Wallenberg, although this has not been proved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rferl.org/a/New_Documents_Emerge_In_Raoul_Wallenberg_Case/2000876.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rferl.org">[RadioFreeEurope/RadioLiberty]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rferl.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rferl.org.</span></span></span>

A joint Swedish-Russian investigation failed to settle whether he died in July 1947, was executed around that time or survived longer in captivity. Witnesses later claimed to have encountered him or someone resembling him in Soviet prisons, but such memories are difficult to verify and sometimes conflict. His personal effects were returned by Soviet authorities only in 1989, deepening suspicions that fuller documentation had existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sweden.se/life/people/raoul-wallenberg-world-war-ii-hero" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sweden.se">[sweden.se]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sweden.se</span><span class="citation-popover-title">raoul wallenberg world war ii hero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Raoul Wallenberg – World War II hero22 Jul 2025 — Raoul Wallenberg&#x27;s fate remains unknown. The mystery is therefore narrower than some popular versions suggest. There is strong evidence that Wallenberg was imprisoned by the Soviet authorities. It is also highly probable that he died in Soviet custody. The genuine unanswered questions are why he was detained, how and when he died, who authorised his treatment and whether decisive records remain inaccessible or were destroyed.

Sweden’s response has itself become part of the controversy. A government-appointed inquiry later characterised the country’s diplomatic handling of the case as a failure, finding that officials had acted too cautiously and had not pressed Soviet authorities effectively enough. ## Why were the dead at Sandby Borg never buried?

[Sandby Borg]({{ 'sandby-borg/' | relative_url }}) is an Iron Age ringfort on the island of Öland. Excavations begun in the early 2010s revealed human remains lying inside houses, alongside jewellery, household goods and other possessions. The evidence indicates that attackers entered the settlement and killed inhabitants during the late fifth or early sixth century. Bodies were left where they fell rather than being gathered for burial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sandbyborg.se/en/home/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sandbyborg.se">[Sandby Borg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sandbyborg.se</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sandbyborg.se.</span></span></span>

The site is exceptional because ordinary processes of clearing, rebuilding and burial did not follow the violence. Skeletons were found in domestic spaces, some with injuries caused at or near the time of death. Valuable objects remained concealed or abandoned. This makes robbery an incomplete explanation: the attackers either did not know about every cache, did not have time to recover them or were motivated by more than the seizure of wealth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rj.se/en/grants/2015/frozen-in-time---histories-of-life-and-moments-of-death-at-sandby-borg/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rj.se">[rj.se]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rj.se</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rj.se.</span></span></span>

Archaeologists can identify the event as an organised massacre with greater confidence than they can identify its perpetrators. The fort belonged to a period of political instability and competition among local elites following the weakening of the Western Roman Empire. Trade connections, imported objects and fortified settlements suggest that communities on Öland were involved in wider networks of status, wealth and armed rivalry. A struggle for local power is therefore plausible, but no surviving text names the attackers or records their grievance.

The failure to bury the dead may be the most revealing clue. In many societies, burial is both a duty to the deceased and a means of restoring order after violence. Preventing or avoiding burial could have expressed deliberate humiliation, fear of the killers, the destruction or expulsion of the surviving community, or a belief that the site had become dangerous or forbidden. Researchers have argued that the unburied bodies may have continued to exert a social effect, warning others against returning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/social-implications-of-unburied-corpses-from-intergroup-conflicts-postmortem-agency-following-the-sandby-borg-massacre/163DD4697DE3D9A0851A4F2397A11D26" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Sandby Borg should not be presented as evidence of an unknown ritual or supernatural catastrophe. The archaeological case for human violence is strong. What remains mysterious is the political story behind it: who ordered the attack, why the settlement was singled out and how a place filled with recoverable wealth could remain largely undisturbed for centuries.

## How did the Andrée expedition die?

In July 1897, Swedish engineer Salomon August Andrée set out to reach the North Pole by hydrogen balloon with Nils Strindberg and Knut Frænkel. Their balloon, the Eagle, left Danskøya in the Svalbard archipelago but soon lost equipment intended to help control its direction. After less than three days in the air, the men landed on drifting sea ice and began hauling heavily loaded sledges towards possible safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.grennamuseum.se/en/the-andree-expedition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: grennamuseum.se">[Grenna Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">grennamuseum.se</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenna Museum The Andrée Expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grenna Museum The Andrée Expedition</span></span></span>

Their progress was painfully slow because the ice itself drifted, sometimes carrying them away from their intended destination. After months of travel, they reached Kvitøya, a remote island east of Svalbard, in October 1897. All three died there. Their final camp, remains, photographs and diaries were not discovered until 1930. The finds answered the broad question of where the expedition ended, but not the precise cause or sequence of the deaths.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2010/04/19/the-ice-balloon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the ice balloon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the ice balloon</span></span></span>

Proposed explanations have included exposure, exhaustion, dehydration, food poisoning, carbon-monoxide poisoning, vitamin toxicity, scurvy and infection from inadequately cooked polar-bear meat. A medical review supporting botulism noted that the evidence was compatible with several causes and that no diagnosis could be considered definitive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10765627/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Later reconstructions have suggested that one or more men may have been injured or killed by a polar bear and that Andrée might then have died by suicide or another cause while alone. These scenarios attempt to explain the arrangement of bodies and equipment at the camp, but they depend on interpretations of a site disturbed before modern forensic examination. The bodies were cremated after their return to Sweden, removing the possibility of many later tests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9e%27s_Arctic_balloon_expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Andrée&#x27;s Arctic balloon expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Andrée&#x27;s Arctic balloon expedition</span></span></span>

The strongest conclusion is therefore a combination rather than a single dramatic cause. By the time the men reached Kvitøya, they had endured prolonged cold, wet clothing, inadequate rest, physical strain and uncertain food safety. A final accident, illness or animal attack could have killed individuals already severely weakened.

The expedition became a Swedish national tragedy partly because it had initially been celebrated as a bold demonstration of technology and national ambition. The recovered diaries transformed a heroic disappearance into a documented story of poor steering, punishing labour and diminishing options. Its remaining mystery is not whether the venture failed, but exactly how its final days unfolded.

## Who was the Bocksten Man?

In June 1936, children gathering peat discovered human bones and remarkably preserved clothing in a bog near Rolfstorp in Halland. The remains, now known as the Bocksten Man, belonged to a medieval man whose garments survived because of the wet, oxygen-poor environment. Clothing styles and radiocarbon evidence place him broadly in the fourteenth century, although precise dating remains difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumhalland.se/en/the-bocksten-man/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumhalland.se">[Hallands Kulturhistoriska Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumhalland.se</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumhalland.se.</span></span></span>

The body had been held beneath the bog with wooden poles. One surviving oak pole was driven through the region of the chest, a detail that encouraged stories that he had been pinned down to prevent his ghost from returning. Such beliefs are known from European folklore, but the physical arrangement does not by itself prove that this was the motive. The poles may also have been intended simply to conceal the body beneath what was then a lake or wetland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumhalland.se/en/the-bocksten-man/preventing-the-bocksten-man-ghost/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumhalland.se">[Hallands Kulturhistoriska Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumhalland.se</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumhalland.se.</span></span></span>

Modern examination has strengthened the conclusion that he died violently. Researchers reconstructing damage to the skull proposed a series of blows to the jaw and head, with the final strike likely fatal. His clothing suggests a person with some status rather than a destitute traveller, but textiles cannot supply a name, occupation or reason for murder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumhalland.se/en/the-bocksten-man/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumhalland.se">[Hallands Kulturhistoriska Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumhalland.se</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumhalland.se.</span></span></span>

One proposed identification is Simon Gudmundi, a senior medieval churchman involved in efforts to secure the canonisation of Catherine of Vadstena. The theory connects documentary history, local travel and possible markings among the man’s possessions. It remains speculative because the dating, identity markers and historical circumstances do not provide a unique match.

The responsible distinction is between three levels of evidence. The man existed and was deliberately placed in the wetland. His skull injuries strongly suggest homicide. His identity, the killers’ motive and the idea that the poles were an anti-ghost precaution remain unproved. The Bocksten Man is therefore an archaeological murder mystery, not confirmation of a medieval supernatural ritual.

## Were Sweden’s Cold War submarines real?

In October 1981, the Soviet submarine U 137 ran aground inside restricted Swedish waters near the naval base at Karlskrona. The incident, popularly called “Whiskey on the Rocks” after the vessel’s NATO class name, proved that at least one Soviet submarine had penetrated deep into Swedish territory. Soviet authorities maintained that navigational error was responsible, while Sweden treated the episode as a grave violation of its neutrality and security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://phpisn.ethz.ch/lory1.ethz.ch/publications/areastudies/documents/subinc/intro_mastny.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phpisn.ethz.ch">[phpisn.ethz.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phpisn.ethz.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ethz.ch.</span></span></span>

The confirmed grounding shaped how later reports were interpreted. Throughout the 1980s, Swedish forces investigated suspected submarine activity in coastal waters, most famously during the extensive Hårsfjärden hunt of 1982. Ships and helicopters used sonar, mines and depth charges, but no second foreign submarine was captured. Investigations accumulated observations, acoustic recordings and seabed traces without consistently identifying a vessel or country of origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0305022" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

There are several reasons for continued disagreement. Swedish waters are acoustically difficult, and biological or civilian sources can resemble mechanical signals. Some observations originally treated as submarine evidence were later reinterpreted. At the same time, the U 137 grounding makes it unreasonable to dismiss every report as imagination. Secret intelligence operations also leave few public records, and both Warsaw Pact and NATO forces had reasons to monitor Swedish waters.

Competing interpretations range from repeated Soviet reconnaissance to occasional Western operations, misidentification or a mixture of all three. Claims that one alliance alone explains every incident exceed the available evidence. The best-supported position is that Sweden faced a real underwater-security problem, but that the number, nationality and purpose of many individual contacts cannot now be reconstructed confidently.

The submarine mystery influenced defence policy and public attitudes towards the Soviet Union. It also illustrates how one confirmed incursion can alter the standard of belief applied to later ambiguous evidence. After U 137, an uncertain sonar contact was no longer merely an odd noise; it became a possible sign that an event already known to be possible was happening again.

## What were the ghost rockets of 1946?

During 1946, people across Sweden and neighbouring countries reported luminous, fast-moving objects often described as rockets, missiles or elongated projectiles. The sightings occurred at a moment of intense uncertainty. The Second World War had ended, captured German rocket technology was being studied by the victorious powers and relations between the Soviet Union and the West were deteriorating.

Swedish and foreign intelligence agencies took the reports seriously enough to investigate whether the objects were secret Soviet weapons. A surviving United States intelligence document from August 1946 discussed reports of “ghost rockets” crossing Sweden towards the Gulf of Bothnia, showing that the phenomenon was treated as a potential military issue rather than simply a matter of folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/1946-08-23.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cia.gov.</span></span></span>

Many reports can plausibly be explained by meteors, particularly because important clusters coincided with the Perseid meteor shower. Witnesses unfamiliar with high-altitude phenomena may also have misjudged distance, speed and direction. Aircraft, astronomical objects and rumours spreading through newspapers probably added further reports. No verified rocket wreckage was recovered from the most famous alleged impact sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_rockets" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ghost rockets</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ghost rockets</span></span></span>

That does not make every observation individually explainable. Some witnesses reported movement or duration that seemed unlike ordinary meteors, while radar or military testimony appeared to support a smaller number of incidents. Yet ambiguous observations are not evidence of extraterrestrial craft, and later UFO interpretations often detach the reports from their immediate post-war setting.

The ghost rockets are best understood as a genuine intelligence puzzle amplified by a climate of technological anxiety. Authorities had good reason to ask whether a new weapon was being tested. What they lacked was recoverable hardware, repeatable data or a confirmed launch source. The mystery survives because the reports cannot all be reconstructed, not because an extraordinary origin was established.

## Why these mysteries still matter

Sweden’s enduring enigmas differ sharply in the quality of their evidence. The Palme case has an enormous documentary record but no prosecutable killer. Wallenberg’s disappearance is constrained by inaccessible or missing state archives. Sandby Borg preserves the aftermath of violence while withholding the names and motives. The Andrée expedition left diaries yet lost the medical evidence needed to identify a final cause of death. The Bocksten Man retains his clothing and injuries but not his identity. Cold War incidents and ghost rockets sit between genuine security concerns and unreliable observation.

These distinctions matter because mystery easily becomes mythology. A gap in evidence does not make every theory equally credible. The absence of a murder weapon does not prove an international conspiracy; an unburied body does not prove fear of ghosts; an unexplained light does not establish an unknown technology. The most useful approach is to ask what a theory explains, what evidence it requires and whether that evidence actually exists.

The cases also reveal changes in Swedish public memory. Palme’s murder became a symbol of lost political innocence. Wallenberg came to represent both humanitarian courage and diplomatic failure. Sandby Borg forces modern visitors to confront organised violence in a period sometimes romanticised through jewellery and heroic legend. Andrée’s expedition has shifted from patriotic adventure to a warning about prestige, planning and the stories nations tell about exploration.

None of these mysteries needs exaggeration. Their power comes from the surviving detail: a prime minister’s abandoned evening, a prisoner reduced to disputed memoranda, families left on the floors of an Iron Age fort, photographs recovered from Arctic ice and medieval clothing preserved around a nameless victim. Sweden’s strangest historical puzzles endure because the evidence carries readers close to an answer, but not quite far enough to close the case.
