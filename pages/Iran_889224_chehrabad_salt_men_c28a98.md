## Introduction

The Salt Men of Chehrabad are among the most remarkable archaeological discoveries in Iran, not because their deaths are mysterious in a supernatural sense, but because they preserve the human story of repeated mining disasters across several centuries. Found in the Chehrabad salt mine in north-western Iran, these naturally mummified miners died in separate cave-ins during different historical periods. Salt, together with the mine's dry, oxygen-poor environment, preserved not only bones but also hair, skin, clothing, tools and even traces of their last meals. The result is an unusually detailed record of ancient working life that raises important questions about labour, risk and mining practices from the Achaemenid Empire through the Sasanian period. While archaeologists broadly agree that mine collapses killed the victims, many details of the disasters and the organisation of the mine remain under investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/aali333" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[Antiquity Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antiquity JournalAncient salt mining and salt men: the interdisciplinary Chehrabad Douzlakh project in north-western Iran &#124; Antiquity Jou...</span></span></span>

## How salt preserved the miners

The first Salt Man was discovered accidentally by modern miners in 1993. Subsequent excavations uncovered additional bodies and body parts, leading researchers to conclude that the mine contains the remains of at least eight individuals rather than a single disaster victim. Radiocarbon dating showed that they did not all belong to one period. Instead, some died during the Achaemenid Empire (roughly fifth to fourth centuries BC), while others died during the Sasanian period, many centuries later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8278039/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 14, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 14, 2021</span></span></span>

Unlike the artificial mummies of Egypt, the Salt Men were preserved naturally. Salt rapidly removed moisture from soft tissue, while the underground environment limited bacterial activity. As a result, some bodies retained beards, hair, skin, leather boots, woollen clothing and wooden tools in extraordinary condition. This preservation extends beyond the people themselves. Excavations have recovered textiles, baskets, ropes, animal remains, seeds and food residues that normally decay quickly at archaeological sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhm.at/en/research/prehistory/research/textile_research/saltmen_iran" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhm.at">[nhm.at]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhm.at</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naturhistorisches Museum WienNaturhistorisches Museum Wien - Saltmen Iran...</span></span></span>

Because so much organic material survived, Chehrabad has become an international research project involving archaeologists, physical anthropologists, textile specialists, geneticists, palaeoparasitologists and mining historians. Their work has transformed the site from an isolated curiosity into one of the world's richest archives of ancient mining communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity-project-gallery/article/ancient-salt-mining-and-salt-men-the-interdisciplinary-chehrabad-douzlakh-project-in-northwestern-iran/8DAAF71123DC15034002DD55F2D2DDAB" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAncient salt mining and salt men: the interdisciplinary Chehrabad Douzlakh project in north-wester...</span></span></span>

## Why researchers believe there were repeated mine collapses

One of the most important discoveries at Chehrabad is that the Salt Men did not all die together. Archaeological layers show that mining continued over long periods, was interrupted by major collapses and was later resumed by new generations of miners.

Evidence supporting repeated disasters includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Radiocarbon dates that place different bodies hundreds of years apart.
* Thick layers of rockfall sealing earlier mining galleries.
* Later mining activity cutting through or above earlier collapse deposits.
* Similar patterns of crushing injuries affecting several individuals from different periods.
* Distinct phases of mine use separated by abandonment and natural sediment accumulation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/aali333" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[Antiquity Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antiquity JournalAncient salt mining and salt men: the interdisciplinary Chehrabad Douzlakh project in north-western Iran &#124; Antiquity Jou...</span></span></span>

</div>

Researchers now believe a major collapse probably ended an Achaemenid phase of mining around 400 BC. After a long interruption, the mine was reopened during the Sasanian era, when another fatal collapse claimed additional lives. Rather than one catastrophic event, Chehrabad records recurring industrial accidents over many generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/aali333" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[Antiquity Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antiquity JournalAncient salt mining and salt men: the interdisciplinary Chehrabad Douzlakh project in north-western Iran &#124; Antiquity Jou...</span></span></span>

The exact trigger for each collapse remains uncertain. Salt deposits naturally deform over time, and the ancient miners appear to have used a pillar-and-chamber method, cutting large underground spaces while leaving supporting pillars of salt. Excavated tools indicate that timber supports were apparently not used, making roof failures a plausible explanation, although archaeologists cannot reconstruct every individual accident with certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/aali333" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[Antiquity Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antiquity JournalAncient salt mining and salt men: the interdisciplinary Chehrabad Douzlakh project in north-western Iran &#124; Antiquity Jou...</span></span></span>

## What the bodies reveal about labour and danger

The Salt Men provide unusually direct evidence of the lives of ordinary workers, a group that is often almost invisible in written ancient history.

Their clothing demonstrates that miners wore practical woollen garments and leather footwear suited to difficult underground conditions. Textile studies have identified sophisticated weaving techniques and the use of natural dyes, showing that work clothing was neither crude nor improvised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/BMCs40494" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Analysis of dyes in textiles from the Chehrabad salt mine in Iran &#124; npj Heritage ScienceSeptember 30, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 30, 2014</span></span></span>

Mining tools, preserved timber and extraction marks reveal how salt was quarried. Iron picks and adzes were used to cut the rock, while the arrangement of galleries shows organised extraction rather than casual digging. The evidence suggests mining was an established industry requiring skilled labour and careful planning, even if it still carried considerable risks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/aali333" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[Antiquity Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antiquity JournalAncient salt mining and salt men: the interdisciplinary Chehrabad Douzlakh project in north-western Iran &#124; Antiquity Jou...</span></span></span>

Scientific examination has also revealed details of health and diet. One exceptionally preserved individual contained ancient tapeworm eggs, providing the earliest known evidence of intestinal parasites in ancient Iran and suggesting the consumption of raw or undercooked meat. Stable isotope studies, archaeobotanical remains and animal bones continue to improve understanding of what mining communities ate and how they obtained food.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8278039/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 14, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 14, 2021</span></span></span>

The preservation extends even further. Ancient DNA has survived in both human and animal remains, allowing researchers to investigate ancestry, livestock management and the wider economy that supported mining communities. One study of a naturally preserved sheep demonstrated exceptional DNA survival and shed light on specialised flocks supplying the miners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8278039/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 14, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 14, 2021</span></span></span>

## What remains uncertain

Despite decades of excavation, several important questions remain unresolved.

The exact number of Salt Men is still debated because some isolated body parts may belong either to known individuals or to previously unidentified victims. Ongoing excavation and DNA analysis may eventually clarify this.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8278039/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 14, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 14, 2021</span></span></span>

Researchers also continue to investigate how mining was organised. It is not yet fully understood whether the workers were state employees, local labourers, seasonal specialists or part of a more complex economic system that changed between the Achaemenid and Sasanian periods. The archaeology shows differences in mining activity across time, but many administrative details have not survived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity-project-gallery/article/ancient-salt-mining-and-salt-men-the-interdisciplinary-chehrabad-douzlakh-project-in-northwestern-iran/8DAAF71123DC15034002DD55F2D2DDAB" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAncient salt mining and salt men: the interdisciplinary Chehrabad Douzlakh project in north-wester...</span></span></span>

Likewise, although collapse is accepted as the most likely cause of death for the miners, individual circumstances cannot always be reconstructed. Some victims appear to have died instantly beneath falling rock, while others may have been trapped during larger structural failures. The evidence is strongest for sudden cave-ins rather than a single dramatic catastrophe affecting the entire mine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/aali333" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[Antiquity Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antiquity JournalAncient salt mining and salt men: the interdisciplinary Chehrabad Douzlakh project in north-western Iran &#124; Antiquity Jou...</span></span></span>

## Why the Salt Men matter in Iran's historical mysteries

The mystery of the Salt Men lies less in who they were than in how completely chance preserved their final moments. Unlike many famous archaeological discoveries, the uncertainty does not centre on hidden treasure or disputed identity. Instead, Chehrabad offers an exceptionally rare window into repeated industrial disasters that would normally have vanished from history without leaving any trace.

Because the mine preserved both people and their working environment, researchers can compare miners separated by hundreds of years and ask how technology, health, diet and labour changed over time. Few archaeological sites anywhere in the world allow such direct comparisons between successive mining communities.

For that reason, the Salt Men occupy a distinctive place in Iran's historical heritage. They remind us that the history of ancient empires was shaped not only by kings and battles but also by the dangerous work of ordinary people whose lives—and sudden deaths—were unexpectedly preserved beneath layers of salt for more than two millennia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity-project-gallery/article/ancient-salt-mining-and-salt-men-the-interdisciplinary-chehrabad-douzlakh-project-in-northwestern-iran/8DAAF71123DC15034002DD55F2D2DDAB" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAncient salt mining and salt men: the interdisciplinary Chehrabad Douzlakh project in north-wester...</span></span></span>
