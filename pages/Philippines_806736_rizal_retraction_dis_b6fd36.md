## Introduction

Did José Rizal truly retract his criticisms of the Catholic Church before he was executed on 30 December 1896? The short answer is that a retraction document almost certainly existed, but its authenticity, exact wording, circumstances and meaning remain among the most fiercely debated questions in Philippine history. The dispute is not simply about whether Rizal signed a piece of paper. It concerns whether his final hours represented a sincere religious reconciliation, a pragmatic decision under the shadow of execution, or a later narrative shaped by political and religious interests. More than a century later, historians continue to examine witness testimony, handwriting, archival records and contemporary publications without reaching complete agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhcp.gov.ph/did-rizal-consider-retracting-while-in-dapitan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhcp.gov.ph">[NHCP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhcp.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 19, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2012</span></span></span>

## What the surviving retraction document says

The text traditionally presented as Rizal's retraction declares that he remained a Catholic, rejected ideas contrary to Church teaching, renounced Freemasonry and authorised Church authorities to publish his declaration. Versions of the text also state that he withdrew writings and actions inconsistent with his faith.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.joserizal.ph/rt03.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: joserizal.ph">[Jose Rizal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">joserizal.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on joserizal.ph.</span></span></span>

The mystery begins because there is not a single uncontested document. Several different texts circulated soon after Rizal's execution:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A version published in newspapers immediately after his death.
* A longer version later attributed to Jesuit priest Fr. Vicente Balaguer.
* A handwritten "original" said to have been rediscovered in the Archdiocese of Manila archives in 1935 after being missing for nearly four decades.
* Shorter printed formulae that differ in wording and punctuation.

</div>

These variations have fuelled arguments over whether the surviving documents represent copies, editorial revisions, or evidence that no definitive original can be established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.joserizal.ph/rt03.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: joserizal.ph">[Jose Rizal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">joserizal.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on joserizal.ph.</span></span></span>

Importantly, even if Rizal signed a retraction, the surviving texts focus primarily on religious matters. They do not contain a broad political confession or an endorsement of Spanish colonial rule. That distinction is often lost in popular retellings.

## Why the evidence remains contested

The debate has never depended on a single question. Instead, historians evaluate several different kinds of evidence together.

### Witnesses from Rizal's final hours

Several Jesuit priests, including Fr. Balaguer and Fr. José Vilaclara, later maintained that Rizal confessed, received communion and signed a written retraction before dawn on the day of his execution. Spanish military personnel also reported that a reconciliation had taken place. Contemporary Church authorities likewise announced that Rizal had returned to the Catholic faith shortly before his death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.filipinaslibrary.org.ph/articles/rizals-last-hours/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: filipinaslibrary.org.ph">[Filipinas Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">filipinaslibrary.org.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Filipinas Library Filipinas Heritage Library &#124; Rizal’s Last Hours</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Filipinas Library Filipinas Heritage Library &#124; Rizal’s Last Hours</span></span></span>

Supporters of the retraction argue that it would require an implausibly large conspiracy for multiple witnesses to fabricate consistent accounts immediately after the execution.

Sceptics respond that many of the principal witnesses belonged to institutions with a strong interest in demonstrating the Church's moral victory over its most famous critic. They also note that several detailed testimonies appeared years after the event, allowing memory and institutional narratives to influence recollections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhcp.gov.ph/did-rizal-consider-retracting-while-in-dapitan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhcp.gov.ph">[NHCP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhcp.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 19, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2012</span></span></span>

### Handwriting and archival custody

The greatest controversy surrounds the document itself.

The handwritten version now associated with Rizal surfaced publicly only in 1935 after reportedly disappearing from Church custody for thirty-nine years. That long gap naturally raised questions about its chain of custody. Critics argued that the absence of continuous documentation weakened confidence that the manuscript was genuinely Rizal's.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.joserizal.ph/rt03.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: joserizal.ph">[Jose Rizal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">joserizal.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on joserizal.ph.</span></span></span>

Handwriting comparisons have also produced disagreement. Some examiners concluded that the signature and handwriting are consistent with Rizal's known writing. Others, including critics such as Ricardo Pascual, argued that differences in letter formation and style suggest the document may not have been written entirely by Rizal. Modern historians generally recognise that handwriting analysis alone cannot settle the issue conclusively, particularly when comparing hurried writing produced under exceptional circumstances with carefully written earlier manuscripts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/946324728/rizal-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Understanding the Rizal Retraction Debate &#124; PDF &#124; Witness &#124; Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Understanding the Rizal Retraction Debate &#124; PDF &#124; Witness &#124; Archive...</span></span></span>

Rather than producing certainty, the surviving manuscript has become another piece of evidence whose interpretation depends partly on broader judgments about provenance and witness credibility.

## Did religious reconciliation erase Rizal's political beliefs?

One of the most common misunderstandings is to assume that a religious retraction would automatically invalidate Rizal's lifelong criticism of colonial injustice.

That conclusion does not follow from the evidence.

Throughout his career, Rizal distinguished between Catholic belief and the abuses committed by some religious orders within the colonial system. His novels criticised corruption, arbitrary power and the political influence of certain friars rather than Christianity itself. Consequently, it is entirely possible—in principle—for someone to reconcile with the Catholic Church while continuing to believe that colonial abuses had been real.

This distinction has become increasingly important in modern scholarship. Even historians who accept that Rizal probably returned to Catholic practice before his execution often argue that such a decision says relatively little about his political philosophy or his criticism of colonial oppression. Conversely, historians who reject the retraction generally do not dispute the broader historical record of Rizal's reformist writings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhcp.gov.ph/did-rizal-consider-retracting-while-in-dapitan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhcp.gov.ph">[NHCP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhcp.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 19, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2012</span></span></span>

## Did Rizal consider retracting before his execution?

An intriguing dimension of the debate comes from Jesuit correspondence written while Rizal was still in exile in Dapitan.

Letters from Fr. Antonio Obach, published from Jesuit missionary archives, report conversations in which Rizal supposedly indicated that he was willing to consider retracting under certain conditions, including restoration of his liberty, compensation for confiscated family property and financial security. Obach portrayed Rizal as tired of conflict but still negotiating the terms under which any reconciliation might occur.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhcp.gov.ph/did-rizal-consider-retracting-while-in-dapitan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhcp.gov.ph">[NHCP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhcp.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 19, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2012</span></span></span>

These letters do not prove that Rizal later signed the famous retraction. They do, however, complicate the older assumption that the very idea of a retraction was completely incompatible with his character. They suggest that discussions about reconciliation may have begun well before the final night in Fort Santiago.

Historians disagree over how much weight these letters deserve. Some regard them as valuable contemporary evidence of Rizal's evolving thinking. Others caution that they survive only through the correspondence of a priest who hoped to persuade Rizal back into the Church.

## Why the controversy never disappears

The debate persists because the stakes extend beyond one historical document.

For many Filipinos, Rizal symbolises intellectual independence and resistance to colonial oppression. If he genuinely retracted, some fear that his moral authority would be diminished. Others argue that acknowledging a religious reconciliation does not lessen either his patriotism or the significance of his writings.

The controversy has therefore become a debate about historical interpretation as much as documentary evidence. Different generations have approached the same sources with different assumptions about nationalism, religion and historical memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nhcp.gov.ph/did-rizal-consider-retracting-while-in-dapitan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhcp.gov.ph">[NHCP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhcp.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 19, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2012</span></span></span>

The issue has also encouraged increasingly careful historical methods. Rather than relying on a single dramatic discovery, researchers compare newspaper reports, Church archives, military records, eyewitness testimony, manuscript provenance and handwriting studies. The result has been greater understanding of Rizal's final hours, even if complete certainty remains elusive.

## The most balanced assessment

Most historians today avoid the extremes of either unquestioningly accepting every traditional account or dismissing all evidence as fabrication.

Several conclusions command relatively broad support:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Rizal spent his final hours in extensive discussion with Jesuit priests.
* Contemporary witnesses believed that some form of religious reconciliation occurred.
* A retraction text circulated immediately after his execution.
* A handwritten document later surfaced that many scholars regard as important but not beyond dispute.
* The surviving evidence does not conclusively resolve every question about authorship, wording or motivation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.joserizal.ph/rt03.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: joserizal.ph">[joserizal.ph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">joserizal.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on joserizal.ph.</span></span></span>

</div>

The enduring mystery therefore lies less in whether a document existed than in how it should be interpreted. Even if Rizal reconciled with the Catholic Church shortly before his death, that does not necessarily mean he abandoned his commitment to justice, reform or the criticisms of colonial abuse that made him one of the [Philippines]({{ 'philippines/' | relative_url }})' most influential historical figures. It is precisely this distinction—between private faith and public political conviction—that keeps the debate over Rizal's final hours alive in Philippine historical memory.
