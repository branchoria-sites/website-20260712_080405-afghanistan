## Introduction

Christopher Columbus reached an island called Guanahani on 12 October 1492, marking the first recorded European landfall of his voyage in the Americas. Yet more than five centuries later, historians, geographers and archaeologists still cannot agree which modern island Guanahani actually was. The leading candidates lie in [The Bahamas]({{ 'the-bahamas/' | relative_url }}), with present-day San Salvador Island holding official recognition, while Samana Cay and several other islands continue to attract serious scholarly support. The debate persists not because historians lack interest, but because the single most important piece of evidence—Columbus's original shipboard journal—has been lost. What survives is incomplete, sometimes ambiguous, and open to multiple interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/columbus-makes-landfall-caribbean/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: education.nationalgeographic.org">[nationalgeographic.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">education.nationalgeographic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Geographic Education Columbus Makes Landfall in the Caribbean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geographic EducationColumbus Makes Landfall in the CaribbeanJune 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2026</span></span></span>

Unlike many historical mysteries, this is not driven by conspiracy theories. It is a genuine problem of historical geography in which small differences in navigation, changing coastlines, missing documents and the remarkably similar appearance of many Bahamian islands all complicate attempts to reconstruct a voyage made in the fifteenth century.

## What the surviving voyage account actually says

The central source for the debate is Columbus's diario (journal). The original manuscript disappeared centuries ago. Modern historians rely mainly on a summary and partial transcription prepared by the Dominican friar and historian Bartolomé de las Casas, who appears to have copied substantial sections while also condensing others. Because scholars cannot compare his version with the lost original, it is impossible to know precisely where details may have been abbreviated or interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1988/september/looking-columbus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Looking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteLooking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1988 Volume 2 Number 4...</span></span></span>

The surviving account nevertheless provides valuable clues. Columbus described:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a low, green island with abundant fresh water;
* a large inland lagoon or lake;
* reefs and a harbour suitable for anchorage;
* nearby islands visible from the shore;
* the sailing distances and compass directions followed after leaving Guanahani.

</div>

Individually, these observations are not distinctive. Many islands across the Bahamian archipelago share similar landscapes. The challenge is finding a single island that matches not only Columbus's first description but also the sequence of later voyages towards Cuba recorded over the following days.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1988/september/looking-columbus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[usni.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Looking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteLooking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1988 Volume 2 Number 4...</span></span></span>

Complicating matters further, fifteenth-century navigation relied on dead reckoning—estimating speed, direction and distance rather than measuring longitude accurately. Wind, current and human estimation could all introduce errors that accumulated over several days at sea. Modern reconstructions therefore must account for uncertainty rather than assuming every recorded course and distance was exact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1988/september/looking-columbus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Looking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteLooking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1988 Volume 2 Number 4...</span></span></span>

Although more than nine islands have been proposed over the past two centuries, the modern debate largely centres on two leading candidates.

### Present-day San Salvador

The island officially known today as San Salvador (formerly Watling Island) is the traditional identification and remains the most widely accepted candidate. Its position became especially influential after naval historian Samuel Eliot Morison retraced Columbus's voyage and argued in 1942 that the evidence settled the matter in favour of Watling Island. The Bahamian legislature had already renamed Watling Island as San Salvador in the 1920s, reinforcing the historical association.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1992/september/columbus-revealed" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Columbus Revealed &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteColumbus Revealed &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1992 Volume 6 Number 3...</span></span></span>

Supporters argue that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the island broadly matches Columbus's physical description;
* the onward route through the Bahamas can be reconstructed successfully from there;
* prevailing winds and currents fit reasonably well with the recorded voyage.

</div>

For these reasons, San Salvador continues to appear in many textbooks and educational resources as the most probable landfall, even while acknowledging that certainty remains impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/columbus-makes-landfall-caribbean/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: education.nationalgeographic.org">[National Geographic Education]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">education.nationalgeographic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Geographic Education Columbus Makes Landfall in the Caribbean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geographic EducationColumbus Makes Landfall in the CaribbeanJune 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2026</span></span></span>

### Samana Cay

In 1986, a National Geographic research team reopened the debate after combining historical translation, navigational analysis, computer modelling and fieldwork. Led by Joseph Judge and colleagues, the project concluded that Samana Cay provided a closer overall fit to the surviving descriptions than San Salvador. They argued that earlier researchers had used an incorrect value for the Spanish sea league, affecting reconstructed sailing distances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1986-10-09-mn-5483-story.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: latimes.com">[latimes.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">latimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Los Angeles TimesNational Geographic Society Finding: Columbus&#x27; First Landing Moved 65 Miles - Los Angeles TimesOctober 9, 1986...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 9, 1986</span></span></span>

Advocates of Samana Cay contend that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the island's shape resembles Columbus's descriptions more closely;
* several geographical features correspond better with the journal;
* the subsequent island-to-island voyage is easier to reconcile using revised navigational calculations.

</div>

Even supporters acknowledge that some features remain imperfect matches, illustrating why the debate has never been conclusively resolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1992/february/landfall-samana-cay" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute The Landfall is Samana Cay &#124; Proceedings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteThe Landfall is Samana Cay &#124; Proceedings - February 1992 Vol. 118/2/1,068...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1992</span></span></span>

### Other proposed locations

Several additional islands have attracted scholarly advocates over the years, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* Conception Island;
* Cat Island;
* Plana Cays;
* East Caicos;
* Grand Turk;
* Great Harbour Cay.

</div>

Most of these proposals attempt to solve one inconsistency while creating others elsewhere in Columbus's recorded route. As a result, none has gained the broad support enjoyed by San Salvador or Samana Cay.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1988/september/looking-columbus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[usni.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Looking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteLooking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1988 Volume 2 Number 4...</span></span></span>

## Why archaeology has not settled the dispute

It might seem that archaeology should provide a definitive answer, but the evidence has proved frustratingly inconclusive.

Lucayan settlements existed across many Bahamian islands before European contact. Discovering Indigenous villages or pottery therefore demonstrates occupation, not that Columbus landed there first. Likewise, the earliest European objects recovered archaeologically may reflect later Spanish visits, trade between Indigenous communities or movement of artefacts after 1492 rather than Columbus's initial landing party.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1988/september/looking-columbus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Looking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteLooking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1988 Volume 2 Number 4...</span></span></span>

Environmental change presents another obstacle. Hurricanes, erosion, shifting sandbars, changing lagoons and sea-level variation have altered many Bahamian coastlines over five centuries. Small differences between Columbus's descriptions and today's landscapes may reflect natural change rather than incorrect identification. Researchers therefore must reconstruct both the historical voyage and the historical geography simultaneously.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1988/september/looking-columbus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Looking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteLooking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1988 Volume 2 Number 4...</span></span></span>

No securely dated archaeological site has yet produced an unmistakable combination of evidence tying it directly to Columbus's first landing. Until such evidence appears, archaeology can narrow possibilities but cannot eliminate all alternatives.

## Why the mystery endures

The continuing debate illustrates how historical uncertainty often survives despite extensive research.

Several factors combine to keep the question open:

* **The original journal is lost.** Historians depend on later copies and summaries rather than Columbus's own manuscript.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1988/september/looking-columbus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Looking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteLooking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1988 Volume 2 Number 4...</span></span></span>
* **Navigation was approximate.** Distances, bearings and speeds recorded in 1492 inevitably contained measurement errors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1992/september/columbus-revealed" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Columbus Revealed &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteColumbus Revealed &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1992 Volume 6 Number 3...</span></span></span>
* **The islands are geographically similar.** Many Bahamian islands are low, flat, reef-fringed and contain lagoons, making descriptions difficult to distinguish.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/columbus-makes-landfall-caribbean/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: education.nationalgeographic.org">[National Geographic Education]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">education.nationalgeographic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Geographic Education Columbus Makes Landfall in the Caribbean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geographic EducationColumbus Makes Landfall in the CaribbeanJune 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2026</span></span></span>
* **Modern landscapes have changed.** Shorelines, inlets and vegetation have evolved considerably since the late fifteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1988/september/looking-columbus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Looking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteLooking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1988 Volume 2 Number 4...</span></span></span>
* **Every theory involves compromises.** Each proposed landfall explains some pieces of evidence better than others, and none fits every surviving detail perfectly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/1988/september/looking-columbus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute Looking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteLooking for Columbus &#124; Naval History Magazine - Fall 1988 Volume 2 Number 4...</span></span></span>

For these reasons, most historians now frame the question in terms of probability rather than certainty. Present-day San Salvador remains the conventional identification, but Samana Cay continues to command serious scholarly attention, while other candidates retain smaller groups of supporters. The dispute is therefore best understood not as a failure of historical research but as a reminder that one missing document can leave even one of world history's most famous voyages permanently open to debate.
