## Introduction

Some of these cases are genuine unresolved puzzles. Others are better described as mysteries that have partly yielded to research but remain controversial because certainty is impossible or politically uncomfortable. Together they show why Tunisia’s past is difficult to reconstruct. Carthage was destroyed by Rome, underwater archaeology began with crude salvage methods, colonial intelligence operations were designed to conceal responsibility, and fragile archives have survived under imperfect conditions. The strongest conclusions therefore come not from a single dramatic clue, but from comparing physical remains, written testimony, scientific analysis and the history of how each claim was produced.

## Did Carthage sacrifice its children?

The most famous and disturbing controversy concerns the ancient burial ground at Carthage commonly called the Tophet. Archaeologists found urns containing the cremated remains of very young children, sometimes mixed with animal bones, beneath stone markers dedicated to Carthaginian deities. Greek and Roman writers also accused the Carthaginians of sacrificing children. The mystery is whether those accounts describe a real religious practice or whether foreign enemies turned an unusual infant cemetery into a tale of barbarity.

The written evidence cannot simply be accepted at face value. Nearly all surviving literary descriptions were produced outside Carthage, often by societies that fought it or regarded its religion with hostility. Carthage’s own historical literature was largely lost after the Roman destruction of the city in 146 BC. This creates a fundamental imbalance: the accused civilisation cannot answer its accusers, while later readers inherit the version written by its rivals.

The physical evidence is also harder to interpret than it first appears. A 2010 study led by Jeffrey Schwartz argued that the age distribution of the remains was consistent with infants who had died naturally before or shortly after birth. From this perspective, the site may have been a special cemetery for children too young to receive ordinary burial. The researchers warned that cremation distorts bones, making precise age estimates difficult, and concluded that the skeletons did not prove systematic sacrifice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCSkeletal Remains from Punic Carthage Do Not Support</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by JH Schwartz · 2010 · Cited by 130 — Child sacrifice: children of Phoenician Punic Carthage were not sacrificed to the gods.... The...</span></span></span>

Other specialists reached the opposite conclusion. Patricia Smith and colleagues argued that the remains included children older than the natural-death model predicted, and that the combination of cremation, animal offerings and dedicatory inscriptions was more consistent with ritual sacrifice. A broader interdisciplinary assessment published in *Antiquity* described the cumulative case for sacrifice as strong, while acknowledging that individual deaths cannot normally be reconstructed from burnt fragments alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/cemetery-or-sacrifice-infant-burials-at-the-carthage-tophet/DAC7C386CD20F5C280C9DB41E5184A2E" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment Cemetery or sacrifice?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Infant burials at the Carthage Tophetby P Xella · 2013 · Cited by 111 — Interdisciplinary research on urns from the Carthaginian Tophet a...</span></span></span>

The disagreement continued because researchers were not merely interpreting the same bones differently; they were disputing how those bones should be measured. Cremation causes shrinkage, fragmentation and warping. A difference of a few weeks in an estimated age can change whether a burial pattern looks like ordinary infant mortality or deliberate selection. Schwartz and his colleagues later defended the natural-death interpretation, stressing that inference had too often been presented as fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/two-tales-of-one-city-data-inference-and-carthaginian-infant-sacrifice/5006E240CB75A1E324B3230F6DA17389" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentTwo tales of one city: data, inference and Carthaginian...by JH Schwartz · 2017 · Cited by 37 — R...</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusion is therefore narrower than either popular extreme. Carthaginian child sacrifice was probably not invented from nothing by Roman propagandists, and the archaeological context makes some form of ritual killing plausible. Yet the Tophet cannot be treated as a straightforward record of thousands of murdered firstborn children. It may have contained sacrifices, natural deaths or both, perhaps under circumstances that varied over centuries. The unresolved question is less “did it ever happen?” than “how often, under what conditions, and what proportion of the burials did it represent?”

The case matters because it illustrates how a civilisation’s reputation can be shaped by the survival of evidence. Modern accounts still repeat lurid images inherited from hostile ancient texts, while sceptical reinterpretations can sometimes swing too far by treating all accusations as propaganda. The Tophet remains a real archaeological puzzle precisely because neither the bones nor the texts can settle the matter independently.

## Where was the Mahdia ship carrying its treasures?

In 1907, Greek sponge divers working off Mahdia encountered a large ancient wreck in roughly 40 metres of water. Salvage and excavation produced bronze and marble sculptures, columns, furniture fittings, architectural pieces, inscriptions, lamps, coins, anchors and other cargo. Much of the recovered material entered the Bardo Museum. The ship is generally dated to the late second or early first century BC, making it one of the Mediterranean’s most important early underwater archaeological discoveries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/archeo-sous-marine/en/heavy-gear-and-mahdia-shipwreck" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[gouv.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">heavy gear and mahdia shipwreck</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">heavy gear and mahdia shipwreck</span></span></span>

The familiar explanation is that the vessel was carrying Greek art from the eastern Mediterranean to wealthy Roman buyers in Italy. Its cargo fits a period when Roman elites collected Greek sculpture and architectural decoration. The wreck has sometimes been linked more specifically to Roman plunder after the general Sulla’s sack of Athens in 86 BC, with the ship imagined as transporting seized treasures westwards.

That story is plausible, but it is not proved. A modern reassessment notes that the Athens-to-Italy route became influential partly because scholars connected the wreck to literary descriptions of Roman art collecting and looting. The archaeological remains themselves do not establish a starting port or final destination with scientific certainty. Even objects associated with Athens may have been traded through several ports before being loaded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hull-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/4672393" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hull-repository.worktribe.com">[Hull Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hull-repository.worktribe.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on worktribe.com.</span></span></span>

The cargo is less uniform than the phrase “ship of stolen Greek masterpieces” suggests. It included recently manufactured decorative works, domestic furnishings, architectural components and everyday shipboard equipment as well as sculpture. Some pieces may have been commercial products made for export rather than famous originals removed from temples. The assemblage therefore looks more like the cargo of a large merchant vessel serving the luxury market than a single aristocrat’s private collection or an organised shipment of war booty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hull-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/4672393" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hull-repository.worktribe.com">[Hull Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hull-repository.worktribe.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on worktribe.com.</span></span></span>

Several details remain uncertain because the wreck was discovered before modern underwater archaeology existed. Early work concentrated on lifting impressive objects, while records and complete inventories were inconsistent. Researchers still face difficulty separating cargo from crew equipment and reconstructing the exact position of objects on the seabed. Later studies have improved the picture, but parts of the site and its documentation remain incomplete.

The ship’s loss was probably caused by bad weather or navigational trouble, although no surviving account records the disaster. Its position off Tunisia does not necessarily mean Mahdia was its intended destination; a vessel crossing the central Mediterranean could have been driven towards the North African coast. Yet a Tunisian port of call cannot be ruled out either. The wreck is consequently both a treasure discovery and a commercial mystery: the objects are known, but the voyage that brought them together remains partly conjectural.

## Who ordered Tunisia’s political assassinations?

Twentieth-century Tunisia produced mysteries of a different kind. Here the difficulty is not burnt bone or a scattered shipwreck, but deliberate secrecy. [Political killings]({{ 'political-killings-221b25/' | relative_url }}) during the colonial and early post-independence periods involved clandestine organisations, intelligence services and rival nationalist factions. In several cases, the identity of the immediate killers is less uncertain than the chain of command behind them.

### Farhat Hached and the hidden machinery of colonial violence

Farhat Hached, founder of the Tunisian General Labour Union and an internationally recognised figure in the independence movement, was murdered near Tunis on 5 December 1952. He was first shot from a passing car and then killed by a second group. Responsibility was publicly associated with the Red Hand, presented at the time as a shadowy extremist organisation defending French rule.

The enduring mystery concerns how independent that organisation really was. Historical research has connected the Red Hand to French security and intelligence structures operating in North Africa. A scholarly study of the organisation concluded that Hached’s murder was carried out with the agreement of French officials in Tunisia, supporting the view that the group functioned as a deniable instrument of state violence rather than merely a private band of colonial militants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/26296892" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

The political context strengthens that interpretation. Hached was under surveillance, had received threats and was viewed by colonial authorities as a major danger because he connected labour organising, Tunisian nationalism and international trade-union support. The assassination provoked unrest far beyond Tunisia and turned him into a symbol of the independence struggle.

What remains unclear is the complete authorisation trail. Which officials approved the operation? How high did knowledge extend within the French state? Were orders written, conveyed orally or deliberately fragmented to preserve deniability? Tunisian demands for fuller access to French archives reflect the fact that broad responsibility and precise command responsibility are not the same thing. In 2013, French President François Hollande promised cooperation over records concerning the murder, but public debate over the completeness of the archival record continued.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nawaat.org/2013/07/05/francois-hollande-sengage-a-restituer-toutes-les-archives-sur-lassassinat-de-farhat-hached/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nawaat.org">[Nawaat]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nawaat.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nawaat.org.</span></span></span>

The case should therefore no longer be described as an entirely unsolved murder. The evidence strongly points towards a French-linked covert operation. The unresolved portion lies in institutional detail: the names, decisions and communications that transformed political hostility into an assassination.

### Salah Ben Youssef and the struggle after independence

Salah Ben Youssef was Habib Bourguiba’s principal rival within the Tunisian nationalist movement. Their dispute concerned both leadership and strategy. Bourguiba accepted a gradual path towards independence negotiated with France, while Ben Youssef opposed the settlement and promoted a broader North African and Arab nationalist position. After losing the internal struggle, Ben Youssef fled Tunisia. He was shot dead in a Frankfurt hotel on 12 August 1961.

Suspicion long centred on agents loyal to Bourguiba, but for decades the assassination sat uneasily within Tunisia’s official national story. Bourguiba was celebrated as the founder of the modern state, making serious investigation of his government’s role politically difficult. Witness accounts, speeches and scattered records existed, but there was no open process capable of assembling them without deference to the presidency.

Tunisia’s Truth and Dignity Commission, created after the 2011 revolution to investigate state abuses, revisited the case. Its work concluded that Tunisian state agencies were responsible and referred the assassination to a specialised court. Proceedings identified Bourguiba and several other figures as suspects, although most were already dead and the hearings became entangled in arguments over law, political memory and the legitimacy of transitional justice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://transitionaljusticedata.org/public_files/reportTCID193.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: transitionaljusticedata.org">[transitionaljusticedata.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">transitionaljusticedata.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">report TCID193</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">report TCID193</span></span></span>

The broad explanation is now stronger than it once was: Ben Youssef was almost certainly killed as part of a state-directed operation against a defeated political rival. The remaining uncertainty concerns the exact division of responsibility. Did Bourguiba personally issue a direct order, approve a general plan, or create a system in which subordinates understood what was expected? Those distinctions matter historically, but they are difficult to establish when participants are dead and crucial records are missing or inaccessible.

The case remains sensitive because it challenges a simple independence narrative in which national unity followed the end of colonial rule. Ben Youssef’s death reveals that the founding of the Tunisian republic also involved internal repression and the violent removal of alternative political futures.

## Why are Kairouan’s manuscripts deteriorating?

Kairouan holds one of the world’s most important collections of early Islamic manuscripts, including parchment works dating from the tenth century or earlier. The collection grew from the city’s religious and scholarly institutions and preserves evidence about law, theology, book production and intellectual life in medieval North Africa. It is significant not only to Tunisia but to the history of the written book.

A modern conservation mystery emerged when specialists observed an unusual form of damage affecting some parchment leaves. The edges darken, become translucent and brittle, distort and appear to gelatinise. More troublingly, the affected areas can continue to expand despite careful storage. Experienced conservators reported that the speed, severity and localised nature of the deterioration differed from the more even, gradual ageing normally seen in parchment. The pattern did not appear to be ordinary ink corrosion, and no simple rule explained which manuscripts were affected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/news/2025-01-08-tunisian-delegation-visit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csmc.uni-hamburg.de">[csmc.uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csmc.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uni-hamburg.de.</span></span></span>

In late 2024, Tunisian specialists took 15 affected objects to the University of Hamburg’s Centre for the Study of Manuscript Cultures. Researchers used methods including multispectral imaging, X-ray fluorescence, genetic sampling, three-dimensional microscopy and atomic-force microscopy. The aim was to identify the chemical, biological or structural processes driving the damage without sacrificing material from irreplaceable pages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/news/2025-01-08-tunisian-delegation-visit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csmc.uni-hamburg.de">[csmc.uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csmc.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uni-hamburg.de.</span></span></span>

Several possible mechanisms must be considered. Parchment is processed animal skin, so its stability depends on the original skin, manufacturing techniques, environmental history and later conservation treatments. Heat and moisture can alter collagen, the main structural protein, while salts, microorganisms or past repairs may accelerate local decay. Yet a plausible list of causes is not the same as a diagnosis. The unusual distribution and rapid development of the Kairouan damage are precisely what make it difficult to explain.

This mystery is different from the Tophet or Ben Youssef assassination because it is an active scientific investigation rather than a permanently closed historical scene. Better imaging, chemical analysis and comparison with unaffected manuscripts may produce a clear answer. The stakes are immediate: identifying the process could determine how the collection is stored, handled and treated, and could prevent the loss of texts that have survived for roughly a millennium.

## What Tunisia’s mysteries actually tell us

Tunisia’s historical enigmas survive for different reasons, and treating them all alike produces bad history. At Carthage, the evidence is ancient, fragmentary and filtered through enemy accounts. At Mahdia, early salvage practices removed objects before their complete archaeological relationships could be recorded. In the political assassinations, secrecy was intentional and archives became instruments of power. In Kairouan, the evidence is present but the physical process remains scientifically obscure.

The cases can be separated into four useful categories:

* **A disputed interpretation:** the [Carthage Tophet]({{ 'carthage-tophet/' | relative_url }}) is real, but its meaning remains contested.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **An incomplete reconstruction:** the Mahdia ship and cargo are known, while its route, customers and final purpose are uncertain.
* **A concealed political history:** the Hached and Ben Youssef killings are increasingly understood, although full command responsibility remains difficult to prove.
* **An unresolved material process:** the Kairouan manuscripts are visibly deteriorating, but research has not yet established a complete cause.

</div>

This distinction protects the subject from both credulity and excessive scepticism. It would be misleading to claim that nothing is known about these cases. It would be equally misleading to present a persuasive theory as settled fact. The real fascination lies in seeing exactly where certainty ends: at a cremated fragment whose age cannot be measured precisely, a ship’s cargo without a manifest, an assassination without an accessible order, or a manuscript whose damaged collagen has not yet disclosed what happened to it.

Tunisia’s mysteries also reflect the country’s position at the centre of Mediterranean history. Phoenician, Roman, Islamic, European colonial and modern national histories overlap there, leaving evidence dispersed across ruins, seabeds, foreign archives, museums and laboratories. What remains unresolved is therefore not peripheral to Tunisia’s past. The gaps themselves reveal who recorded history, who destroyed it, who controlled access to it and what modern science can—and cannot yet—recover.
