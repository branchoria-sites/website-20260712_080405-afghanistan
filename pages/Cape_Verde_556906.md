## Were the islands really uninhabited?

The conventional account says that Portuguese navigators reached the Cape Verde islands around 1460 and found no settled population. Santiago was colonised soon afterwards, and Ribeira Grande—now Cidade Velha—became the first permanent European colonial town in the tropics. UNESCO’s assessment of the site describes the area as previously uninhabited, and the surviving historical record contains no firm account of an established pre-Portuguese community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1310/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreCidade Velha, Historic Centre of Ribeira GrandeThe town of Ribeira Grande, renamed Cidade Velha in the late 1...</span></span></span>

That does not prove that no one had ever visited. Cape Verde lies several hundred kilometres west of the African mainland, within reach of sailors familiar with the coast of present-day Senegal, the Gambia and Guinea. Fishing parties, storm-driven craft or occasional navigators could theoretically have landed without founding settlements or leaving durable structures. Early European claims of “discovery” also have to be read carefully: they usually meant discovery within a European geographical and political system, not necessarily the first sighting by any human being.

The difficulty is evidential. No securely dated pre-fifteenth-century settlement, burial ground or artefact assemblage has been accepted as proof of earlier occupation. Archaeological work on Santiago has instead revealed material associated with the first Portuguese and West African communities created after colonisation. West African pottery and locally made wares demonstrate that African people and practices were present from the beginning of colonial society, but they do not establish an earlier, independent population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/excavating-alcatrazes-santiago-island-cape-verde-early-colonial-impacts-on-land-people-and-material-culture/D8CF4E8A457FF4852ED9EEC66148F8A4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

The fairest conclusion is therefore cautious. Cape Verde was probably uninhabited when permanent Portuguese settlement began, but occasional earlier visits cannot be ruled out. A convincing challenge to the established account would require an artefact or site found in an undisturbed context and reliably dated to before about 1460. Until then, claims of ancient colonies, Phoenician outposts or forgotten African kingdoms belong to speculation rather than demonstrated history.

## Who ordered Amílcar Cabral’s assassination?

The assassination of Amílcar Cabral is Cape Verde’s most consequential unresolved political mystery. Born in Guinea to Cape Verdean parents, Cabral became the principal strategist of the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde. Although the armed struggle was fought mainly in Portuguese Guinea, his political project sought the liberation and eventual union of both territories. He was killed in Conakry on 20 January 1973, months before Guinea-Bissau declared independence and two years before Cape Verde became independent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve06/d66" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[state.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The assassination January 20 in Conakry, Guinea, of Amilcar Cabral, Secretary General of the African Party...Read more...</span></span></span>

The immediate killers were not mysterious outsiders. Cabral was stopped near his residence by members of his own organisation and shot during an attempted takeover. Inocêncio Kani, a PAIGC naval commander, was identified as a leading participant. The conspirators also tried to seize other party leaders and escape by sea, but their operation failed. A contemporary United States intelligence assessment reported that Kani had confessed after his flight was blocked with the assistance of a Soviet vessel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve06/d66" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The assassination January 20 in Conakry, Guinea, of Amilcar Cabral, Secretary General of the African Party...Read more...</span></span></span>

What remains disputed is who stood behind the plot. Three explanations dominate.

**An internal PAIGC rebellion.** Cabral’s movement contained serious tensions between Cape Verdean and mainland Guinean members, as well as personal rivalries, disciplinary grievances and disputes over authority. The gunmen came from within this environment, making an internally generated coup attempt entirely plausible. The United States assessment written soon after the killing treated the assassination primarily as the product of factional conflict, although intelligence available at the time was incomplete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve06/d66" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The assassination January 20 in Conakry, Guinea, of Amilcar Cabral, Secretary General of the African Party...Read more...</span></span></span>

**Manipulation by Portuguese intelligence.** Portugal’s political police had a clear motive to remove Cabral and had previously sought ways to weaken the PAIGC. Some historical accounts argue that Portuguese agents cultivated dissidents or encouraged a plan to abduct him, which then became a murder. Yet publicly available evidence has never established a complete chain of command from Lisbon to the gunmen. The declassified American assessment did not find direct Portuguese responsibility, but neither did it regard possible complicity as conclusively excluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve06/d66" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The assassination January 20 in Conakry, Guinea, of Amilcar Cabral, Secretary General of the African Party...Read more...</span></span></span>

**Involvement by Guinea’s president, Ahmed Sékou Touré.** Cabral operated from exile in Guinea and depended on Touré’s government for protection. Later speculation suggested that Touré feared Cabral’s growing prestige or wanted tighter control over the liberation movement. This theory has never been supported by evidence as strong as that establishing the internal conspirators, and it remains the least securely demonstrated of the major explanations.

The aftermath made reconstruction more difficult. Large numbers of alleged participants were arrested, and many PAIGC members accused of involvement were summarily killed. Those executions removed witnesses who might have clarified the planning, financing and external contacts behind the coup. They also meant that much of the surviving narrative came from victors, intelligence agencies or later political memoirs rather than a transparent judicial investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Am%C3%ADlcar_Cabral" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Amílcar Cabral</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amílcar Cabral</span></span></span>

The strongest interpretation is not that either an internal plot or foreign interference must be chosen exclusively. The assassination was physically carried out by PAIGC dissidents whose grievances were real. Portuguese intelligence may nevertheless have encouraged, infiltrated or attempted to exploit those divisions. What remains unproved is the decisive connection: an authenticated order, payment record, operational report or testimony independently linking the killers to an external sponsor.

For Cape Verde, the uncertainty matters because Cabral became the moral and intellectual figurehead of independence. Competing explanations for his death also imply different national lessons. One emphasises colonial subversion; another warns of internal division and authoritarian violence within liberation movements. The unresolved case sits uneasily between both histories.

## The colonial settlement that disappeared

Alcatrazes on Santiago offers a different kind of mystery: not who committed an act, but how an important settlement almost vanished from recorded memory. It was established as the centre of Cape Verde’s second colonial captaincy during the early Portuguese occupation. Written references and the ruins of the Gothic church of Nossa Senhora da Luz indicated its approximate location, yet the settlement around the church remained largely unidentified for centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/finding-alcatrazes-and-early-lusoafrican-settlement-on-santiago-island-cape-verde/8339B945BB080CA4CFE0C8D84E6ED78A" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentFinding Alcatrazes and early Luso-African settlement on...by C Evans · 2017 · Cited by 12 — The g...</span></span></span>

Archaeological survey located the associated settlement in 2012. Excavations subsequently uncovered buildings, imported goods, West African pottery and locally produced ceramics. The discoveries showed that occupation continued after official administrative functions were transferred elsewhere, correcting the simple picture of a failed settlement abruptly abandoned when its political importance declined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/excavating-alcatrazes-santiago-island-cape-verde-early-colonial-impacts-on-land-people-and-material-culture/D8CF4E8A457FF4852ED9EEC66148F8A4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Alcatrazes is important because written colonial records concentrate on governors, churches, trade and official decisions. Archaeology exposes the less documented population living between those entries: African labourers, enslaved people, craftspeople, farmers, traders and mixed households. Pottery fragments and food remains can reveal cultural practices that administrators either ignored or did not understand.

The site is no longer “lost” in the literal sense, but many questions remain. Researchers are still reconstructing its full extent, population and relationship with nearby rural communities. They are also investigating how imported plants, animals and farming methods transformed Santiago’s environment. Recent excavations indicate a more enduring and culturally complex settlement than the sparse administrative record suggested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/excavating-alcatrazes-santiago-island-cape-verde-early-colonial-impacts-on-land-people-and-material-culture/D8CF4E8A457FF4852ED9EEC66148F8A4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Alcatrazes demonstrates how places disappear historically even when their ruins remain visible. A church survived above ground, but the everyday settlement around it became almost invisible because maps, memory and official records preserved only part of the story.

## What lies beneath Cape Verde’s waters?

Cape Verde stands beside shipping routes that once connected Europe, West Africa, Brazil and the Indian Ocean. Reefs, currents, poor charts and sudden weather made the islands hazardous, leaving an exceptional concentration of wrecks. A modern national catalogue has recorded about 141 shipwrecks, while archaeological projects investigated dozens of sites around the islands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/43516148/Maritime_Archaeology_in_Cabo_Verde_wrecks_interventions_report" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maritime Archaeology in Cabo Verde, wrecks interventions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maritime Archaeology in Cabo Verde, wrecks interventions</span></span></span>

Among the most famous are British East India Company ships carrying valuable cargoes:

* **Princess Louisa**, wrecked near Maio in 1743 with chests of Spanish silver aboard.
* **Hartwell**, lost near Boa Vista in 1787 while reportedly carrying several tonnes of silver.
* **Lady Burgess**, wrecked near Boa Vista in 1806 on a voyage towards India.
* **Le Dromadaire**, a French East India Company vessel lost in 1762.
* **USS Yorktown**, an American naval sloop wrecked near Maio in 1850.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/43516148/Maritime_Archaeology_in_Cabo_Verde_wrecks_interventions_report" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[academia.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maritime Archaeology in Cabo Verde, wrecks interventions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maritime Archaeology in Cabo Verde, wrecks interventions</span></span></span>

</div>

These wrecks invite a simple treasure-hunt question: how much silver or valuable cargo remains unfound? Historically, however, the more serious mystery is how much information has already been lost through commercial recovery.

A shipwreck is not merely a container of coins. The location of every cannon, tool, personal possession, food vessel and timber fragment can help reconstruct how a ship was built, how its crew lived and how the vessel broke apart. When objects are removed rapidly for sale, their relationship to one another may be lost. Coins can survive in a collection while the evidence that explained where they were stored, how the cargo was organised or what happened during the sinking disappears.

Commercial operations around Cape Verde recovered large quantities of material during the late twentieth century. Work on Princess Louisa, for example, involved hundreds of dives and the recovery of lead ingots, coin groups and other artefacts. Some recovered material remained in Cape Verde, while other objects entered the international auction market.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/43516148/Maritime_Archaeology_in_Cabo_Verde_wrecks_interventions_report" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maritime Archaeology in Cabo Verde, wrecks interventions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maritime Archaeology in Cabo Verde, wrecks interventions</span></span></span>

This created two overlapping uncertainties. The first concerns identification: scattered wreckage may be assigned to a named ship using cargo, guns, construction details and archival records, but disturbed sites make conclusions harder. The second concerns ownership and public value: should wreck material be treated as saleable treasure, state property, the heritage of the flag nation, or evidence belonging to humanity’s shared maritime past?

UNESCO’s underwater-heritage framework rejects commercial exploitation as the guiding purpose of excavation and stresses preservation, documentation and public benefit. Critics of treasure salvage argue that selling selected artefacts encourages the recovery of valuable objects while neglecting ordinary material that may be historically more informative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark%3A/48223/pf0000181554" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesdoc.unesco.org">[UNESCO Digital Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesdoc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Cape Verde’s underwater mystery is therefore partly irreversible. Some ships remain undiscovered, and known wreck sites may still hold important evidence. But researchers cannot always determine what was removed in earlier operations, where every object was found or what unnoticed material was discarded. The missing “treasure” may be less significant than the missing archaeological record.

## The famine whose true scale is hard to recover

Cape Verde suffered repeated droughts and food crises from the sixteenth century into the twentieth. The disasters of the 1940s were especially severe, combining failed rains, malnutrition, disease, colonial neglect and outward migration. Historians agree that many thousands died, but exact totals vary because registration was uneven, rural deaths were easily missed and migration altered population figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://edoc.hu-berlin.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/3f1dc859-ad56-4375-97b7-33e005e2ed72/content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: edoc.hu-berlin.de">[hu-berlin.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">edoc.hu-berlin.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">edoc Managing the Prospect of Famine: Cape Verdean Officials,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">edoc Managing the Prospect of Famine: Cape Verdean Officials,</span></span></span>

The mystery is not whether famine occurred. It is how many people died, how preventable those deaths were and how responsibility should be divided between drought and colonial policy.

Environmental conditions created the immediate danger, but they do not explain the whole catastrophe. Research on the period describes warnings from medical and local officials, widespread malnutrition and the absence of an adequate emergency plan when the rains failed again in 1947. In the Praia district, roughly four-fifths of the population reportedly required urgent assistance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://edoc.hu-berlin.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/3f1dc859-ad56-4375-97b7-33e005e2ed72/content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: edoc.hu-berlin.de">[edoc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">edoc.hu-berlin.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">edoc Managing the Prospect of Famine: Cape Verdean Officials,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">edoc Managing the Prospect of Famine: Cape Verdean Officials,</span></span></span>

Colonial restrictions also limited the ways islanders could protect themselves. Historical research has argued that authorities and landowning interests discouraged or prohibited sections of the population from owning seaworthy fishing craft, partly because boats could be used by enslaved people, prisoners or soldiers to escape. The result was a striking contradiction: communities experienced starvation beside productive Atlantic fishing grounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/cabo-verde-gulag-of-the-south-atlantic-racism-fishing-prohibitions-and-famines1/E883F45C2DE0E6574DFC6F572C361CAA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Migration to plantations in São Tomé and Príncipe offered survival for some but raised another contested question: whether these departures were voluntary labour migration, coerced recruitment or a choice made meaningless by hunger. The answer varied between individuals, but the colonial government’s failure to provide sufficient relief narrowed the alternatives available to desperate families.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259424432_Managing_the_Prospect_of_Famine_Cape_Verdean_Officials_Subsistence_Emergencies_and_the_Change_of_Elite_Attitudes_During_Portugal%27s_Late_Colonial_Phase" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate Managing the Prospect of Famine: Cape Verdean Officials</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate Managing the Prospect of Famine: Cape Verdean Officials</span></span></span>

Later estimates sometimes present a single death toll for the 1940s famines, but such figures should be treated as approximations rather than precise counts. Missing civil registrations, inconsistent categories of death and the movement of survivors make exact calculation difficult. Deaths caused indirectly by weakened immunity and infectious disease may not have been recorded as famine deaths at all.

This uncertainty has political consequences. A low estimate can make the disaster seem like an unavoidable natural emergency; a higher one strengthens the case that it was a mass failure of colonial government. The surviving evidence supports a clear middle position: drought initiated the crisis, but policy, delayed relief and structural poverty substantially determined how deadly it became.

## The unresolved legacy of Cabo Verde's historical mysteries
Cape Verde’s unresolved history is shaped by the unequal survival of evidence. Colonial institutions documented taxation, appointments and trade more reliably than the lives of enslaved people, rural families or famine victims. Political organisations preserved heroic narratives more readily than internal conflicts. Treasure salvors valued coins and bullion more than broken domestic objects. Entire settlements became footnotes when their administrative purpose disappeared.

That pattern explains why the strongest Cape Verdean mysteries often concern missing context rather than missing events. We know that Cabral was murdered but not whether foreign manipulation was decisive. We know that Alcatrazes existed but are still reconstructing the community that lived there. We know that ships carried wealth into the archipelago’s reefs but cannot always recover the archaeological information lost during salvage. We know that famine killed on a large scale but cannot name or count every victim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve06/d66" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[state.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The assassination January 20 in Conakry, Guinea, of Amilcar Cabral, Secretary General of the African Party...Read more...</span></span></span>

Modern archaeology, declassified documents and heritage research have narrowed these uncertainties. They have not produced dramatic solutions to every case. Instead, they show where confidence is justified and where the record remains incomplete.

That distinction is essential. Cape Verde’s history is mysterious not because it requires hidden civilisations or elaborate conspiracies, but because islands at the centre of Atlantic empire were often recorded from the viewpoint of governments, merchants and outsiders. Recovering the rest means reading archives against their silences, protecting what remains under the sea and treating oral memory and material evidence as parts of the historical record rather than decorative additions to it.
