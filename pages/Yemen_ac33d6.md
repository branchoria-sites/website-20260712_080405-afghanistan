## Was the Queen of Sheba a ruler of ancient Yemen?

The most famous Yemeni enigma begins with an important distinction: the kingdom of Saba was historical, but the [Queen of Sheba]({{ 'queen-of-sheba/' | relative_url }}) has not been independently verified.

Saba developed around Marib in what is now Yemen and became one of ancient South Arabia’s major kingdoms. Its rulers controlled productive agricultural land and participated in the trade networks that carried incense and other valuable goods northwards. The archaeological landscape includes the ancient city of Marib, major temples, settlements and the monumental remains of the Marib dam. UNESCO describes the surviving sites as evidence of Saba’s architectural, technological and political achievements from the first millennium BC until the early Islamic period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLandmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib25 Jan 2023 — A serial property comprising seven archaeologica...</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that the celebrated queen appears in religious and literary traditions rather than in surviving Sabaean royal records. Thousands of inscriptions from ancient Yemen name kings, officials, gods, building works, wars and dedications, but none securely identifies the visitor to King Solomon described in the biblical account. A French government archaeological resource summarises the problem plainly: although Yemen is widely called the land of the Queen of Sheba, she is absent from the known archaeological and ancient inscriptional record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/en/yemen-land-queen-sheba" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[Archéologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ArchéologieYemen: the land of the Queen of Sheba &#124; ArchéologieAlthough Yemen is familiarly known as the Land of the Queen of Sheba, she i...</span></span></span>

This does not mean that the story was invented without any historical foundation. Long-distance contact between southern Arabia, the Red Sea and the eastern Mediterranean is entirely plausible. Saba was wealthy, organised and connected to international trade. Female rulers are also recorded elsewhere in ancient Arabia, although not securely among the known monarchs of Saba. What cannot presently be demonstrated is that a particular Sabaean queen travelled to Jerusalem during Solomon’s reign. Even the historical scale and chronology of Solomon’s kingdom remain subjects of archaeological debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/queen-sheba-legends-arise" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ebsco.com">[EBSCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ebsco.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen of Sheba Legends Arise &#124; History &#124; Research StartersNo clear geographical or archaeological proofs substantiate the origin and...</span></span></span>

The temples near Marib illustrate how later memory can attach itself to ancient ruins. The Awam and Baran temples were dedicated to a Sabaean deity, but in local tradition they became associated with the Queen of Sheba. One is often called her sanctuary and the other her throne. Archaeology confirms that these were major religious buildings; it has not confirmed that they belonged to the legendary queen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700/maps/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLandmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib - MapsGeographical data (7); 1700-001, Ancient City of Ma&#x27;ri...</span></span></span>

The competing Yemeni and Ethiopian traditions do not need to be reduced to a simple contest over ownership. Communities on both sides of the Red Sea were connected by migration, commerce, language and political influence. A story rooted in an ancient South Arabian trading kingdom could therefore be adopted and transformed in the Horn of Africa. The historical Sabaean world crossed modern national boundaries more easily than later retellings suggest.

The most responsible conclusion is therefore twofold. **Sheba was real; the queen remains unproven.** She may preserve the memory of an actual diplomatic mission, an unnamed female ruler, a later literary symbol of wisdom and wealth, or a combination of these. A securely dated inscription naming her would change the argument immediately. None has yet been found.

## What is hidden beneath the Sana’a Qur’an?

In 1972, restoration work at the Great Mosque of Sana’a exposed a concealed deposit containing tens of thousands of manuscript fragments. The material appears to have been stored rather than discarded because damaged religious writing was treated with respect. Among the discoveries was a palimpsest: parchment on which an earlier Qur’anic text had been erased and a second Qur’anic text written over it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/publications/aom/mom/01-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csmc.uni-hamburg.de">[csmc.uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csmc.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uni-hamburg.de.</span></span></span>

A palimpsest is not automatically evidence of secrecy or suppression. Parchment was expensive and was often reused in the manuscript cultures of Europe, North Africa and the Middle East. The mystery lies in the relationship between the two layers. Why was the lower writing erased? How early was it written? What do its differences reveal about the circulation of the Qur’an during the first generations of Islam?

The surviving Sana’a palimpsest consists of several dozen fragments. Its upper layer broadly belongs to the standard Qur’anic textual tradition. The lower layer contains changes in wording, spelling, verse order and arrangement that make it one of the most important surviving witnesses to the Qur’an’s earliest manuscript history. Scholars have stressed, however, that the differences are limited in scale and resemble types of variation reported in early Islamic accounts of individual written collections. They do not amount to an entirely different book.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/scepticism-as-method-in-the-study-of-quranic-origins-a-review-article-of-stephen-j-shoemaker-creating-the-quran-a-historicalcritical-study-berkeley-university-of-california-press-2022/118AF4B5308122CE4AEAAAF257E97680" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Dating the manuscript is more complicated than viral claims often imply. Radiocarbon tests date the animal skin from which the parchment was made, not the exact moment when a scribe wrote on it. Handwriting, spelling, page layout, ink and the physical construction of the manuscript must also be considered. These methods generally place the lower text in the seventh century, but they cannot produce a single undisputed year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://luminosoa.org/chapters/139/files/8fd91cfd-2504-4a86-a86e-54e995e6ba27.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: luminosoa.org">[luminosoa.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">luminosoa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Radiocarbon Dating and the Origins of the Qur&#x27;an</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radiocarbon Dating and the Origins of the Qur&#x27;an</span></span></span>

Several explanations for the erasure remain plausible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Ordinary reuse:** an old or damaged manuscript may have been washed so that valuable parchment could be used again.
* **Replacement after standardisation:** a copy whose wording or arrangement differed from the increasingly dominant written form may have been judged unsuitable for continued use.
* **Scribal or teaching material:** the lower layer may have been a working copy rather than a formal public codex.
* **Physical deterioration:** damage, incompleteness or poor execution may have mattered more than textual disagreement.

</div>

No surviving note explains the decision. The manuscript therefore cannot settle every debate about how the Qur’an reached its canonical written form. Its real importance is more precise: it provides physical evidence that early transmission involved scribes, corrections, variant readings and manuscript production, not merely an abstract text preserved outside history.

The Sana’a discovery also has a specifically Yemeni significance. The Great Mosque was one of the early Islamic world’s major religious sites, and the manuscript deposit shows that Yemen was not peripheral to the preservation of early Islamic writing. Modern controversy sometimes treats the fragments as though they appeared from nowhere to overturn all previous knowledge. In reality, they fit within a growing body of early Qur’anic manuscripts while retaining exceptional value because of the erased lower text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/publications/aom/mom/01-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csmc.uni-hamburg.de">[uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csmc.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uni-hamburg.de.</span></span></span>

## Did Yemeni observers see the supernova of 1006 first?

One of Yemen’s most intriguing archival puzzles concerns an object that briefly became brighter than anything else in the night sky.

The supernova now called SN 1006 was observed across Asia, the Middle East and Europe. For centuries, its earliest well-known records were dated to late April or the beginning of May 1006. Researchers examining later Yemeni historical works found reports attributed to an earlier chronicler that appear to place the first sighting around 17 April, roughly a fortnight earlier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06126" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The claim depends on two Yemeni texts written centuries after the event. One is associated with a fourteenth-century historian; another, later account appears to rely upon it or upon a shared source. The surviving passage describes the object’s appearance, timing, position and continuing visibility. Researchers converted its Islamic calendar date and compared the astronomical details with the sky as seen from Sana’a. They concluded that the description is internally consistent with a mid-April sighting. In particular, the reported time at which the object rose fits the earlier date better than the dates in some other surviving traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06126" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

Why is the question unresolved? The original eyewitness document has not survived. Historians are working with chronicles copied long after 1006, so an error could have entered through transmission, calendar conversion or later rewriting. Medieval historical works sometimes condensed earlier material without preserving its source exactly.

Yet dismissing the Yemeni report simply because it is late would also be unwise. Its details are unusually specific, and separate evidence suggests that the supernova may have been visible before the conventional late-April date. Researchers have noted possible chronological support from East Asian and European records, although those sources carry their own textual uncertainties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AN....338....8N/abstract" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ui.adsabs.harvard.edu">[Adsabs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ui.adsabs.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

The best assessment is that Yemen has a **credible claim**, not a proven priority. The account may preserve an observation made in or near Sana’a before the earliest surviving records elsewhere. It could also preserve an accurate description attached to an incorrect date. The case demonstrates how astronomical events can help test historical manuscripts: unlike most political or religious episodes, the night sky can be reconstructed mathematically.

It also reveals the importance of Yemeni chronicles beyond national history. A passage copied by a regional historian may change the global chronology of one of the brightest stellar explosions ever witnessed by human beings.

## Who ordered the killing of Ibrahim al-Hamdi?

On 11 October 1977, Ibrahim al-Hamdi, the president of North Yemen, was assassinated in Sana’a along with his brother. He was thirty-four. The killing was officially presented through conflicting and often implausible accounts, no transparent public investigation established responsibility, and the case remains one of modern Yemen’s defining political mysteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aljazeera.com/opinions/2020/3/26/yemenis-still-want-answers-about-ibrahim-al-hamdis-assassination" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aljazeera.com">[Al Jazeera]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aljazeera.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">yemenis still want answers about ibrahim al hamdis assassination</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">yemenis still want answers about ibrahim al hamdis assassination</span></span></span>

Al-Hamdi had taken power in 1974 and pursued central government reform, development programmes and limits on the influence of powerful tribal and military figures. He also sought a more independent regional position. His death occurred shortly before a planned visit to South Yemen, where discussions were expected to include closer relations and eventual unity between the two Yemeni states.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aljazeera.com/opinions/2020/3/26/yemenis-still-want-answers-about-ibrahim-al-hamdis-assassination" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aljazeera.com">[Al Jazeera]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aljazeera.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">yemenis still want answers about ibrahim al hamdis assassination</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">yemenis still want answers about ibrahim al hamdis assassination</span></span></span>

The basic facts beyond the deaths become contested. Accounts differ over the location and circumstances of the final meeting, the presence of women whose bodies were allegedly used to suggest a scandal, and the identities of those who organised the ambush. Later narratives have implicated senior North Yemeni officers, tribal leaders and Saudi officials in various combinations. Some accusations may be credible; others reflect later political struggles. The absence of a judicial process allowed testimony, propaganda and memory to merge.

Contemporary United States diplomatic and intelligence records show that suspicion quickly centred on Ahmad al-Ghashmi, who succeeded al-Hamdi. A US government assessment reported persistent rumours that al-Ghashmi was personally responsible and noted the damage those suspicions caused to public confidence. Another declassified analysis treated the murder primarily through its consequences for relations between North Yemen and Saudi Arabia, illustrating how foreign officials understood the killing as part of a wider regional power struggle. These documents record assessments and rumours, not courtroom proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v18/d233" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

Al-Ghashmi was himself killed in June 1978 by an explosive device carried by an envoy from South Yemen. His death removed the person most widely suspected of knowing what had happened to al-Hamdi. Ali Abdullah Saleh became president the following month and remained Yemen’s dominant ruler for decades, but no authoritative investigation followed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aljazeera.com/opinions/2020/3/26/yemenis-still-want-answers-about-ibrahim-al-hamdis-assassination" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aljazeera.com">[Al Jazeera]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aljazeera.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">yemenis still want answers about ibrahim al hamdis assassination</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">yemenis still want answers about ibrahim al hamdis assassination</span></span></span>

Three broad explanations continue to compete:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **An internal military plot:** officers threatened by al-Hamdi’s reforms and concentration of authority arranged his removal.
* **A tribal-political coalition:** influential domestic networks acted to prevent further erosion of their power.
* **A plot with external support:** Saudi Arabia, worried about al-Hamdi’s independence and contacts with South Yemen, encouraged or assisted Yemeni conspirators.

</div>

These explanations are not mutually exclusive. A domestic assassination could have received regional approval without being directly commanded from abroad. That possibility helps explain why the case is difficult to reduce to a single culprit.

Al-Hamdi’s death matters because it became a dividing line in Yemeni political memory. Admirers remember him as a rare modernising leader whose programme was cut short; critics warn that some of this reputation has been idealised by later disappointment. Either way, the failure to investigate reinforced a culture in which political killings could be absorbed into a new balance of power rather than examined in public. The mystery is therefore not just who pulled the trigger. It is why successive authorities found uncertainty more useful than resolution.

## Where have Yemen’s missing antiquities gone?

Not every historical mystery begins in the distant past. Some are being created now, as objects lose their archaeological context and disappear into private collections.

Yemen’s war has damaged museums, monuments and excavation sites while weakening the institutions responsible for guarding them. UNESCO reported that around one hundred objects looted from Yemen after 2011 had appeared in European and American auction markets, with an estimated combined sale value of about one million US dollars. The International Council of Museums has issued an emergency list illustrating categories of Yemeni material at risk, including inscriptions, statues, manuscripts, architectural pieces and coins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/cultural-heritage-objects-stake-armed-conflicts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cultural heritage objects stake armed conflicts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cultural heritage objects stake armed conflicts</span></span></span>

The known auction cases represent only the visible part of the trade. An artefact dug up without documentation may pass through several dealers, acquire an invented ownership history and surface years later with no reliable record of where it came from. This creates two linked mysteries: whether the item was legally removed, and what historical information was destroyed when it was separated from its original setting.

A carved inscription, for example, may still be translated in a museum or private collection. But without knowing its exact location, associated buildings and surrounding objects, researchers may be unable to determine who commissioned it, when it was installed or what political event it records. Looting therefore removes evidence even when the object itself survives.

The problem also complicates attempts to recover Yemen’s heritage. Lists of stolen museum pieces are incomplete; many archaeological sites were never fully inventoried; and objects may have left the country before modern export laws or during earlier periods of colonial collecting. Each claim requires evidence connecting a particular object to a documented site, collection or unlawful removal.

Current research indicates that trafficking intensified after the civil war escalated in 2015, but the organisation of the trade remains poorly understood. The available evidence points to networks involving local excavation, cross-border transport, intermediaries and international buyers rather than a single centrally controlled operation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/strengthening-capacities-fight-illicit-trafficking-cultural-property-djibouti-and-yemen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

This is a historical mystery with an urgent deadline. The longer an unrecorded object circulates, the easier it becomes to manufacture a respectable-looking ownership history and the harder it is to return the piece or reconstruct its origins.

## Why Yemen produces so many unresolved histories

Yemen’s mysteries share several causes. Ancient inscriptions preserve official achievements rather than private biography. Manuscripts survive through chance, reuse and selective copying. Modern political archives remain restricted or were never securely maintained. War has limited excavation, damaged collections and displaced specialists. Foreign researchers have often had better access to Yemeni material held overseas than Yemeni scholars have had to sites and archives inside the country.

Public memory then fills the gaps. Temples become the palace of a legendary queen. An assassinated president becomes either a lost national saviour or a symbol used by later factions. A manuscript variation becomes, depending on the narrator, either meaningless or revolutionary. The historian’s task is to resist both extremes.

The strongest conclusions are usually narrower than the legends:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Ancient Saba was a powerful Yemeni kingdom, but no contemporary evidence identifies its famous queen.
* The Sana’a palimpsest is an exceptionally early Qur’anic manuscript, but it does not support claims of a completely different lost scripture.
* The Yemeni SN 1006 report may record the earliest known observation, but it survives only in later chronicles.
* Ibrahim al-Hamdi was murdered amid serious domestic and regional tensions, but surviving suspicions have not been tested through a credible legal investigation.
* Yemeni antiquities are appearing outside the country, but incomplete inventories make the full scale and routes of loss difficult to reconstruct.

</div>

These cases endure because they concern more than unanswered questions. They shape competing ideas of Yemen itself: heir to an ancient trading civilisation, guardian of early religious texts, contributor to world science, victim of political impunity and custodian of a heritage increasingly scattered beyond its borders. The surviving evidence is substantial enough to reject fantasy, yet incomplete enough to keep the central questions open.
