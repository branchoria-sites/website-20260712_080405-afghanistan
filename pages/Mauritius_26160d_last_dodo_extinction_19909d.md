## Introduction

The dodo's extinction is certain. The mystery is not **whether** it disappeared from [Mauritius]({{ 'mauritius/' | relative_url }}), but **exactly when the last surviving birds died out**. For centuries, the extinction date was commonly given as 1662, based on what was long regarded as the last reliable eyewitness account. However, later discoveries in Dutch colonial records, together with modern statistical analysis of historical sightings, have reopened the debate. Some researchers argue that small populations probably survived for several decades longer, while others believe that many supposed later reports actually described a different Mauritian bird altogether.

The uncertainty matters because it reveals how fragmentary the historical record of Mauritius was during the seventeenth century. The dodo vanished during the island's early Dutch settlement, when wildlife was poorly documented and colonial records were concerned far more with trade and administration than conservation.

## The last widely accepted sighting

For much of the twentieth century, historians accepted a single event as the dodo's final confirmed appearance.

In 1662, Dutch shipwreck survivor **Volkert Evertsz** reported seeing dodos on a small offshore islet near Mauritius after the wreck of the *Arnhem*. His account is widely regarded as the last unequivocal eyewitness description of living dodos because it contains details consistent with earlier observations and comes from a contemporary source rather than later recollection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/426245a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When did the dodo become extinct? &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When did the dodo become extinct? &#124; NatureNovember 20, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 20, 2003</span></span></span>

This date became deeply embedded in books, museums and popular culture. Yet even at the time, there was reason to suspect that the story was incomplete. The previous reliable observation had occurred more than two decades earlier, suggesting that the species had already become scarce long before 1662. Such large gaps between records make it difficult to use the final sighting as the actual date of extinction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/426245a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When did the dodo become extinct? &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When did the dodo become extinct? &#124; NatureNovember 20, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 20, 2003</span></span></span>

Modern ecologists point out that extinctions are almost never witnessed directly. A rare animal may survive for years in isolated pockets without being recorded, especially on a sparsely explored island.

## Why later records may describe another bird

The strongest challenge to the 1662 date emerged after researchers examined the hunting journals of Dutch colonial official **Isaac Johannes Lamotius**, who governed Mauritius during the 1680s.

His records contain several references to birds called "dodaars" or similar names between 1685 and 1688. At first glance, these entries appeared to prove that dodos survived for at least another quarter-century after Evertsz's report. Some statistical studies incorporating these records estimated that extinction probably occurred around 1690–1693 rather than 1662.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nature02688" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dutch diaries and the demise of the dodo &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dutch diaries and the demise of the dodo &#124; NatureJune 10, 2004...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2004</span></span></span>

However, the interpretation remains disputed.

Many ornithologists now argue that the Dutch name formerly applied to the dodo had gradually shifted to another extinct Mauritian species, the **red rail** (sometimes called the red hen). Language changes of this kind were common among colonial settlers, especially when one familiar animal became increasingly rare. If hunters were actually recording red rails rather than dodos, the apparent late sightings disappear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1474-919X.2006.00478.x/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryEstablishing extinction dates – the curious case of the Dodo Raphus cucullatus and the Red Hen Aphanapteryx bonasia...</span></span></span>

Several points support this interpretation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The descriptions attached to later hunting records are often too brief to identify the bird securely.
* Contemporary Dutch settlers increasingly used overlapping names for different flightless birds.
* The red rail remained more widespread during the late seventeenth century and would have been encountered more frequently by hunters.
* No detailed late eyewitness description clearly matching a dodo survives after the early 1660s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1474-919X.2006.00478.x/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryEstablishing extinction dates – the curious case of the Dodo Raphus cucullatus and the Red Hen Aphanapteryx bonasia...</span></span></span>

</div>

A further disputed report comes from an escaped slave who allegedly saw dodos in 1674. Because the evidence survives only indirectly and lacks detailed description, historians generally treat it as possible but unconfirmed rather than definitive proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/426245a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When did the dodo become extinct? &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When did the dodo become extinct? &#124; NatureNovember 20, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 20, 2003</span></span></span>

The result is an unusual historical puzzle: later documentary references exist, but their meaning depends largely on whether colonial bird names remained consistent.

## What caused the dodo's extinction?

Although the precise year remains uncertain, the causes of extinction are much less controversial.

Early European sailors certainly hunted dodos for food, although contemporary accounts suggest the meat was not especially prized. More damaging was the transformation of Mauritius following permanent Dutch settlement from 1638 onwards. Forest clearance reduced habitat, while introduced animals—including pigs, rats, cats, dogs and monkeys—raided nests and ate eggs and chicks. Because dodos nested on the ground and laid only a single egg at a time, they were particularly vulnerable to these new predators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/the-dodo-bird-the-real-facts-about-this-icon-of-extinction.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhm.ac.uk">[Natural History Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhm.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nhm.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The extinction was therefore probably driven by several pressures acting together rather than by hunting alone:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Habitat loss through forest clearance.
* Predation by introduced mammals.
* Hunting by visiting sailors and settlers.
* Slow reproduction, making population recovery difficult.
* Small, isolated populations becoming increasingly vulnerable as numbers declined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/the-dodo-bird-the-real-facts-about-this-icon-of-extinction.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhm.ac.uk">[Natural History Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhm.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nhm.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

</div>

This combination explains why the dodo disappeared within less than a century of sustained European settlement, even if the exact final year remains uncertain.

## Why historians still disagree

The debate survives because the available evidence is unusually thin.

Unlike many modern extinctions, no scientist studied living dodos in their natural environment. Most surviving information comes from sailors' journals, colonial correspondence and scattered administrative records. There was no organised wildlife survey, no biological monitoring and no recognition that the species was approaching extinction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/the-dodo-bird-the-real-facts-about-this-icon-of-extinction.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhm.ac.uk">[Natural History Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhm.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nhm.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Modern researchers therefore rely on three different kinds of evidence:

* **Eyewitness reports**, which may be accurate but are often separated by long intervals.
* **Colonial administrative records**, whose bird names are not always consistent.
* **Statistical extinction models**, which estimate when a species probably vanished based on the pattern of known sightings rather than proving an exact date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/426245a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When did the dodo become extinct? &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When did the dodo become extinct? &#124; NatureNovember 20, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 20, 2003</span></span></span>

These approaches do not necessarily contradict one another. It is entirely possible that the last indisputable sighting occurred in 1662 while a few isolated birds survived into the 1680s without leaving unmistakable records.

## Last Dodo in the wider historical record
The dodo has become the world's best-known symbol of extinction, yet one of the most basic questions about its disappearance remains unanswered. The uncertainty illustrates how easily an animal can vanish before anyone realises that careful documentation is needed.

For Mauritius, the mystery also highlights the limitations of colonial archives. The records preserve valuable fragments of information but leave crucial gaps about the island's natural world. Rather than pointing to a hidden conspiracy or a lost surviving population, the debate shows how history is reconstructed from incomplete evidence.

Today, most scholars agree on three broad conclusions: the dodo became rare well before 1662, the last indisputable sighting dates from that year, and the species may have survived for a short time afterwards, although later documentary references remain open to competing interpretations. The exact moment when the final dodo died is therefore still one of Mauritius's most enduring—and scientifically grounded—historical uncertainties.
