## Introduction

Three questions therefore dominate Comoros’s enigmatic history. Was deposed president [Ali Soilih]({{ 'ali-soilih/' | relative_url }}) murdered in 1978 rather than killed while escaping? Who ordered the shooting of President Ahmed Abdallah in 1989? And how should the islands’ mixed African, Asian and Middle Eastern heritage be reconciled with stories of Persian founders? In each case, the broad setting is clear, but decisive proof remains elusive.

## Why Comoros produced so many unanswered questions

Comoros became independent from France in 1975, but its early national history was repeatedly disrupted by coups, attempted coups and foreign mercenaries. French soldier of fortune Bob Denard took part in four interventions in the archipelago and exercised enormous influence through the presidential guard during Ahmed Abdallah’s restored government. This instability damaged the normal machinery by which historical certainty is created. Governments changed suddenly; witnesses were politically exposed; security forces were controlled by rival factions; and investigations crossed several jurisdictions. Events that occurred in Moroni might later be examined in Paris, while important intelligence or diplomatic records remained inaccessible.

That does not mean every rumour about Comorian history is credible. The best approach is to separate three levels of uncertainty:

* **Established events:** coups occurred, named leaders were detained or killed, and particular armed groups controlled key institutions.
* **Reasonable but unproved interpretations:** a death may have been an execution disguised as an escape attempt, or the result of a struggle within the presidential guard.
* **Speculation:** claims that depend on unnamed intelligence services, secret international plans or alleged orders for which no documentary trail has emerged.

</div>

The two presidential deaths illustrate why these distinctions matter.

## Was Ali Soilih executed after the 1978 coup?

Ali Soilih governed Comoros from 1976 until mercenaries led by Bob Denard overthrew him on 13 May 1978. The operation restored the political camp of former president Ahmed Abdallah, who had himself been removed shortly after independence. Soilih was captured and held under guard. On 29 May, only sixteen days after the coup, the new authorities announced that he had been shot while attempting to escape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1978_Comorian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1978 Comorian coup d&#x27;état</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1978 Comorian coup d&#x27;état</span></span></span>

The official account was questioned almost immediately. A contemporary report in *The Washington Post* recorded the authorities’ claim that Soilih had been killed when supporters tried to release him, but also noted observations from Madagascar that an escape attempt would have been uncharacteristic of him. Later reference works continued to describe his death as mysterious or merely “reputed” to have occurred during an attempted escape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/national/1978/05/30/comoro-politician-killed/53ca1d57-51cd-4518-b641-8e99f8afb0bb/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostComoro Politician Killed29 May 1978 — The rule Politico-Military Executive said Soilih, 42, was shot when a group of s...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1978</span></span></span>

Several circumstances make the official story difficult to accept without reservation. Soilih was a deposed head of state whose survival posed an obvious political risk to the restored regime. He was in the custody of forces created by a mercenary takeover, rather than a stable judicial system. His death also removed the person best placed to organise resistance or challenge the legitimacy of the new authorities.

Yet suspicion is not the same as proof. Publicly accessible accounts disagree over the precise circumstances, including whether Soilih was killed during gunfire, deliberately shot or assaulted before his death. No transparent contemporary inquiry produced forensic findings, reliable witness testimony or a detailed reconstruction that could settle the matter.

The most plausible interpretation is that Soilih’s death was at least politically convenient and probably involved more than a spontaneous escape attempt. Describing it confidently as an ordered assassination, however, goes beyond the surviving public evidence. What can be said securely is that the official explanation was never independently demonstrated and has failed to persuade many later historians.

### Why the uncertainty endured

The coup government controlled both Soilih and the first public account of his death. There was no strong independent judiciary capable of examining the actions of the new rulers, while foreign journalists had limited access to the islands. The conflicting versions were therefore never tested against a complete evidential record.

Soilih’s later reputation further complicated the story. His revolutionary government had pursued radical social policies and was accused of repression, making him a divisive figure rather than a universally mourned victim. Political arguments over whether his rule was progressive, destructive or both sometimes overshadow the narrower historical question of how he died. Whatever judgement is made about his presidency, the circumstances of his death remain inadequately documented.

## Who killed President Ahmed Abdallah in 1989?

The death of Ahmed Abdallah is Comoros’s most substantial unresolved political mystery. After returning to power in 1978, Abdallah governed alongside a presidential guard commanded by Bob Denard. The guard became a powerful institution, while Denard accumulated political influence and commercial interests. By the late 1980s, the relationship between president, regular armed forces and mercenary-controlled security apparatus was increasingly unstable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Denard" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bob Denard</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bob Denard</span></span></span>

On the night of 26–27 November 1989, Abdallah was shot dead at the presidential residence in Moroni. Denard was present and was wounded. What followed was effectively another seizure of power by the presidential guard, although French pressure and military intervention forced Denard to leave Comoros shortly afterwards. At the time, Denard characterised the president’s death as an accident amid confusion inside the palace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1989/12/14/france-sends-force-to-oust-mercenary/ef41cc6d-b43e-430e-a987-46047d4379cd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on washingtonpost.com.</span></span></span>

The central theories are not equally well supported, but none has been decisively proved.

**A planned killing by Denard or his associates.** French prosecutors later argued that Abdallah was killed because he intended to reduce Denard’s power or dismantle the presidential guard. This explanation supplies a clear motive and fits the broader tension within the regime. Reporting before the 1999 trial stated that the prosecution alleged Abdallah had been shot during a staged attack after an argument in his office.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/1999/may/05/paulwebster" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Mercenary tried for Comoran killing &#124; World news</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Mercenary tried for Comoran killing &#124; World news</span></span></span>

**A shooting by Comorian soldiers or guards during an internal confrontation.** Denard maintained that Abdallah had been killed by his own bodyguards. The palace contained rival armed personnel, and the country’s security structure was fragmented enough for an exchange of fire or internal mutiny to be possible. Reuters reported that Denard continued to advance this version until his death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/article/economy/factbox-french-mercenary-bob-denard-dies-idUSL14177134/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FACTBOX-French mercenary Bob Denard dies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FACTBOX-French mercenary Bob Denard diesOctober 14, 2007 — 14 Oct 2007 — In 1999, Denard -- who always maintained the president ha...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 14, 2007</span></span></span>

**A wider political operation involving outside interests.** Because Denard repeatedly claimed covert French support during his career, Abdallah’s death is sometimes folded into broader theories about French intelligence, South Africa and Cold War influence in the Indian Ocean. There is evidence that Denard operated within murky international networks and that French officials possessed significant knowledge of some later mercenary activity. A United Nations report noted that he remained under investigation for Abdallah’s assassination when he was able to organise his 1995 coup attempt; a later French case concerning that coup raised further questions about contacts with French services. Neither finding proves that France ordered or helped arrange Abdallah’s death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/228322/files/E_CN.4_1996_27-EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library System Economic and Social Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library System Economic and Social Council</span></span></span>

### Why the Paris trial settled so little

Denard and his associate Dominique Malacrino were tried in France in 1999. The proceeding might have resolved the central accusation, but both men were acquitted because the court found the evidence insufficient. Abdallah’s family had withdrawn its civil action shortly before the trial, and the prosecution’s case was largely circumstantial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/1999/may/05/paulwebster" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Mercenary tried for Comoran killing &#124; World news</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Mercenary tried for Comoran killing &#124; World news</span></span></span>

An acquittal establishes that guilt was not proved to the required legal standard. It does not demonstrate that the shooting was accidental, identify another killer or validate Denard’s account. The case therefore ended with a legal answer—insufficient evidence—but no satisfying historical explanation.

The safest conclusion is that Abdallah died during a power struggle involving the institutions around him. Denard and the presidential guard had both motive and opportunity, but the public record does not establish who fired the fatal shots, whether the killing was prearranged, or whether the outcome emerged from a confrontation that went wrong. Claims naming a definite mastermind should therefore be treated cautiously.

## Who first settled the Comoro Islands?

The oldest Comorian mystery is less violent but equally important: where did the islands’ first communities come from?

For generations, local histories and elite genealogies connected rulers and important families with migrants from Persia, especially the city of Shiraz. Similar “Shirazi” foundation traditions appear throughout the Swahili coast. They commonly describe foreign Muslim founders arriving by sea, establishing towns or dynasties and marrying into local families. Historians have long debated whether these accounts preserve memories of actual migration, express political status, or combine both.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/oral-historiography-and-the-shirazi-of-the-east-african-coast/E7961C092A5BB79E1DDB7BEFB919EB20" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Archaeology and genetics now show that the islands’ population history was far more complex than a single Persian migration.

Research on modern Comorian genetic diversity found ancestry connected with sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East and Island Southeast Asia. The islands appear to have functioned as a meeting point within western Indian Ocean navigation rather than as an isolated colony founded by one people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/ejhg2010128" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

Archaeological and archaeobotanical evidence also indicates connections stretching in several directions. Early sites have produced Asian crops, imported ceramics and evidence of trade with Madagascar, the East African coast and lands farther east. The settlement process therefore probably involved repeated arrivals, intermarriage and commercial movement rather than one founding fleet. The exact dates, proportions and routes remain debated because the archaeological record is uneven and many sites have not been investigated as extensively as major centres on the East African mainland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shs.hal.science/halshs-00706172/document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shs.hal.science">[SHS Hal Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shs.hal.science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hal.science.</span></span></span>

### Dembeni and the unexplained wealth of early Comoros

The archaeological site of Dembeni on Mayotte gives the mystery a concrete focus. Between roughly the ninth and twelfth centuries, the settlement participated in extensive Indian Ocean trade. Excavations have uncovered imported Chinese and Persian ceramics and large quantities of rock crystal associated with Madagascar. The scale and richness of the material suggest that the Comoro Islands occupied a more important place in early maritime commerce than their small size might imply.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344292325_Islamic_Archaeology_in_the_Comoros_The_Swahili_and_the_Rock_Crystal_Trade_with_the_Abbasid_and_Fatimid_Caliphates" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

What is still uncertain is precisely how that commerce was organised. Dembeni may have acted as an intermediary between Madagascar, the Swahili coast and merchants connected to the Persian Gulf or Red Sea. Its inhabitants could have been local navigators, immigrant trading communities or a mixed population. Imported goods prove contact, but they do not by themselves reveal the identities or languages of the people handling them.

Evidence from an early mosque excavated in Comoros likewise complicates traditional stories about who introduced Islam. Archaeological work has challenged attempts to attribute conversion to a single remembered missionary or founder. Islamisation appears more likely to have been a gradual process connected with trade, settlement and changing local authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.udsm.ac.tz/index.php/sap/article/view/88/80" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.udsm.ac.tz">[University of Dar es Salaam Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.udsm.ac.tz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on udsm.ac.tz.</span></span></span>

## Were the Shirazi founders real?

The answer depends on what “real” means. Trade and migration between the Persian Gulf and the East African world certainly occurred. Genetic research also supports some Middle Eastern contribution to Comorian ancestry. It would therefore be unreasonable to claim that Persian or Arabian migrants never reached the islands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/ejhg2010128" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

The doubtful element is the literal dynastic narrative: a clearly identified Persian prince or family arriving at a particular moment and founding the society from above. Such traditions often served later political purposes. Claiming descent from prestigious overseas Muslims could strengthen a ruler’s legitimacy, distinguish an elite lineage and connect a town to the wider Islamic world. Comparable arguments have shaped the broader scholarly debate over Shirazi identity on the Swahili coast.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/oral-historiography-and-the-shirazi-of-the-east-african-coast/E7961C092A5BB79E1DDB7BEFB919EB20" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

The best-supported interpretation is therefore neither “the legends are entirely factual” nor “the legends are fabricated”. They probably preserve a cultural memory of real maritime connections while compressing centuries of migration and intermarriage into memorable stories about founders. Archaeology demonstrates cosmopolitan contact; genetics demonstrates mixed ancestry; oral tradition explains how later communities gave that mixture a meaningful political genealogy.

What remains missing is the evidence needed to connect a particular legendary founder to a securely dated settlement, grave, inscription or documentary record. Until such material appears, Shirazi stories should be treated as historically valuable traditions rather than literal passenger lists.

## Fact, folklore and modern mythmaking

Comoros’s genuine mysteries are vulnerable to exaggeration because the documented history is already unusually dramatic. A European mercenary repeatedly returned to a small island state, presidents were deposed and killed, and foreign governments intervened without always clarifying what they knew. Little invention is needed to make the story remarkable.

Several common distortions should nevertheless be avoided.

First, Bob Denard did not single-handedly control every event in post-independence Comoros. He depended on Comorian political allies, local soldiers, financiers and changing international circumstances. Treating the country as merely the stage for one mercenary’s adventures removes Comorian agency and obscures internal political struggles.

Second, Denard’s contacts with French officials do not prove that every coup or killing was ordered by Paris. Later court testimony and reporting support the view that mercenary activity sometimes overlapped with unofficial or deniable state interests. That is different from possessing documentary proof of a government command in a specific assassination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/article/world/french-mercenary-bob-denard-dies-idUSL14497575/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">french mercenary bob denard dies idUSL14497575</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">French mercenary Bob Denard dies14 Oct 2007 — Denard, whose death was confirmed by his sister Georgette Garnier, became legendary...</span></span></span>

Third, imported objects do not automatically identify colonists. A Persian pot found at an island settlement may have arrived through several merchants and ports. Archaeologists combine such finds with settlement patterns, food remains, burials, architecture, language and genetic evidence before drawing conclusions about migration.

Finally, oral traditions are not worthless simply because they are not literal chronicles. They can preserve memories of social change, distant connections and claims to authority. Their value lies in being read alongside physical and documentary evidence, rather than accepted unquestioningly or dismissed as fantasy.

## What would resolve the mysteries?

The political deaths would require evidence more direct than the material now publicly available: contemporary autopsy findings, complete ballistic records, authenticated orders, reliable eyewitness testimony or newly opened diplomatic and intelligence files. Because many principal participants have died, archival disclosure is increasingly more likely to change the historical picture than a new criminal investigation.

The settlement debate is more open to scientific progress. Further excavation, improved dating, ancient DNA, isotopic analysis of human remains and closer comparison with Madagascar and East African coastal sites could reveal when particular groups arrived and how mobile early island communities were. Even then, the result will probably remain a history of mixture rather than the discovery of one original Comorian people.

These cases matter because they concern more than unanswered details. Soilih’s death shaped the memory of revolution and counter-revolution. Abdallah’s killing symbolises the vulnerability of the early republic to armed patronage and outside interference. The debate over Shirazi ancestry concerns how Comorians understand their place between Africa, Madagascar, the Arab world and maritime Asia.

The enduring lesson is that Comoros’s past is not mysterious because nothing is known. It is mysterious because several different kinds of evidence point towards a complicated history while stopping just short of a definitive answer.
