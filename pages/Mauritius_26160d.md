## Introduction

These cases are not equally mysterious. Some contain a genuine gap in the evidence; others are better understood as disputes over how incomplete evidence became public memory. The strongest conclusion is that Mauritius’s unresolved history often lies at the meeting point of archives and oral tradition. Colonial officials recorded ships, property and punishment more carefully than the experiences of enslaved people, marginal communities or disappearing wildlife. Later generations filled those silences with stories—sometimes responsibly, sometimes romantically, and sometimes for tourism or entertainment.

## The pirate treasure that may begin with a modern invention

The legend of Olivier Levasseur, the French pirate known as [La Buse]({{ 'la-buse/' | relative_url }}), is the best-known treasure mystery associated with the western Indian Ocean. Mauritius is frequently drawn into the search because pirates used the Mascarene islands and Madagascar’s coast, while supposed clues have been linked to sites across the region. Yet the historical core of the story is much narrower than the modern legend.

Levasseur was a real pirate, and contemporary accounts support his involvement with John Taylor in the capture of the Portuguese vessel *Nossa Senhora do Cabo* in 1721. The ship carried senior passengers, valuable cargo and enslaved people. Its seizure was an exceptionally rich act of piracy, although spectacular modern estimates of the loot should be treated cautiously. The popular tale adds that Levasseur concealed his share, then threw a coded message into the crowd before his execution in 1730, challenging onlookers to find his treasure.

That dramatic gallows scene is not supported by a known contemporary account. The familiar cryptogram first appeared in a 1934 book by French historian and librarian Charles de La Roncière. Research comparing the legend with earlier records found no independent manuscript of the cipher and no earlier evidence that Levasseur threw such a document at his execution. The study concludes that La Roncière probably combined real piracy with literary invention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dspace.ut.ee/bitstreams/d9f57dc9-f585-4550-b2a3-6ac8534b1557/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dspace.ut.ee">[Dspace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dspace.ut.ee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ut.ee.</span></span></span>

The same problem affects the supposed “Fiery Cross of Goa”, often described as a gigantic jewel-covered cross requiring several men to carry it. Contemporary material supports the existence of valuable religious objects and mentions a small golden insignia belonging to the former Viceroy of Goa, but not the enormous object celebrated in modern treasure accounts. The grand cross appears to be a later addition to the mythology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dspace.ut.ee/bitstreams/d9f57dc9-f585-4550-b2a3-6ac8534b1557/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dspace.ut.ee">[Dspace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dspace.ut.ee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ut.ee.</span></span></span>

The fairest assessment separates three layers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Documented fact:** Levasseur existed, participated in major Indian Ocean piracy and shared in valuable captured cargo.
* **Plausible but unproved possibility:** some portion of pirate wealth may have been hidden, lost or dispersed without a surviving record.
* **Weakly supported legend:** the gallows challenge, the famous cryptogram and the colossal cross.

</div>

This does not make the story unimportant. Its real mystery is how a twentieth-century literary puzzle became widely treated as an eighteenth-century artefact. Treasure hunters have repeatedly approached landscapes in Mauritius and neighbouring islands as if every carving, stone alignment or cave were part of a master code. Once a theory starts from an unauthenticated cipher, however, almost any feature can be made to look significant. The absence of treasure is then explained not as evidence against the theory but as proof that the code has not yet been solved.

La Buse therefore belongs in Mauritian mystery history less as a credible map to buried riches than as a warning about circular reasoning. The raid is history; the treasure trail is largely cultural memory.

## What really happened at Le Morne?

[Le Morne]({{ 'le-morne/' | relative_url }}) Brabant, the steep peninsula in south-west Mauritius, is one of the country’s most important landscapes of slavery and resistance. UNESCO recognises it as an exceptional testimony to maroonage: the flight of enslaved people from plantations and their efforts to survive beyond direct colonial control. Archaeological evidence, the mountain’s terrain and oral traditions all support its use as a refuge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1259/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLe Morne Cultural LandscapeLe Morne Cultural Landscape is an exceptional testimony to maroonage or resistance...</span></span></span>

The disputed element is the story commonly attached to the abolition of slavery in 1835. According to the best-known version, officials climbed the mountain to tell the maroons that they were free. Mistaking the approaching party for soldiers sent to recapture them, some or all of the fugitives threw themselves from the cliffs.

The story is powerful because it compresses the cruelty of slavery into one terrible misunderstanding: people were so conditioned to fear capture that they could not recognise freedom when it arrived. It has become part of commemorations, school narratives and public explanations of the site. Yet historians have struggled to identify contemporary documentary evidence confirming a mass suicide at Le Morne on or around the date of abolition.

That absence does not prove that no deaths occurred. Colonial archives routinely reduced enslaved people to entries concerning ownership, punishment, escape or labour. A remote incident involving maroons could have gone unrecorded, been recorded in a lost file, or survived principally through oral testimony. But the scale and precise circumstances found in later retellings remain uncertain.

The well-established facts are substantial without the dramatic ending. Enslaved people did escape in Mauritius. Maroons used difficult terrain to resist recapture. Le Morne’s cliffs, caves and relative isolation made it a credible refuge, and its symbolic importance is grounded in that broader history. UNESCO’s case for the landscape does not depend solely upon proving one mass-suicide event; it rests on the site’s physical and cultural testimony to resistance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1259/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLe Morne Cultural LandscapeLe Morne Cultural Landscape is an exceptional testimony to maroonage or resistance...</span></span></span>

Three interpretations remain possible:

**A real event enlarged through retelling.** A small number of deaths, or an encounter between maroons and officials, may have become a collective tragedy in oral memory.

**A symbolic narrative rooted in historical truth.** The exact incident may not have occurred as told, but the story conveys an authentic reality: escapees faced recapture, punishment and profound distrust of colonial authority.

**A later national legend.** The abolition-day episode may have emerged after slavery as a memorable explanation of the mountain’s meaning.

These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. Oral tradition can preserve history while reshaping chronology, scale and motive. The responsible position is therefore neither to dismiss the story as “only a myth” nor present every detail as settled fact. Le Morne matters because resistance there is well supported; the famous final plunge remains an unresolved part of its remembrance.

## When did the last dodo die?

The dodo is Mauritius’s most recognisable historical emblem, but surprisingly little is known about the final living population. Dutch sailors recorded the bird after arriving in 1598, yet no trained naturalist carried out a modern field study before it disappeared. What survives consists of brief travel accounts, drawings of uneven reliability, scattered remains and later fossil discoveries.

The commonly accepted last credible sighting dates to 1662, when shipwrecked sailors reported large flightless birds on an islet near Mauritius. Later records, especially hunting notes from the 1680s, have been interpreted as evidence that dodos survived for another generation. A statistical study using the sequence of claimed observations proposed that extinction may have occurred around the end of the seventeenth century rather than in 1662. Other researchers argue that the later reports actually describe the Mauritius red rail, another flightless bird whose names may have been confused with those used for the dodo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The dispute cannot be settled merely by choosing the latest date in a list. Early observers used inconsistent names and rarely supplied descriptions detailed enough to distinguish species. A record saying that a “dodo” was caught is valuable only if the writer meant the animal now known scientifically by that name. This linguistic uncertainty is why the official extinction date and the probable date of the last individual need not be identical.

The cause of extinction is clearer than the exact year. Direct hunting contributed, but habitat disruption and introduced animals—particularly pigs and other species capable of damaging nests or altering the island ecosystem—were probably more decisive. The dodo had evolved without mammalian predators and could not rapidly adapt to the ecological changes accompanying settlement.

Mare aux Songes, a fossil-rich former wetland in south-east Mauritius, has added another dimension to the mystery. Nineteenth-century excavations recovered large numbers of dodo bones but recorded their position poorly. Modern work beginning in 2005 found a roughly 4,200-year-old bone deposit containing dodos, tortoises and other native animals. Researchers concluded that the site preserves evidence of a prehistoric ecological crisis, probably associated with severe drought, long before human settlement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dare.uva.nl/personal/pure/en/publications/dodo-remains-from-an-in-situ-context-from-mare-aux-songes-mauritius%289586e982-c8d9-4f9d-b874-9dc6b4861796%29.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dare.uva.nl">[uva.nl]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dare.uva.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dodo remains from an in situ context from Mare aux...by HJM Meijer · 2012 · Cited by 17 — Since 2005, excavations at Mare aux Songes, Ma...</span></span></span>

This discovery corrected a tempting misconception. A concentration of dead dodos is not evidence that sailors slaughtered a final flock at the marsh. The deposit is thousands of years older than Dutch colonisation. It instead shows that dodos survived major natural stresses before human arrival, strengthening the case that their eventual extinction resulted from a rapid combination of human pressures.

The remaining mystery is therefore not whether people transformed the conditions in which dodos lived, but how long a small, increasingly isolated population endured before the final bird died unnoticed. There may never be a dated eyewitness account of that moment.

## The death of Kaya and the limits of official explanation

On 21 February 1999, Mauritian musician Joseph Réginald Topize, known as Kaya, died while held by police in Port Louis. He had been arrested after publicly smoking cannabis at an event advocating reform of the law. His death triggered several days of protest and rioting, exposing deep frustration over policing, poverty and the position of the Creole community.

The immediate dispute concerned his injuries. Police accounts reportedly attributed them to self-inflicted harm, while a post-mortem examination commissioned by his family found signs interpreted as consistent with beating. Amnesty International recorded the contradiction and noted that an official inquiry had not been completed by the end of 1999. Contemporary human-rights reporting also described allegations of police brutality in Mauritius and the intense unrest that followed Kaya’s death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.refworld.org/reference/annualreport/amnesty/2000/en/86043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: refworld.org">[Refworld]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">refworld.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on refworld.org.</span></span></span>

The basic facts are not in dispute: Kaya was arrested for a minor drug offence, died in custody three days later, and had injuries requiring explanation. What remains contested is how those injuries were caused, what happened inside the cell and whether institutional responsibility was fully established.

Kaya’s importance extends beyond the forensic question. He helped create seggae, a fusion of reggae and the traditional Mauritian music style sega, and became associated with social criticism and Creole identity. His death therefore resonated as more than an isolated custody case. For many Mauritians, it appeared to confirm a wider pattern of unequal treatment and official impunity. International reporting at the time noted that the unrest was concentrated in poorer districts and was intensified by longstanding ethnic and social tensions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/1999/feb/25/7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Rioting in Mauritius set off by jail death of singer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Rioting in Mauritius set off by jail death of singer</span></span></span>

This case requires particular restraint. It should not be turned into a conspiracy story unsupported by evidence, but neither should uncertainty be used to blur the state’s responsibility to account for a death in custody. The historical mystery lies in the gap between competing explanations and the failure of the surviving public record to produce a universally trusted account.

Kaya’s death also shows how quickly a disputed event can enter national memory. To some, he became a martyr killed by police; to others, the subsequent riots overshadowed the original questions. A sound historical treatment keeps the focus on both the individual and the institutions: a man entered custody alive, died with injuries, and left behind unresolved questions that became inseparable from broader demands for dignity and accountability.

## Why Mauritius’s mysteries endure

The most persistent Mauritian mysteries survive for different reasons. The La Buse treasure grew because an entertaining literary construction was mistaken for archival evidence. The Le Morne story endures because oral memory carries experiences that colonial documents often excluded. The dodo’s extinction remains imprecise because seventeenth-century observers lacked consistent scientific language. Kaya’s death remains contested because institutional explanations failed to secure public confidence.

Together they suggest a useful test for judging any claimed national enigma:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Is the central object or document traceable to the period in question?
* Does the story appear in contemporary records, or only in much later retellings?
* Could the silence of the archive reflect the exclusion of marginalised people?
* Are different names being used for the same person, place or species?
* Does new evidence genuinely test the story, or merely reinterpret every clue to preserve it?

</div>

Mauritius’s historical mysteries are most compelling when the uncertainty is described precisely. There is strong evidence for maroon resistance at Le Morne, but not for every detail of the abolition-day tragedy. There is strong evidence for Levasseur’s piracy, but not for his famous cipher. There is no doubt that the dodo became extinct after human settlement, but no secure date for the death of the last individual. There is no doubt that Kaya died injured in police custody, while the exact sequence of events remains disputed.

That distinction between fact, inference and legend does not diminish the stories. It reveals why they matter. Each mystery marks a place where evidence was lost, neglected, mistrusted or reshaped—and where Mauritians have continued to ask who is remembered, whose testimony counts and how the country should understand its past.
