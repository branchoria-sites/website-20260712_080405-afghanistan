## Introduction

One of [Yemen]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-shape-yemens-past/' | relative_url }})'s most intriguing historical puzzles is not about a lost city or forgotten treasure, but about the night sky. Some medieval Yemeni chronicles appear to record the appearance of the supernova of AD 1006—the brightest stellar explosion ever reliably observed from Earth—roughly two weeks before the earliest sightings traditionally accepted by historians. If the date preserved in these chronicles is correct, observers in Yemen may have been the first people known to witness the event. The claim is fascinating because it rests on later historical texts preserving an even earlier observation, and because converting medieval Islamic calendar dates into the modern calendar is far from straightforward. The evidence is suggestive rather than conclusive, leaving historians and astronomers with a genuine but carefully bounded mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/asna.201613139" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryInterpretation of the historic Yemeni reports of supernova SN 1006: Early discovery in mid‐April 1006? - Neuhäuser...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1006</span></span></span>

## What the Yemeni chronicles describe

The key evidence comes from two Yemeni historians writing centuries after the event: al-Yamānī and the later Ibn al-Daybaʿ. Their accounts describe the sudden appearance of an exceptionally bright new star that was visible in the southern sky. Modern researchers concluded that the later account largely derives from the earlier one or from a common source, making al-Yamānī's version the more valuable witness because it preserves greater detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06126" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Supernova SN 1006 in two historic Yemeni reportsAugust 25, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2015</span></span></span>

The reports describe a brilliant object that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* first appeared on the evening of the 15th day of Rajab in the Islamic year 396 AH;
* rose shortly after sunset;
* remained fixed among the stars rather than moving like a comet;
* was extraordinarily bright and remained visible for many months before fading.

</div>

These characteristics closely match what is independently known about SN 1006, whose remnant still exists today. Unlike a comet, a supernova remains fixed against the background stars while gradually fading over weeks and months.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06126" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Supernova SN 1006 in two historic Yemeni reportsAugust 25, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2015</span></span></span>

## How historians converted the reported date

The central question is whether the reported Islamic date really corresponds to about 17 April 1006 on the Julian calendar.

Converting medieval Islamic dates is not a simple matter. The Islamic calendar was based on actual sightings of the crescent Moon, so the beginning of a month could vary by a day or two from modern calculated calendars. The surviving Yemeni texts were also written centuries after the event, creating opportunities for copying mistakes or slight alterations.

Researchers Wafiq Rada and Ralph Neuhäuser analysed the original Arabic wording and concluded that the reported date most likely corresponds to 17 April 1006, with an uncertainty of about two days. That would place the Yemeni observation roughly a week and a half earlier than the dates usually accepted from East Asian and other historical records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06126" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Supernova SN 1006 in two historic Yemeni reportsAugust 25, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2015</span></span></span>

The researchers also examined the description that the star "rose half an hour after sunset". Astronomical calculations show that this matches the sky as seen from Sana'a around mid-April remarkably well. Had the observation been made in late April or early May, the geometry would fit much less closely. This consistency strengthens the case that the reported date may preserve a genuine early observation rather than a later error.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/asna.201613139" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryInterpretation of the historic Yemeni reports of supernova SN 1006: Early discovery in mid‐April 1006? - Neuhäuser...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1006</span></span></span>

## Why an early sighting from Yemen is plausible

An early Yemeni discovery is not as surprising as it may first appear.

Sana'a lies at a relatively low northern latitude and at high elevation. SN 1006 appeared in the constellation Lupus, well into the southern sky. Observers in Yemen therefore enjoyed a more favourable view than observers much farther north in East Asia or Europe, where the star rose lower above the horizon.

If the supernova was initially becoming brighter over several days, southern observers with a clearer horizon could realistically have detected it before people in less favourable locations. Researchers argue that Yemen's geography makes such an early observation physically plausible rather than exceptional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/asna.201613139" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryInterpretation of the historic Yemeni reports of supernova SN 1006: Early discovery in mid‐April 1006? - Neuhäuser...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1006</span></span></span>

## Why the early sighting remains disputed

Despite the attractive case, historians have not reached complete agreement.

Several factors continue to prevent the Yemeni record from being accepted as definitive:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The reports are not contemporary manuscripts.** The surviving chronicles were written centuries after AD 1006, even if they probably preserve earlier material.
* **Calendar conversion remains uncertain.** Because the medieval Islamic calendar depended on local Moon sightings, any conversion carries an unavoidable uncertainty of one or two days.
* **Independent confirmation is limited.** Most other well-known historical records from China, Japan, Egypt and elsewhere have traditionally been interpreted as beginning around 28–30 April, although some of those dates themselves have been re-examined.
* **Transmission history is incomplete.** It remains uncertain exactly which earlier source al-Yamānī relied upon and whether the wording changed over successive copies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06126" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arxiv.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Supernova SN 1006 in two historic Yemeni reportsAugust 25, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2015</span></span></span>

</div>

Researchers supporting the earlier date have argued that several independent pieces of evidence—including a difficult-to-interpret Japanese record and the reported three-month visibility noted by European observers at St Gallen—fit better if the supernova became visible in mid-April rather than at the end of the month. Those arguments have attracted interest but have not eliminated all scholarly caution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.02915" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interpretation of the historic Yemeni reports of supernova SN 1006: early discovery in mid-April 1006?...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1006</span></span></span>

## What the best evidence suggests

The mystery today is no longer whether Yemen recorded SN 1006 at all—that identification is widely accepted—but whether its chronicle preserves the world's earliest known observation.

Most specialists accept that the Yemeni texts describe the supernova itself. The unresolved issue concerns chronology rather than identification. The proposed mid-April date is supported by careful philological work, astronomical modelling and the reported observing circumstances, yet it depends on interpreting later historical copies and converting a medieval lunar calendar with inherent uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-20794" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historical Records of Supernovae &#124; Springer Nature Link</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historical Records of Supernovae &#124; Springer Nature Link...</span></span></span>

The most balanced conclusion is therefore that Yemen has a credible claim to preserving the earliest known report of SN 1006, but not one that has achieved universal scholarly acceptance. Rather than overturning the historical record outright, the Yemeni chronicles have prompted historians to reconsider how the world's brightest recorded supernova may have first entered human history—and whether the first observers were watching from the highlands around Sana'a.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06126" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Supernova SN 1006 in two historic Yemeni reportsAugust 25, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 25, 2015</span></span></span>
