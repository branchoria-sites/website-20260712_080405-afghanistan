## Introduction

The death of Sultan Abdulaziz on 4 June 1876 remains one of the Ottoman Empire's most enduring historical controversies. Only days after being deposed in a military-backed coup, the former sultan was found dead in his room at Feriye Palace in Istanbul with deep cuts to his wrists. The official explanation at the time was suicide. Yet rumours of murder spread almost immediately, fuelled by political rivalry, contradictory witness accounts and widespread distrust of the new regime. Five years later, a dramatic political trial reached the opposite conclusion, convicting leading statesmen of organising Abdulaziz's murder. Even so, many modern historians remain unconvinced that the trial established what really happened. Instead, the case survives as a historical mystery because the physical evidence was limited, forensic medicine was still in its infancy, and later legal proceedings were themselves deeply entangled with politics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cmes.fas.harvard.edu/publications/ottoman-rule-law-and-modern-political-trial-yildiz-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cmes.fas.harvard.edu">[Center for Middle Eastern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cmes.fas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

## The Death at Feriye Palace

Sultan Abdulaziz was removed from the throne on 30 May 1876 during a constitutional crisis involving senior civilian and military officials. After a brief stay at Topkapı Palace, he was transferred under guard to Feriye Palace on the Bosphorus, where he lived in confinement with members of his household. Contemporary accounts describe him as distressed by his deposition, his isolation and the restrictions placed upon him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://worldhistoryedu.com/the-1876-coup-detat-that-removed-ottoman-sultan-abdulaziz/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldhistoryedu.com">[World History Edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldhistoryedu.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the 1876 coup detat that removed ottoman sultan abdulaziz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World History EduThe 1876 coup d&#x27;état that removed Ottoman Sultan Abdulaziz - World History EduOctober 11, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 11, 2024</span></span></span>

On the morning of 4 June, attendants discovered the former sultan dead. Official investigators concluded that he had used a pair of scissors to cut the veins in both wrists. Medical examinations carried out shortly afterwards supported the government's announcement that the wounds were self-inflicted, and the death was publicly declared a suicide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalarchive.library.bogazici.edu.tr/server/api/core/bitstreams/4ff75358-500b-4274-b29f-355e3eca3818/content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalarchive.library.bogazici.edu.tr">[Boğaziçi Digital Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalarchive.library.bogazici.edu.tr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Boğaziçi Digital Archive THE DEATHS AND FUNERAL CEREMONIES OF OTTOMAN SULTANS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Boğaziçi Digital Archive THE DEATHS AND FUNERAL CEREMONIES OF OTTOMAN SULTANS</span></span></span>

Almost immediately, however, doubts emerged. Critics questioned whether a heavily guarded former ruler could have obtained the instrument used, whether the wounds were consistent with suicide, and why some witness statements appeared inconsistent. Because the palace was under military control and the investigation remained in official hands, suspicions were never fully dispelled. Those early uncertainties became the foundation for nearly 150 years of debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cmes.fas.harvard.edu/publications/ottoman-rule-law-and-modern-political-trial-yildiz-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cmes.fas.harvard.edu">[Center for Middle Eastern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cmes.fas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

## Suicide and Murder Arguments

The mystery persists because both interpretations can point to genuine, though incomplete, evidence.

Those who accept the suicide verdict note several factors:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Abdulaziz had suffered an abrupt fall from supreme authority to confinement within a few days.
* Contemporary physicians examined the body soon after death and initially certified suicide.
* No decisive contemporary physical evidence proving homicide has ever emerged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalarchive.library.bogazici.edu.tr/server/api/core/bitstreams/4ff75358-500b-4274-b29f-355e3eca3818/content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalarchive.library.bogazici.edu.tr">[Boğaziçi Digital Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalarchive.library.bogazici.edu.tr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Boğaziçi Digital Archive THE DEATHS AND FUNERAL CEREMONIES OF OTTOMAN SULTANS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Boğaziçi Digital Archive THE DEATHS AND FUNERAL CEREMONIES OF OTTOMAN SULTANS</span></span></span>

</div>

Supporters of the murder theory emphasise different points:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The deposed sultan was under close guard, raising questions about how he could have acted without intervention.
* Witness testimony was sometimes contradictory regarding the discovery of the body and the sequence of events.
* Political opponents had an obvious motive if they feared that Abdulaziz might be restored to the throne.
* Later judicial proceedings accepted that the death had been engineered rather than self-inflicted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cmes.fas.harvard.edu/publications/ottoman-rule-law-and-modern-political-trial-yildiz-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cmes.fas.harvard.edu">[Center for Middle Eastern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cmes.fas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

</div>

Modern historians are generally cautious about both positions. They point out that nineteenth-century forensic medicine could not answer many questions that would today be examined through blood-spatter analysis, fingerprint evidence or modern pathology. Equally important, much of the surviving testimony comes from participants with strong political loyalties, making it difficult to separate observation from later justification. As a result, historians often distinguish between the historical event itself and the political narratives that quickly formed around it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cmes.fas.harvard.edu/publications/ottoman-rule-law-and-modern-political-trial-yildiz-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cmes.fas.harvard.edu">[Center for Middle Eastern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cmes.fas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

## How the Yildiz Trial Shaped the Story

The mystery acquired a second life through the famous Yıldız Trial of 1881. By then, Abdulaziz's nephew, Sultan Abdulhamid II, had firmly established himself on the throne. The government reopened the investigation and prosecuted several prominent figures associated with the 1876 deposition, including the celebrated reformer Midhat Pasha.

The proceedings attracted enormous attention inside and outside the Ottoman Empire. Prosecutors argued that Abdulaziz had been murdered as part of a political conspiracy and that the suicide narrative had been fabricated to conceal the crime. The court convicted several defendants and imposed death sentences, although Abdulhamid II later commuted many of them to life imprisonment or exile. Midhat Pasha was eventually imprisoned in Taif, where he died in 1884.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cmes.fas.harvard.edu/publications/ottoman-rule-law-and-modern-political-trial-yildiz-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cmes.fas.harvard.edu">[harvard.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cmes.fas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

The significance of the trial extends well beyond its verdict. Modern scholarship argues that it became one of the Ottoman Empire's earliest large-scale political trials, combining legal procedure with intense public debate, newspaper coverage and struggles over legitimacy. Rather than resolving the mystery, the proceedings helped create competing historical traditions that survived long after the participants had died.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cmes.fas.harvard.edu/publications/ottoman-rule-law-and-modern-political-trial-yildiz-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cmes.fas.harvard.edu">[Center for Middle Eastern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cmes.fas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

## Why Historians Still Disagree

The central problem is not simply conflicting opinions but conflicting kinds of evidence.

The original medical examination supported suicide, yet later testimony challenged its conclusions. The trial accepted murder, but many historians question whether judges operating under Abdulhamid II could genuinely act independently when the outcome carried enormous political significance. Conversely, accepting the original suicide verdict requires confidence in an investigation conducted by the very authorities who had just overthrown the sultan.

Because each body of evidence carries its own potential bias, no interpretation commands universal acceptance. Recent historical research has therefore shifted away from trying to declare a definitive winner and instead examines how different governments, political movements and later historians have used Abdulaziz's death to support broader arguments about constitutional reform, absolutism and the legitimacy of Ottoman rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cmes.fas.harvard.edu/publications/ottoman-rule-law-and-modern-political-trial-yildiz-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cmes.fas.harvard.edu">[Center for Middle Eastern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cmes.fas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

## Why the Mystery Still Matters

The disputed death of Sultan Abdulaziz remains significant not only because of uncertainty over how he died but because it illustrates how historical mysteries can be shaped as much by politics as by missing evidence.

Unlike many famous unsolved deaths, the case produced an official verdict, a later criminal trial and generations of competing interpretations. Each stage reflected the political priorities of its own era. For that reason, the question "Was Sultan Abdulaziz murdered after his deposition?" continues to be discussed not because historians lack theories, but because every major piece of evidence is connected to institutions and witnesses whose impartiality has itself become part of the mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cmes.fas.harvard.edu/publications/ottoman-rule-law-and-modern-political-trial-yildiz-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cmes.fas.harvard.edu">[Center for Middle Eastern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cmes.fas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>
