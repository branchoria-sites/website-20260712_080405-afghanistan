## Introduction

The mystery surrounding Lalibela's rock-hewn churches is not whether they were carved by human hands—they clearly were—but whether all eleven churches were created during the reign of a single king. Ethiopian religious tradition credits King Lalibela, who ruled in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, with creating a "New Jerusalem" by carving an extraordinary complex directly from solid volcanic rock. Modern archaeology broadly accepts that his reign transformed the site, but increasing evidence suggests that parts of the complex were older, others were altered later, and several buildings changed function over time. The real historical puzzle is therefore not *who* built Lalibela, but *how long the work took* and *how many generations contributed* to the landscape seen today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/18" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Rock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreRock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## The Sacred Story of King Lalibela

According to Ethiopian Orthodox tradition, King Lalibela received a divine vision to create a substitute for Jerusalem after Christian access to the Holy Land became increasingly difficult following the Muslim conquests of the late eleventh and twelfth centuries. His medieval biography even describes angels working alongside human craftsmen at night, allowing the churches to be completed within about twenty-four years. This sacred account remains central to the religious identity of the site rather than simply functioning as a historical construction record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/the-rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art The Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtThe Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

The churches certainly fit this religious programme. Their layout deliberately echoes places associated with biblical Jerusalem. A stream running through the complex is named the River Jordan, ceremonial pathways recreate pilgrimage routes, and churches are dedicated to major Christian figures. UNESCO describes the complex as a medieval "New Jerusalem", reflecting both royal ambition and living religious practice that continues today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/18" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Rock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreRock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

For centuries, this royal narrative dominated historical understanding. Only relatively recent archaeological study has questioned whether the entire monument could realistically have been completed during a single reign.

## Could One King Have Built Everything?

The scale of the engineering is astonishing regardless of who directed it. Unlike ordinary masonry churches, Lalibela's monuments were carved by removing enormous quantities of rock rather than assembling stone blocks.

Builders first isolated huge rectangular masses of volcanic tuff by cutting deep trenches around them. They then carved roofs, walls, windows and decorative details from the top downward while hollowing out the interiors. Drainage channels, stairways, tunnels, courtyards and ceremonial passages were integrated into the surrounding landscape rather than added afterwards. Because every mistake permanently damaged the living rock, the work demanded careful planning before carving began.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/18" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Rock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreRock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

The churches also differ markedly in design:

* Some resemble earlier Aksumite basilicas.
* Others show architectural features associated with later medieval Ethiopian churches.
* A few appear to have been adapted from structures that may originally have served secular purposes.
* Decorative styles vary enough to suggest changing architectural ideas over time rather than one uninterrupted building campaign.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/the-rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art The Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtThe Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

These differences have led many scholars to conclude that the site evolved through successive phases.

## Clues to Several Building Phases

One of the strongest arguments against a single construction campaign comes from architectural comparison.

Researchers increasingly suggest that the complex was created in four or five major phases extending across several centuries. Some elements may date as early as the seventh or eighth century, while the largest royal expansion belongs to the Zagwe period under King Lalibela in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries. Later generations continued altering interiors, adding paintings, modifying entrances and adapting buildings for changing liturgical needs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/the-rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art The Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtThe Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

Several churches provide particularly intriguing evidence.

**Biete Gabriel-Rufael** possesses defensive characteristics and a layout unlike a conventional church. Many archaeologists think it may originally have been part of a royal palace or fortified administrative complex before conversion into religious use. UNESCO likewise notes that Biete Gabriel-Rafael and Biete Mercoreos may once have been royal residences rather than churches from the outset.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/18" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Rock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreRock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

**Biete Mercoreos** also appears unusual. Architectural features and later modifications suggest repeated rebuilding, reinforcing the idea that functions within the complex changed over time rather than remaining fixed from the beginning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/18" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Rock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreRock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Meanwhile, the celebrated **Biete Ghiorgis**, carved as a near-perfect cross, differs so noticeably in both design and isolation that some scholars believe it represents one of the final stages of development rather than part of the earliest works. Although no consensus exists on its exact date, its architectural confidence hints that builders were refining techniques developed during earlier phases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/18" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Rock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreRock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## Why Dating the Churches Is So Difficult

Unlike timber buildings, Lalibela's churches contain little organic material suitable for radiocarbon dating. Stone itself cannot normally be dated to reveal when it was carved.

Instead, researchers combine several indirect forms of evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* architectural comparison with other Ethiopian churches;
* inscriptions and historical chronicles;
* analysis of tool marks and carving techniques;
* relationships between buildings and surrounding rock formations;
* later paintings and repairs whose dates can sometimes be estimated.

</div>

These methods can establish relative sequences but rarely produce precise construction years. That uncertainty explains why scholars often disagree over whether particular churches belong to the earliest phases or represent later additions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/the-rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art The Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtThe Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

## How an Evolving Site Became One Royal Vision

Even if the churches were built over several centuries, this does not necessarily contradict Ethiopian tradition.

A ruler who dramatically expanded an existing sacred landscape could naturally become remembered as its creator. Medieval royal histories frequently compressed long building programmes into the reign of a single celebrated monarch, especially when that ruler reshaped an entire religious centre. King Lalibela may therefore have transformed earlier monuments into a coherent pilgrimage complex while later generations attributed the whole achievement to him.

The physical layout supports this possibility. Existing structures appear to have been integrated into a carefully planned sacred landscape connected by trenches, symbolic rivers and ceremonial routes. Rather than constructing every church from nothing, Lalibela's builders may have reorganised, enlarged and unified monuments that already existed in different forms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/the-rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art The Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtThe Rock-hewn Churches of Lalibela - The Metropolitan Museum of Art...</span></span></span>

## What the Best Evidence Suggests Today

Most historians no longer see the debate as a choice between complete legend and complete fact.

Several points command broad agreement:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* King Lalibela played a decisive role in creating the medieval pilgrimage centre.
* The churches were carved directly from living volcanic rock using sophisticated engineering.
* Some buildings probably underwent significant alteration after their original excavation.
* Certain structures may have begun life as royal or secular buildings before conversion into churches.
* The complex likely developed through multiple construction phases rather than one continuous project.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/18" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Rock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreRock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

What remains unresolved is the precise chronology. No single piece of evidence definitively establishes when every church was begun or completed, leaving room for ongoing archaeological debate.

## Why the Mystery Still Matters

The enduring mystery of Lalibela lies not in claims of supernatural construction but in the remarkable way history, faith and archaeology overlap. For pilgrims, the churches remain a sacred landscape closely associated with Saint Lalibela regardless of the precise sequence of excavation. For historians, they illustrate how medieval memory can preserve genuine historical events while simplifying complex processes into the story of a single visionary ruler.

Rather than diminishing the achievement, the possibility of centuries of construction makes Lalibela even more extraordinary. It suggests a sacred landscape continually reshaped by successive generations until separate monuments became one of Christianity's most remarkable pilgrimage centres—an achievement whose full construction history is still being uncovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/18" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Rock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreRock-Hewn Churches, Lalibela - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>
