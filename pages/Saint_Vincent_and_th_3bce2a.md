## Did the Garifuna begin with a shipwreck?

One of the best-known stories in Caribbean history says that Africans reached Saint Vincent after a slave ship was wrecked near the island during the seventeenth century. The survivors supposedly joined the Indigenous population, and their descendants became the people whom colonial writers called “Black Caribs” and who today identify as Garifuna.

The broad historical foundation is secure. By the colonial period, Saint Vincent contained communities of mixed African and Indigenous ancestry that resisted European control. After Britain defeated them in the Second Carib War, thousands were expelled from Saint Vincent, held on Balliceaux and eventually transported to Roatán, off the coast of present-day Honduras. Their descendants established communities across Honduras, Belize, Guatemala and Nicaragua. UNESCO describes Garifuna culture as the result of Indigenous Caribbean and African traditions, originating among people forced from Saint Vincent in the eighteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/language-dance-and-music-of-the-garifuna-00001" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ich.unesco.org">[UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ich.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO Intangible Cultural HeritageLanguage, dance and music of the GarifunaA population of mixed origin incorporating cultural elements...</span></span></span>

What remains uncertain is whether one particular wreck created this population. Early European writers repeated versions of the shipwreck story, and oral tradition has preserved a date commonly given as 1635. Yet researchers have struggled to identify a matching vessel in surviving shipping records. Some historians therefore argue that Africans arrived by several routes: shipwrecks, escapes from plantations on neighbouring islands, raids, inter-island trade and independent migration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/40654135" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">THE DEVELOPMENT OF GARIFUNA RITUAL ON ST...by B FOSTER · 1987 · Cited by 21 — recorded in 1674 that while the bulk of the Africans...</span></span></span>

The most plausible explanation is not that the tradition is wholly false, but that it compresses a long and complicated process into one memorable event. A wreck may have contributed survivors without being the sole beginning of Garifuna society. Alternatively, the wreck could function as a founding story that expresses an essential truth—African newcomers joining Indigenous communities—even where precise names and dates were lost.

This distinction matters because older colonial histories often described the Garifuna as accidental outsiders rather than a people formed within Saint Vincent through generations of alliance, kinship and resistance. Modern scholarship treats their emergence as a process of ethnogenesis: the creation of a new community and identity under changing historical conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S1870-41152007000200401&amp;script=sci_arttext" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scielo.org.mx">[SciELO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scielo.org.mx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How did The Garifuna become an indigenous people?by J Palacio · 2007 · Cited by 14 — The Garifuna – an indigenous people formed from the...</span></span></span>

## How did Joseph Chatoyer die?

Joseph Chatoyer, also known as Satuye, led Garifuna resistance during the conflict now called the Second Carib War. He was killed at Dorsetshire Hill on 14 March 1795 and is recognised as Saint Vincent and the Grenadines’ first national hero. The uncertainty concerns the precise circumstances of his death.

The familiar colonial version describes a dramatic encounter in which Major Alexander Leith killed Chatoyer in single combat. This story was repeated in later British accounts and became the basis for paintings and commemorative narratives. Leith’s family history also preserved the claim that he had personally defeated the resistance leader.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oxfordaasc.com/display/10.1093/acref/9780195301731.001.0001/acref-9780195301731-e-73630" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oxfordaasc.com">[oxfordaasc.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oxfordaasc.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on oxfordaasc.com.</span></span></span>

There are reasons for caution. The main written descriptions came from British officials, soldiers and landowners whose interests were directly opposed to Chatoyer’s. The Garifuna left no comparable written military record, meaning that historians are heavily dependent on accounts produced by his enemies. A modern biographical study notes that the principal early sources include the writings of colonial administrator Sir William Young and the nineteenth-century historian Charles Shephard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://projects.kora.matrix.msu.edu/files/16-23-106157/Joseph_Chatoyer_DCALAB.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: projects.kora.matrix.msu.edu">[projects.kora.matrix.msu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">projects.kora.matrix.msu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Chatoyer, Joseph &#124; Oxford African American Studies Center</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chatoyer, Joseph &#124; Oxford African American Studies Center</span></span></span>

The setting also complicates the romantic duel narrative. Chatoyer died during confused night fighting around Dorsetshire Hill, not at a formally arranged contest. Vincentian historian Adrian Fraser has questioned whether a clean, heroic duel is credible under such conditions and has drawn attention to competing descriptions of an ambush, battlefield encounter or collective attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://enslaved.org/fullStory/16-23-106157/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: enslaved.org">[enslaved.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">enslaved.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on enslaved.org.</span></span></span>

Several elements are reasonably well established:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Chatoyer was killed during fighting at Dorsetshire Hill.
* Alexander Leith was credited by British sources with killing him.
* His death weakened, but did not immediately end, Garifuna resistance.
* No surviving account from Chatoyer’s own side gives a detailed alternative version.

</div>

The doubtful element is the polished scene of two leaders meeting in honourable single combat. That presentation may have served colonial memory by transforming the killing of an anti-colonial commander into a tale of British martial courage. It also reduced a wider war over territory, treaty rights and settlement to a personal contest between two men.

The uncertainty surrounding Chatoyer’s death therefore reveals a larger archival problem: many of Saint Vincent’s foundational events are known through records written by those attempting to conquer the island. The question is not simply who struck the fatal blow, but how a colonial battlefield report became accepted as national history.

## What happened to the people imprisoned on Balliceaux?

Balliceaux, a small island in the Grenadines, was used by British authorities as a detention site after the defeat of the Garifuna. Thousands of men, women and children were confined there in 1796 before the surviving deportees were transported to Roatán in 1797. Disease, malnutrition, exposure and overcrowding caused catastrophic loss of life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.winchester.ac.uk/files/3319702/2964142_Finneran_ShadowOfBalliceaux_original.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.winchester.ac.uk">[University of Winchester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.winchester.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2964142 Finneran ShadowOfBalliceaux original</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2964142 Finneran ShadowOfBalliceaux original</span></span></span>

The central facts are not mysterious. The imprisonment occurred, mortality was extremely high, and the deportation permanently altered the history of Saint Vincent and Central America. The unresolved questions concern numbers, burial places and the physical evidence left behind.

Different sources give somewhat different totals. Research cited in an archaeological study estimates that roughly 4,195 detainees were sent to Balliceaux and that about half died within several months. Contemporary political and commemorative accounts commonly refer to approximately 2,000 or nearly 2,500 deaths. Such variations reflect differences between surrendered prisoners, people actually transferred to the island, deaths occurring before or during detention, and those eventually placed aboard deportation vessels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.winchester.ac.uk/files/3319702/2964142_Finneran_ShadowOfBalliceaux_original.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.winchester.ac.uk">[University of Winchester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.winchester.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2964142 Finneran ShadowOfBalliceaux original</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2964142 Finneran ShadowOfBalliceaux original</span></span></span>

No comprehensive archaeological excavation has mapped the burial areas or established an exact death toll. The locations of graves are largely unmarked, and the island’s later private ownership limited sustained heritage work. Balliceaux consequently occupies an unusual position: it is one of the most historically important landscapes in the country, yet much of its physical record has never been systematically documented.

This absence has encouraged dramatic claims, but the strongest interpretation is straightforward. Balliceaux is not a supernatural “island of death”, nor is its tragedy an invention created by modern tourism or politics. It is a documented site of mass confinement whose surviving records and landscape remain incomplete.

The island has also become a place of Garifuna memory. Pilgrimages and commemorations connect Saint Vincent with descendant communities in Central America and the wider diaspora. Recent public debate about the island’s future has focused on whether it should be protected as a memorial and heritage site rather than treated simply as private property.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343015907_Mourning_Balliceaux_Towards_a_biography_of_a_Caribbean_island_of_death_grief_and_memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate Mourning Balliceaux: Towards a biography of a Caribbean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate Mourning Balliceaux: Towards a biography of a Caribbean</span></span></span>

The real mystery of Balliceaux is therefore archival and archaeological: who died there, where they were buried, and how much evidence survives beneath the ground? Careful non-invasive survey, documentary comparison and community-led archaeology could answer some of these questions without disturbing human remains unnecessarily.

## Who carved the rocks, and what do they mean?

Saint Vincent and the Grenadines contains one of the most important concentrations of rock art in the Lesser Antilles. Around 18 sites have been recorded, mainly near coastal river valleys, with examples on Saint Vincent and in the Grenadines. The carvings appear on boulders, exposed rock faces and at least one rock shelter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5749/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The best-known example is the large engraved boulder at Layou. National heritage information tentatively associates it with the Saladoid period, roughly the first millennium AD, while UNESCO’s tentative World Heritage description notes similarities between the main figure and religious imagery found farther north in the Caribbean. The Layou engraving is described as the largest individual petroglyph in the Lesser Antilles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5749/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Those comparisons are useful, but they do not amount to a translation. Petroglyphs are not necessarily writing in the ordinary sense. They may represent ancestors, spiritual beings, mythic events, territorial markers, water-related rituals or combinations of these meanings. Without a surviving explanation from the makers, identifying a visual resemblance does not prove that the same figure carried the same name or function everywhere.

Dating is also difficult. Designs carved directly into stone rarely contain material suitable for radiocarbon testing. Archaeologists must instead use their relationship to nearby settlements, carving styles, weathering and comparisons with pottery or imagery from better-dated sites. This means that apparently precise dates quoted in tourism material should be understood as estimates rather than direct laboratory measurements.

Some interpretations have gone further. Individual researchers have proposed that certain Vincentian carvings represent wrapped ancestors or figures connected with burial customs. Others have suggested astronomical alignments or influences from cultures outside the Caribbean. The ancestor interpretation has parallels elsewhere in Indigenous Caribbean belief, but more elaborate claims require stronger evidence than visual similarity alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.world-archaeology.com/features/caribbean-petroglyphs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: world-archaeology.com">[World Archaeology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">world-archaeology.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Archaeology Caribbean Petroglyphs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Archaeology Caribbean Petroglyphs</span></span></span>

The safest conclusions are limited but significant:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The carvings are genuinely pre-Columbian or Indigenous in origin.
* They belong to a wider Caribbean tradition of marking meaningful places in stone.
* Watercourses, valleys and landscape position were probably important to their use.
* Their exact names, stories and ceremonial functions remain unknown.

</div>

Calling the carvings “Carib stones” can also be misleading if it implies that every example was made by the latest pre-colonial or early colonial Kalinago inhabitants. Saint Vincent was occupied by different Indigenous communities over many centuries. Some carvings may pre-date the social groups encountered by Europeans, while others could belong to later traditions.

Their mystery survives because the images remain visible while the explanatory culture was violently disrupted. They should therefore be treated neither as meaningless decoration nor as a blank surface on which modern observers can project any theory they choose.

## The wreck beneath Kingstown Harbour

A wreck investigated in Kingstown Harbour offers a more conventional archaeological puzzle. Divers encountered the remains of a substantial wooden vessel armed with heavy iron cannon and protected by copper sheathing. Archaeological work in 1997–98 established that it was probably a French naval ship from the late 1770s or early 1780s, but the vessel’s name remained uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/3383530/In_Soufreries_Shadow_An_Introduction_to_an_Historic_Shipwreck_in_Kingstown_Harbour_St_Vincent_and_the_Grenadines" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

Evidence included a French 12-pounder cannon dated 1776, the size of the ship and material suggesting naval rather than ordinary commercial use. The location and estimated date placed the wreck within a turbulent period when France and Britain repeatedly contested Saint Vincent during the wider American Revolutionary War.

Later archival research produced a strong candidate: the French frigate *Junon*. Records indicate that the 32-gun ship reached Saint Vincent with hospital supplies and was lost in Kingstown Harbour during the Great Hurricane of 1780. A French-American archaeological team returned to the site in 2021, describing *Junon* as the likely identity, and further investigation followed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.staugustinelighthouse.org/2023/05/22/return-to-the-french-frigate-junon/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: staugustinelighthouse.org">[staugustinelighthouse.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">staugustinelighthouse.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Return to the French Frigate, Junon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Return to the French Frigate, Junon</span></span></span>

This is best described as a mystery approaching resolution rather than a fully unidentified wreck. The archaeological date, nationality, armament, harbour location and documentary account all fit *Junon*. Absolute certainty, however, normally requires a uniquely identifying artefact, inscription or combination of structural features that excludes every other candidate.

The case demonstrates how historical investigation works when several kinds of evidence survive. Archaeology first narrowed the vessel to a period and nationality; archival research then supplied a name that matched the material remains. Unlike legends of pirate gold, the importance of the wreck lies in what it can reveal about naval warfare, hurricanes, French occupation and everyday life aboard an eighteenth-century warship.

## Why these mysteries endure

The most enduring mysteries of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines share a common cause: unequal survival of evidence. Colonial governments generated extensive records, but those records reflected their own priorities. Indigenous and Garifuna knowledge was more often transmitted orally, while war, deportation and population dispersal broke the continuity through which stories and place-based knowledge were preserved.

That imbalance explains why apparently simple questions remain difficult. The British recorded military victory but not Chatoyer’s own account of his final battle. Officials counted prisoners, yet did not leave a complete and uncontested record of those who died on Balliceaux. European observers described [Garifuna origins]({{ 'garifuna-origins/' | relative_url }}), but their stories cannot be separated easily from oral tradition, racial categorisation and political interests. Archaeologists can record the shapes of petroglyphs, but not recover with certainty the words once spoken beside them.

The best-supported interpretation of the country’s enigmatic past therefore avoids both extremes. Colonial accounts should not be accepted uncritically, but neither should every gap be filled with conspiracy or legend. Oral tradition can preserve genuine historical knowledge without functioning as a precise shipping register. Archaeological resemblance can suggest cultural connections without providing a literal translation.

These cases continue to matter because they concern people whose descendants are still seeking recognition, places that remain politically contested and symbols that form part of national identity. Their unresolved elements are not failures of history. They are reminders that the historical record was created unevenly—and that recovering Saint Vincent and the Grenadines’ past requires listening closely to archives, landscapes, objects and living communities together.

      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
