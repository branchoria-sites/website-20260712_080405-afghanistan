## Introduction

Iroungou Cave is one of the most remarkable archaeological discoveries ever made in [Gabon]({{ 'what-still-mystifies-us-about-gabons/' | relative_url }}), yet it remains one of the country's greatest historical mysteries. Hidden beneath the rainforest in Ngounié Province, the cave contains the remains of at least 28 people, together with hundreds of valuable objects dating to the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Archaeologists are confident that this was not an accidental death trap or a natural bone accumulation. Instead, it was a carefully chosen burial place used repeatedly by a community with sophisticated funerary customs and far-reaching trade connections. What remains unknown is who these people were, why they alone were buried in such an inaccessible place, and what social or religious beliefs lay behind the practice. Those unanswered questions make Iroungou one of the most important unresolved archaeological puzzles in West Central Africa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

## What archaeologists found in the cave

Iroungou Cave lies near Mouila in southern Gabon. Although the cave itself was known earlier, its archaeological importance only became clear after systematic exploration beginning in 2018. Access is extremely difficult: today the main chamber can only be reached by descending through a natural shaft around 15 metres deep into a cave system extending roughly 25 metres below the surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

Inside, researchers discovered thousands of scattered human bones representing at least 28 individuals. The assemblage includes men, women and children, indicating that the cave was not reserved for a single age or sex. Although most skeletons had become disarticulated through centuries of natural disturbance, a few partially connected bones showed that complete bodies—not isolated bones—had originally been placed inside the cave. Gravity, water flow, calcite formation and animal activity later disturbed many of the remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

Radiocarbon dating places the burials mainly in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, shortly before Portuguese contact with the West Central African coast. Dating also indicates that the cave was used on more than one occasion rather than during a single event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

Equally striking was the quantity of grave goods. Archaeologists documented more than 500 metal artefacts alongside marine shells and animal teeth used as ornaments. The finds include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Iron bracelets and rings.
* Knives, axes and spearheads.
* Iron hoes, which may also have functioned as prestige or exchange objects.
* Copper ornaments.
* Atlantic marine shells carried far inland.
* Pierced carnivore teeth used as personal adornments.

</div>

The sheer richness of these deposits has no close archaeological parallel yet identified in West Central Africa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

## What the teeth and grave goods reveal

One of the cave's most distinctive discoveries is that every sufficiently preserved upper jaw lacks its permanent front incisors. This was not caused by decay or post-mortem damage. Bone remodelling around the tooth sockets demonstrates that the teeth had been deliberately removed during life.

Intentional tooth removal was practised in various African societies for many different reasons, including marking adulthood, expressing ethnic identity, signalling beauty ideals or demonstrating membership of a particular community. At Iroungou, however, every examined individual appears to share the same distinctive pattern: removal of all four upper incisors while leaving the lower front teeth intact. Researchers believe this uniformity strongly suggests a shared cultural identity, but they cannot determine exactly what that identity represented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

The artefacts provide equally important clues. Copper was relatively scarce in West Central Africa and appears to have been imported over considerable distances, probably from the Niari Basin in what is now the Republic of the Congo, around 400 kilometres away. The Atlantic shells also demonstrate long-distance exchange between coastal and inland communities. Rather than depicting an isolated forest population, the cave points towards participation in extensive regional trade networks before European arrival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

Some artefacts appear to have been prestige goods rather than everyday equipment. Combined with the labour required to place bodies into such a difficult location, the burials imply considerable planning and investment. The cave therefore represents a socially significant place rather than a convenient cemetery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

## Why the identities of the dead remain unknown

Despite the remarkable preservation of many bones, archaeology cannot yet answer the question that attracts the greatest public interest: who exactly were these people?

No written records describe the cave or the community that used it. The burials predate sustained European documentation of the region, while the tropical environment has destroyed many forms of organic evidence that survive elsewhere.

Recent biological anthropology has added another layer of complexity. Analysis of cranial morphology suggests that the individuals display considerable variation rather than representing a single, biologically uniform population. Some show similarities with Central African Bantu populations, others with Bayaka groups, while still others exhibit affinities with populations farther east or south. Researchers caution that these comparisons are statistical rather than direct evidence of ancestry, but they indicate that the people buried at Iroungou cannot easily be assigned to one modern ethnic group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002%2Fajpa.24997" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryPopulation affinities in pre‐colonial West Africa: The case of the burial cave Iroungou (Gabon, 14th–15th century CE)...</span></span></span>

This diversity raises several possibilities. The cave may have served:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A politically important community drawing people from different backgrounds.
* A regional elite connected by status rather than ancestry.
* A ritual association whose membership crossed ordinary community boundaries.
* A burial place linked to marriage networks or long-distance exchange.

</div>

At present, none of these explanations can be demonstrated conclusively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002%2Fajpa.24997" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryPopulation affinities in pre‐colonial West Africa: The case of the burial cave Iroungou (Gabon, 14th–15th century CE)...</span></span></span>

## The leading explanations for the burials

Archaeologists have considered several competing interpretations, each supported by some evidence but none fully explaining every feature of the site.

### An elite burial place

The quantity of valuable metal objects is the strongest argument that those buried here held unusually high status. Wealthy individuals are more likely to receive elaborate funerary treatment, and the transport of expensive copper and iron objects into the cave suggests deliberate ceremonial investment.

The difficulty is explaining why children were included alongside adults if the cave served only political leaders or warriors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

### A burial place for a distinctive community

The shared dental modification points towards a group with common cultural practices. Rather than reflecting wealth alone, the cave may have been reserved for members of a lineage, religious society or community whose identity was marked by tooth ablation.

This explanation fits the uniform body modification but leaves unanswered why the community adopted such an inaccessible burial place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

### Ritual burial involving accompanying deaths

Researchers have cautiously raised the possibility that some individuals may have been retainers buried alongside socially important people. Comparable practices occurred in parts of Africa and elsewhere, where attendants accompanied rulers in death.

However, the evidence remains circumstantial. No clear skeletal signs demonstrate sacrifice, and archaeologists emphasise that this hypothesis requires much more evidence before it can be accepted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

### A response to violence or catastrophe

Mass burials often suggest warfare, epidemic disease or famine. Yet Iroungou does not resemble a hurried emergency disposal. The presence of abundant prestige goods, the repeated use of the cave over time and the absence of widespread traumatic injuries argue against a simple disaster scenario. While a crisis cannot be completely excluded, it currently has less support than interpretations centred on specialised funerary practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

## Why Iroungou Cave matters

Iroungou Cave has transformed understanding of pre-colonial Gabon. For decades, archaeologists possessed relatively few excavated human remains from this part of Central Africa, limiting knowledge of burial customs, health, identity and regional connections. The cave demonstrates that sophisticated ceremonial traditions flourished in the rainforest centuries before sustained European contact.

It also challenges outdated assumptions that dense tropical forests were isolated from wider political and economic networks. Imported copper, Atlantic shells and elaborate metalwork reveal communities connected through long-distance exchange extending across hundreds of kilometres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>

At the same time, the cave reminds historians of the limits of archaeological evidence. Researchers can establish when the burials occurred, how bodies were deposited and what objects accompanied them. They cannot yet identify the names, language, political affiliations or beliefs of those interred there. Until further excavation, biomolecular analysis and regional archaeological discoveries provide additional evidence, the people of Iroungou remain anonymous, making the cave one of Gabon's most compelling and enduring historical mysteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mortuary-behaviour-and-cultural-practices-in-precolonial-west-central-africa-new-data-from-the-iroungou-burial-cave-gabon/DE4A50C77E13DF2BD677011450312C03" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentMortuary behaviour and cultural practices in pre-colonial West Central Africa: new data from the I...</span></span></span>
