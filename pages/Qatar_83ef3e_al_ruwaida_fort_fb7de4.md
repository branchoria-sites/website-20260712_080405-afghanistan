## Introduction

The mystery surrounding Al Ruwaida is not whether the site existed—it plainly did—but whether its great fort began life as a Portuguese military stronghold during the sixteenth century. Archaeologists agree that Al Ruwaida was one of the largest fortified coastal settlements in northern [Qatar]({{ 'what-does-qatars-past-still-refuse-to/' | relative_url }}) between roughly the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. What remains uncertain is who first built its massive central enclosure. A Portuguese map from 1563 appears to show an important settlement somewhere on the Qatari peninsula, and parts of the fort's design resemble Portuguese military architecture from elsewhere in the Gulf. Yet no inscription, document or securely identifiable artefact has confirmed Portuguese construction. The debate therefore remains one of archaeology and historical comparison rather than established fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://qm.org.qa/en/visit/heritage-sites/al-ruwaida-archaeological-site/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qm.org.qa">[qm.org.qa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qm.org.qa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Qatar Museums Al Ruwaida Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Qatar MuseumsAl Ruwaida Archaeological Site - Qatar Museums...</span></span></span>

## The Fortified Town Beneath the Ruins

Al Ruwaida stretches for around 2.5 kilometres along Qatar's north-western coast and preserves the remains of a substantial maritime settlement. Excavations have revealed a large central fort, mosques, warehouses, wells, domestic buildings and evidence for boat repair or maintenance, indicating that the town was an active commercial and maritime centre rather than simply a military outpost. Occupation appears to have begun around the sixteenth century and continued, with rebuilding and periods of decline, into the eighteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://qm.org.qa/en/visit/heritage-sites/al-ruwaida-archaeological-site/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qm.org.qa">[Qatar Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qm.org.qa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Qatar Museums Al Ruwaida Archaeological Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Qatar MuseumsAl Ruwaida Archaeological Site - Qatar Museums...</span></span></span>

The fort dominates the site. Archaeological work has shown that it passed through several major construction phases rather than being built all at once. The earliest enclosure covered approximately 2.25 hectares, making it the largest known fortification of its type in Qatar. Later rebuilding altered both its layout and defensive system before the occupied area was eventually reduced. These successive phases make it difficult to identify the original builders because later occupants modified much of the earlier fabric.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1590" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>

This layered history is central to the mystery. Archaeologists can reconstruct how the fort changed over time, but the earliest phase has not produced a definitive inscription, foundation text or unmistakably Portuguese military equipment that would settle the question.

## The Portuguese Map and Architectural Clues

The strongest argument for Portuguese involvement comes from a combination of cartographic evidence and architectural comparison rather than direct excavation.

In 1563 the Portuguese cartographer Lázaro Luís produced a map showing a settlement labelled "Cidade de Catar" ("City of Qatar"). It is the earliest known European map to depict a named settlement on the Qatari peninsula. Because Al Ruwaida was one of the largest fortified coastal towns known from the appropriate period, several historians have suggested that it could be the settlement represented on the map. The identification is plausible but not certain because the map lacks the geographical precision needed to pinpoint the location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruwayda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Architecture provides the second strand of the theory. Researchers have noted features in the earliest fortification that resemble Portuguese military works elsewhere around the Gulf and Indian Ocean during the age of artillery. These include aspects of the enclosure's scale, defensive planning and adaptation to firearms that differ from many later local forts in Qatar. Archaeologists have therefore argued that the earliest construction belongs within the broader military architecture of the sixteenth-century Gulf rather than the better-known eighteenth- or nineteenth-century Qatari forts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1590" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>

These observations fit the wider historical context. During the sixteenth century Portugal controlled key maritime routes through the Gulf after establishing itself at Hormuz and constructing or occupying numerous coastal fortifications. Portuguese written sources and maps also indicate that they maintained a fortress somewhere in Qatar during this period, although they never clearly identify its precise location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://explorer.nmoq.org.qa/api/en/artefact/?culture=english&amp;id=aHR0cHM6Ly9xY3BtYWluc3Rvbm1vcWttMDEuYmxvYi5jb3JlLndpbmRvd3MubmV0L3Byb2RxbWRpZ2xpYi9hc3NldHMvRzMvQVJDLjAwMDAuMDAuMDcuanBn0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: explorer.nmoq.org.qa">[NMOQ Explorer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">explorer.nmoq.org.qa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NMOQ Explorer Artefact</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NMOQ Explorer Artefact</span></span></span>

## Why the Portuguese Theory Remains Unproven

Despite these intriguing clues, the evidence falls short of proving Portuguese construction.

The principal difficulty is the absence of direct archaeological confirmation. Excavations have not uncovered inscriptions naming Portuguese officials, dated foundation stones, coins from a securely dated construction level or military artefacts that can only be explained by Portuguese occupation. Instead, the argument depends on circumstantial evidence drawn from architecture and historical geography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1590" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>

Dating also presents problems. Although the settlement clearly flourished during the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries, the earliest construction phases cannot yet be tied precisely to documented Portuguese activity. Buildings were repeatedly repaired, altered and reused, making it difficult to isolate the original layout from later modifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1590" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>

Finally, Portuguese records themselves are incomplete. Their maps and reports acknowledge an interest in Qatar during their struggle to dominate Gulf shipping, but surviving documentation does not identify Al Ruwaida by name. As a result, historians must infer connections rather than demonstrate them conclusively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://explorer.nmoq.org.qa/api/en/artefact/?culture=english&amp;id=aHR0cHM6Ly9xY3BtYWluc3Rvbm1vcWttMDEuYmxvYi5jb3JlLndpbmRvd3MubmV0L3Byb2RxbWRpZ2xpYi9hc3NldHMvRzMvQVJDLjAwMDAuMDAuMDcuanBn0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: explorer.nmoq.org.qa">[NMOQ Explorer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">explorer.nmoq.org.qa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NMOQ Explorer Artefact</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NMOQ Explorer Artefact</span></span></span>

## Regional Builders and Rival Explanations

Not all scholars accept that Portuguese engineers built the fort.

One alternative interpretation is that the fort was constructed by local Arab rulers or merchants who adapted defensive ideas circulating around the Gulf. Military architecture spread through trade, warfare and observation, meaning that similarities to Portuguese forts do not necessarily imply Portuguese builders. Local communities could have adopted effective features such as thicker walls or gunpowder-era defensive layouts without direct foreign construction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1590" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>

Another possibility is that the fort belonged to a regional political power allied with, opposed to or influenced by the Portuguese rather than to the Portuguese themselves. The Gulf during the sixteenth century saw overlapping influence from local Arab dynasties, Hormuz, the Portuguese Empire and, later, the Ottomans. Architectural borrowing was common in this competitive environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://explorer.nmoq.org.qa/api/en/artefact/?culture=english&amp;id=aHR0cHM6Ly9xY3BtYWluc3Rvbm1vcWttMDEuYmxvYi5jb3JlLndpbmRvd3MubmV0L3Byb2RxbWRpZ2xpYi9hc3NldHMvRzMvQVJDLjAwMDAuMDAuMDcuanBn0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: explorer.nmoq.org.qa">[NMOQ Explorer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">explorer.nmoq.org.qa</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NMOQ Explorer Artefact</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NMOQ Explorer Artefact</span></span></span>

Some archaeologists therefore favour a more cautious conclusion: the fort reflects the military landscape of the sixteenth-century Gulf but cannot yet be assigned confidently to any single state or empire.

## Why the Debate Still Matters

The question of who first built Al Ruwaida is more than an architectural puzzle. It affects how historians understand Qatar's place in the changing political geography of the Gulf during the early modern period.

If the Portuguese theory were confirmed, Al Ruwaida would represent the clearest archaeological evidence of Portugal's direct military presence on the Qatari peninsula and would help explain how European naval expansion intersected with Gulf trade and pearling. If the theory is disproved, the site instead demonstrates that regional builders developed or adopted sophisticated fortification techniques independently within the same strategic environment.

Current archaeological evidence favours neither extreme. The settlement's importance is firmly established, the fort unquestionably belongs to the era when Portugal dominated much of the Gulf's maritime network, and several architectural features invite comparison with Portuguese fortifications. Yet the decisive proof that would identify its original builders has not been found. Until new excavations uncover datable inscriptions, distinctive artefacts or previously unknown archival records, Al Ruwaida will remain one of Qatar's most intriguing archaeological debates rather than a solved historical mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1590" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeopresspublishing.com">[archaeopresspublishing.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeopresspublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeopresspublishing.com.</span></span></span>
