## Introduction

What makes these cases important is the gap between what can be proved and what people continue to suspect. In some, such as the disappearance of Mona Blades, the basic sequence is clear but the decisive evidence is missing. In others, such as the Kaimanawa “wall”, the physical puzzle has largely been explained, yet the rejected theory remains culturally persistent. Together, they show how New Zealand mysteries grow from incomplete records, inaccessible landscapes, institutional failures and the understandable human desire to turn uncertainty into a finished story.

## The Crewe murders: a wrongful conviction without a solved crime

Harvey and Jeannette Crewe disappeared from their farmhouse at Pukekawa, south of Auckland, in June 1970. Their 18-month-old daughter was found alive several days later, apparently having been fed while her parents were missing. Harvey’s body was recovered from the Waikato River in August; Jeannette’s was found there the following month. Both had been shot.

A neighbouring farmer, Arthur Allan Thomas, was convicted twice. The prosecution case relied heavily on a cartridge case said to have been found in the Crewes’ garden and linked to Thomas’s rifle. After years of public campaigning, Thomas received a royal pardon in 1979. That finding resolved one question but deepened another. If Thomas did not kill the Crewes, who did? Later police reviews reconsidered suspects and evidence but did not establish a prosecution case against anyone else. New Zealand’s official crime history therefore records both that Thomas was pardoned and that responsibility for the murders remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/culture/nz-crime-timeline" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nz crime timeline</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New Zealand crime timeline1975 Disappearance of Mona Blades. The case endures because it is simultaneously a murder mystery and a documented miscarriage of justice. Speculation has focused on family relationships, financial tensions and people known to the couple, but no alternative theory has been proved. The responsible conclusion is not that a particular uncharged person “must” have done it. It is that a compromised investigation and planted evidence may have made the original crime permanently harder to solve.

## Mona Blades and the orange Datsun

On 31 May 1975, 18-year-old Mona Blades set out to hitchhike from Hamilton to her family home in Hastings. She intended to surprise her family at her nephew’s first birthday celebration and was carrying a present for him. She was last reliably reported in the back seat of an orange Datsun station wagon.

A fencing contractor later saw a similar vehicle on Matea Road, off the highway between Taupō and Napier. He reported seeing a young woman fitting Mona’s description in the rear and a middle-aged man driving. When he passed again, the car was empty. Police searched a vast stretch of highway and investigated hundreds of orange Datsuns, but Mona’s body and belongings were never found. No one was charged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/keyword/mona-blades" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mona BladesEighteen-year-old Mona Blades was last seen sitting in the back seat of an orange Datsun station wagon. Her body was...</span></span></span>

The car sighting is the case’s most memorable clue and also one of its limitations. A mass search organised around a make and colour of vehicle can create many leads without identifying the correct one. Witness memories may be broadly accurate yet wrong in detail, while an innocent owner can resemble a description or travel through the area at the wrong time.

Police reopened and reviewed the case decades later, including a major reinvestigation beginning in 2004, but it produced no breakthrough. The likely explanation remains abduction and murder, although the absence of remains, forensic evidence or a conclusively identified vehicle prevents a complete reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/mona-blades-vanishes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mona Blades vanishesEighteen-year-old Mona Blades was last seen sitting in the back seat of an orange Datsun station wagon. Her...</span></span></span>

Mona’s disappearance also captures a particular period in New Zealand social history. Hitchhiking was common, long-distance roads could be sparsely travelled, and there were no mobile-phone records, automatic number-plate cameras or widespread roadside surveillance. A brief encounter on an isolated road could leave almost no permanent trace.

## Kirsa Jensen: the mystery beside the river

Fourteen-year-old Kirsa Jensen disappeared near Napier on 1 September 1983 after going out to ride her horse, Commodore. The horse was later found without her near the Tutaekuri River. Kirsa was not found, and the case became one of New Zealand’s most prominent missing-child investigations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/page/1983-key-events" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1983 key events</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1983 key events</span></span></span>

Witness accounts placed her near the river and reportedly described interactions with a man, but the available information did not lead to a prosecution. Police came to believe that she had been abducted and killed. Searches and later appeals failed to locate her remains or establish a definitive sequence of events.

As in the Mona Blades case, geography mattered. Riverbanks, farmland, roads and the nearby coast offered numerous places where evidence could be dispersed or concealed. Investigators were also working before modern digital records and routine DNA profiling transformed missing-person enquiries.

Kirsa’s case has attracted psychic claims and repeated rumours, but these have not supplied verifiable evidence. Its genuine mystery lies in the narrow interval between an ordinary afternoon ride and a disappearance for which no physical explanation has ever been recovered.

## The General Grant: a wreck, a cavern and missing gold

The *[General Grant]({{ 'general-grant/' | relative_url }})* sailed from Melbourne for London in May 1866 carrying passengers, wool, hides and a documented shipment of 2,576 ounces of gold. Near the Auckland Islands, hundreds of kilometres south of mainland New Zealand, the ship was becalmed and driven towards the cliffs. Accounts describe it entering or striking a coastal cavern before sinking.

Of the 83 people aboard, only 15 reached land. Ten were eventually rescued after about 18 months; others died during the wreck, on the islands or while attempting to seek help. The survival story alone made the disaster famous, but the unrecovered gold turned it into a treasure mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.doc.govt.nz/Documents/science-and-technical/has1entire.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doc.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doc.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Grafton wreck and Epigwaitt hut site, Auckland Islands</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Grafton wreck and Epigwaitt hut site, Auckland Islands</span></span></span>

The documented bullion is only part of the legend. Successful miners travelling as passengers may have carried private gold that was not fully listed. Later treasure stories also suggested that cargo recorded as zinc or “spelter” concealed much larger quantities of precious metal. Evidence for these expanded fortunes is much weaker than the ship’s formal manifest and insurance records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://maanz.org.nz/projects/gengrant.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: maanz.org.nz">[maanz.org.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">maanz.org.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on maanz.org.nz.</span></span></span>

Searchers have repeatedly tried to locate the wreck, but the western coast of Auckland Island is exceptionally difficult. Heavy seas, strong winds, poor underwater visibility, cliffs and numerous caves complicate both historical identification and modern diving. Even if wreck material is detected, proving that it belongs to the *General Grant* rather than another vessel is a separate problem.

The most plausible explanation is straightforward: the ship lies broken, buried or scattered somewhere near the coast described by survivors. Whether a recoverable treasure remains is much less certain. Gold could be trapped beneath collapsed rock, spread across the seabed, hidden in an inaccessible cavern or already removed without public record. The wreck is therefore a genuine archaeological problem surrounded by a much larger layer of treasure-hunting folklore.

## Was William Sutch a Soviet spy?

William Ball Sutch was a prominent economist, writer and former senior public servant. In September 1974, New Zealand Security Intelligence Service officers observed him meeting Soviet diplomat Dimitri Razgovorov in Wellington. Sutch was arrested and accused under the Official Secrets Act of passing information to the Soviet Union.

The prosecution faced a fundamental difficulty: it could not clearly identify the secret information allegedly supplied. Its case concentrated on the clandestine-looking meetings and Sutch’s initial denial that he knew Razgovorov. The defence argued that the meetings had innocent explanations and that Sutch’s evasiveness resulted from embarrassment or confusion. A jury acquitted him in February 1975.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nzsis.govt.nz/news/release-of-selected-sutch-papers-and-sir-guy-powles-top-secret-report-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzsis.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzsis.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">release of selected sutch papers and sir guy powles top secret report 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">release of selected sutch papers and sir guy powles top secret report 2</span></span></span>

Acquittal meant the Crown had not proved the charge beyond reasonable doubt; it did not settle every historical question about Sutch’s contacts. Declassified intelligence papers and later material attributed to Soviet archives have encouraged the view that he had maintained a long relationship with Soviet intelligence. Supporters of Sutch have replied that intelligence files can contain exaggeration, mistaken identification and claims written by agents seeking to impress their superiors.

The released New Zealand material also raised questions about the conduct of the Security Intelligence Service itself. A report by Chief Ombudsman Sir Guy Powles found that some surveillance actions, including burglary and bugging, had been unlawful. This complicates attempts to treat the intelligence record as a neutral account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nzsis.govt.nz/news/release-of-selected-sutch-papers-and-sir-guy-powles-top-secret-report-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzsis.govt.nz">[nzsis.govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzsis.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">release of selected sutch papers and sir guy powles top secret report 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">release of selected sutch papers and sir guy powles top secret report 2</span></span></span>

The fairest assessment separates several propositions. Sutch held secretive meetings with a Soviet official and was not candid about them. There is later evidence consistent with a longer intelligence relationship. Yet the 1975 prosecution did not prove that he passed specified official secrets, and the surviving public record does not provide an uncontested account of what, if anything, he delivered. The mystery persists because “contact”, “source”, “agent” and “spy” are not automatically interchangeable terms.

## Erebus: disputed responsibility, not an unknown cause

On 28 November 1979, Air New Zealand Flight 901 struck Mount Erebus during a sightseeing flight over Antarctica, killing all 257 people aboard. The disaster is sometimes described as a mystery, but the aircraft’s impact and immediate physical circumstances are well documented. The lasting dispute concerned responsibility and the institutional handling of evidence.

The first official investigation, led by Chief Inspector of Air Accidents Ron Chippindale, placed substantial emphasis on decisions made by the flight crew. Justice Peter Mahon’s later Royal Commission reached a sharply different conclusion. Mahon found that a navigational track had been altered without the crew being properly informed and that the pilots were affected by a whiteout, in which cloud, snow and diffuse light destroy visible contrast. He cleared the crew of responsibility and placed blame on Air New Zealand’s systems and actions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/media/photo/erebus-inquiry-peter-mahon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[govt.nz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">erebus inquiry peter mahon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">erebus inquiry peter mahon</span></span></span>

Mahon also accused the airline of presenting what he famously characterised as an organised pattern of deception. Air New Zealand challenged his findings. Subsequent judicial proceedings criticised aspects of the process by which Mahon reached his strongest conclusions, particularly findings affecting people who had not been given a sufficient opportunity to respond. Those rulings did not restore a simple pilot-error explanation or erase the navigational and organisational failures identified by the inquiry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://teara.govt.nz/en/zoomify/6559/the-mahon-report-controversy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: teara.govt.nz">[Te Ara Encyclopedia of New Zealand]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">teara.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govt.nz.</span></span></span>

Erebus remains an important example of how a disaster can be technically explained while its public meaning remains contested. The central questions are no longer whether the aircraft hit the mountain or whether the route data had changed. They concern how blame was assigned, what the airline disclosed, and why the flight crew initially carried so much of the burden in public memory.

## The Kaimanawa “wall”: mystery after explanation

In the Kaimanawa State Forest is a rock exposure divided into large, roughly rectangular sections. Photographs can make it resemble carefully fitted masonry. Since the 1990s, some writers have claimed it is the remnant of a structure built by an unknown civilisation predating Polynesian settlement.

Geological interpretation offers a far less dramatic answer. The apparent “blocks” are natural fractures in volcanic rock, formed as it cooled more than 300,000 years ago. Te Ara, New Zealand’s national encyclopedia, identifies the feature as a natural formation rather than an archaeological wall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://teara.govt.nz/en/document/1603/the-kaimanawa-wall" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: teara.govt.nz">[Te Ara Encyclopedia of New Zealand]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">teara.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govt.nz.</span></span></span>

The wider pre-Māori civilisation theory also conflicts with archaeological, linguistic and genetic evidence. Excavations, including work at Wairau Bar, established that the earliest known settlers were Polynesians and that the “moa hunters” once imagined as a separate people represented an early phase of Māori culture. Current evidence places sustained Polynesian settlement in the late 13th century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://teara.govt.nz/en/ideas-about-maori-origins/page-5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: teara.govt.nz">[Te Ara Encyclopedia of New Zealand]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">teara.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">page 5</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">page 5</span></span></span>

Why, then, does the claim persist? The formation looks artificial in selected photographs; the site has not been excavated in the manner proponents demand; and the theory offers the appealing possibility of overturning accepted history. It also draws strength from older colonial-era stories that wrongly treated Moriori as a primitive population displaced from mainland New Zealand by later Māori arrivals.

The Kaimanawa case is valuable precisely because it shows the difference between an unexplained object and a disputed explanation. People may continue to distrust the geological account, but visual resemblance alone is not archaeological evidence. A human-built structure would require signs such as shaped surfaces, construction debris, cultural material, occupation layers or a credible dating sequence. None has established the existence of a lost pre-Māori civilisation at the site.

## The Howard mystery and a severed hand

In October 1885, a boy found a neatly folded set of men’s clothes on the beach at Sumner near Christchurch. The articles appeared to belong to a man named Arthur Howard, creating the impression that he had drowned. Later, a human hand was found at nearby Taylor’s Mistake, apparently reinforcing the story.

The episode became a public sensation, but it eventually emerged as an attempted insurance fraud rather than an unexplained death. Howard was alive, and evidence indicated that the disappearance had been staged. The hand, obtained to make the supposed drowning more convincing, turned the affair into one of colonial New Zealand’s strangest criminal deceptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://teara.govt.nz/en/community-contribution/7206/the-howard-mystery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: teara.govt.nz">[Te Ara Encyclopedia of New Zealand]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">teara.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the howard mystery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the howard mystery</span></span></span>

Its remaining uncertainties concern details rather than Howard’s survival: the precise origin of the hand, how the participants organised the fraud and the degree of knowledge held by each person involved. The case demonstrates how an apparently powerful physical clue can be manufactured to control a narrative. A severed hand seemed to confirm death because observers naturally connected it to the abandoned clothes. Once that assumption failed, the same evidence pointed towards deliberate staging.

## How New Zealand’s mysteries enter public memory

These cases survive for different reasons, but several patterns recur.

**Missing bodies leave stories open.** Mona Blades, Kirsa Jensen and the victims or evidence sought in other disappearance cases cannot be placed into a complete forensic sequence. Without remains, investigators may lack a cause of death, location, trace evidence and a reliable timeline.

**Remote landscapes protect uncertainty.** The Auckland Islands, river systems, rural highways and exposed coastlines can destroy, move or conceal evidence. New Zealand’s geography is not merely a dramatic backdrop; it has materially shaped what can be recovered.

**Institutional failure can become part of the mystery.** In the Crewe case, planted evidence helped secure a wrongful conviction. In the Sutch affair, unlawful intelligence methods complicated the official record. After Erebus, competing investigations produced radically different accounts of responsibility.

**A memorable clue can dominate beyond its value.** The orange Datsun, the *General Grant*’s gold, the Crewe cartridge case and the Kaimanawa “blocks” are powerful images. Each can narrow public attention so strongly that uncertainty surrounding the clue itself is forgotten.

**Later retellings often increase certainty rather than knowledge.** Documentaries, books, tourism stories and online discussions may turn possibilities into claims: an alleged cargo becomes an enormous fortune, an intelligence contact becomes definitive proof of espionage, or natural rock becomes suppressed archaeology. Repetition can make a theory familiar without making it better supported.

## What is genuinely unresolved?

The strongest New Zealand historical mysteries are those in which a clear evidential gap remains after serious investigation.

The killer or killers of Harvey and Jeannette Crewe have not been identified, even though Arthur Allan Thomas’s conviction was discredited. Mona Blades and Kirsa Jensen remain missing, with no publicly established account of their final moments. The wreck of the *General Grant* has not been conclusively located, and the fate of its recoverable gold is unknown. William Sutch’s exact relationship with Soviet intelligence remains disputed because the evidence supports suspicion more strongly than it supplies a complete, testable narrative.

Other puzzles are better described as controversies or myths. Erebus is not an unexplained crash, although the struggle over blame and disclosure is historically significant. The Kaimanawa formation is visually striking but has a credible geological explanation. The Howard mystery began as an apparent death but became an exposed fraud.

That distinction does not make the explained cases uninteresting. It reveals something more important: a national mystery may endure not because evidence is absent, but because evidence, institutional trust and public memory do not always move together.
