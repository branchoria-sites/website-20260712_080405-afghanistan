## Introduction

Betty Stiven’s tomb is one of Tobago’s most enduring historical riddles because the mystery lies not in who was buried there, but in a single baffling sentence carved into her memorial. The eighteenth-century tomb, near Fort James in Plymouth, commemorates a young woman who died on 25 November 1783 together with her child. Its inscription describes her as “a mother without knowing it, and a wife without letting her husband know it”, a phrase that has puzzled visitors for more than two centuries. Unlike many famous historical mysteries, there is no lost treasure or missing body to discover. Instead, the puzzle survives because the family story that once explained the words appears to have vanished, leaving historians to weigh fragmentary records against generations of local folklore. The result is an instructive example of how missing evidence can transform a private tragedy into a national historical enigma.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com/2020/11/tombstones-of-tobago.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com">[caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Caribbean History Archives: Tombstones of Tobago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 18, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 2020</span></span></span>

## What the surviving inscription says

The memorial stands in Plymouth, close to Fort James, and dates from 1783. Although the original stone deteriorated and was later replaced, the unusual wording has been carefully preserved in modern reproductions. The key passage states:

> “She was a mother without knowing it, and a wife without letting her husband know it, except by her kind indulgences to him.”

The rest of the inscription identifies the deceased as Mrs Betty Stiven, records that she died aged just twenty-three, and notes that she was buried with her child while her husband, Alex (or Alexander) Stiven, would mourn her for the rest of his life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com/2020/11/tombstones-of-tobago.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com">[blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Caribbean History Archives: Tombstones of Tobago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 18, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 2020</span></span></span>

The riddle is unusual because the first part appears almost self-explanatory—many readers assume she died during childbirth before regaining consciousness—while the second part seems grammatically and emotionally perplexing. How could someone be "a wife" without her husband knowing it?

## Why the inscription became a mystery

No contemporary letter, diary or family account explaining the epitaph has been found. That absence is the real mystery.

The wording itself is not impossible to understand, but it lacks the context that eighteenth-century relatives would almost certainly have possessed. If the inscription referred to a private family circumstance, a local custom or an event everyone involved recognised, no further explanation would have seemed necessary in 1783. Two centuries later, however, the supporting story has disappeared while the stone has survived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com/2020/11/tombstones-of-tobago.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com">[caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Caribbean History Archives: Tombstones of Tobago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 18, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 2020</span></span></span>

As a result, historians are left with only three secure facts:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Betty Stiven died in November 1783 at about twenty-three years of age.
* A child was buried with her.
* The inscription deliberately emphasised an unusual aspect of her marriage and motherhood.

</div>

Everything beyond those points depends on interpretation rather than documentary proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com/2020/11/tombstones-of-tobago.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com">[caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Caribbean History Archives: Tombstones of Tobago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 18, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 2020</span></span></span>

## Theories about motherhood, marriage and identity

Several explanations have been proposed over the years, but none can be demonstrated conclusively.

The most straightforward interpretation is medical. Betty may have become unconscious during labour, died before awakening and therefore never knew her child had been born. That would explain the phrase "a mother without knowing it" and fits the grim reality of eighteenth-century childbirth, when maternal mortality remained high. This explanation is commonly repeated by local guides and many visitors because it requires the fewest assumptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.com/Attraction_Review-g1219455-d9731566-Reviews-Mystery_Tombstone-Plymouth_Tobago_Trinidad_and_Tobago.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.com">[Tripadvisor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mystery Tombstone, Plymouth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mystery Tombstone, Plymouth...</span></span></span>

The second half of the inscription has generated more debate.

One interpretation is that the phrase refers to the emotional rather than legal nature of marriage. The wording "without letting her husband know it, except by her kind indulgences to him" may have been intended as an elaborate compliment to Betty's modesty, suggesting that she demonstrated her devotion through actions rather than declarations. Modern readers often find this construction awkward, but eighteenth-century epitaphs frequently employed highly sentimental and rhetorical language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com/2020/11/tombstones-of-tobago.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com">[caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Caribbean History Archives: Tombstones of Tobago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 18, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 2020</span></span></span>

Another theory centres on Betty's identity. Caribbean historian Gerard Besson drew attention to an Anglican register recording three mixed-race ("mulatto" in the terminology of the period) children of Alexander Stivens in 1781, while noting that surviving church records contain no marriage entry for Alexander and Betty. He suggested that Betty may have been an enslaved or formerly enslaved woman whom Alexander regarded privately as his wife, even if colonial law or social convention did not. In that reading, the epitaph represents an unusually public acknowledgement of a relationship that society had refused to recognise. The surviving records, however, are incomplete and cannot confirm this interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com/2011/08/tobago.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com">[caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Caribbean History Archives: Tobago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Caribbean History Archives: Tobago</span></span></span>

## How folklore filled gaps in the historical record

Because documentary evidence is so limited, folklore gradually expanded to fill the silence.

Stories developed claiming that Betty secretly bore several children, that she and Alexander were involved in a forbidden interracial relationship concealed from colonial society, or that extraordinary circumstances surrounded both the marriage and childbirth. Some versions even contradict one another while sharing no documentary support.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/49250475%40N06/4512582910" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Plymouth Tobago, Mystery Tomb, Read below this picture. &#124; Flickr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Plymouth Tobago, Mystery Tomb, Read below this picture. &#124; FlickrApril 11, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2010</span></span></span>

These tales illustrate a common pattern in historical memory. Once a puzzling inscription survives without its original explanation, later generations naturally attempt to reconstruct a narrative that makes emotional sense. The stories often reveal more about changing attitudes towards slavery, race and colonial society than they do about Betty herself.

Tourism has also reinforced the legend. The grave is widely promoted as Tobago's "Mystery Tombstone", encouraging visitors to devise their own solution to the riddle. That popular appeal has helped preserve the monument, but it has also blurred the distinction between documented history and imaginative reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tobagobeyond.com/local-culture-people-heritage/history-culture/historical-sites-and-attractions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tobagobeyond.com">[tobagobeyond.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tobagobeyond.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Tobago Historical Sites and Attractions &#124; Tobago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Tobago Historical Sites and Attractions &#124; Tobago</span></span></span>

## What the evidence actually supports

The strongest evidence does not solve Betty Stiven's story; instead, it narrows the possibilities.

The inscription almost certainly reflects a real circumstance known to those who commissioned it. The reference to motherhood fits the burial of Betty and her child and is consistent with death during or immediately after childbirth. Beyond that, certainty disappears. There is no surviving document explaining precisely what was meant by "a wife without letting her husband know it", nor is there conclusive evidence proving later theories about racial identity, legal marriage or concealed family relationships.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com/2011/08/tobago.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com">[caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caribbeanhistoryarchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Caribbean History Archives: Tobago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Caribbean History Archives: Tobago</span></span></span>

For that reason, Betty Stiven's tomb remains a genuine historical puzzle rather than a supernatural mystery. Its enduring fascination comes from the survival of an enigmatic epitaph after the ordinary family knowledge needed to interpret it was lost. The stone preserves the words, but not the conversation that once made those words perfectly clear.
