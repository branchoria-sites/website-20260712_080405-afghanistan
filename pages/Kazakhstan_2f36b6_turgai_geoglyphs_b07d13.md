## Introduction

The Turgai geoglyphs are among [Kazakhstan]({{ 'kazakhstan/' | relative_url }})'s most intriguing archaeological mysteries because they are undeniably human-made yet their purpose remains unknown. Scattered across the Turgai region of northern Kazakhstan, these enormous geometric earthworks—crosses, rings, squares and other patterns—are so large that they are most easily recognised from satellite imagery rather than from the ground. Although their existence only became widely known in the twenty-first century, archaeological investigation has shown that at least some of them are ancient rather than modern constructions. The central mystery is not whether people built them, but why. Were they ritual monuments, territorial markers, gathering places, astronomical alignments or something else entirely? Despite growing archaeological research, no single explanation has won broad acceptance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352226715300052" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## How the earthworks were discovered

The Turgai geoglyphs entered public awareness in 2007 after Kazakh enthusiast Dmitriy Dey noticed unusual geometric patterns while examining Google Earth imagery. Subsequent surveys identified dozens of sites spread across the Turgai Depression, a broad corridor of steppe in northern Kazakhstan. What initially appeared to be isolated curiosities turned out to form a wider landscape of monumental earthworks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352226715300052" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Archaeologists later confirmed that many of the features were genuine ancient constructions rather than natural formations or recent agricultural works. Today more than sixty possible geoglyphs have been reported, although only a proportion have been examined directly in the field. The best-known examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Ushtogay Square**, a square of earthen mounds roughly 270 metres across.
* **Turgai Swastika**, a hooked cross whose shape predates the twentieth-century political symbol by many centuries.
* Numerous circles, crosses and linear arrangements formed from low mounds or embankments.

</div>

Unlike Peru's Nazca Lines, these are "positive" geoglyphs, built by piling up earth rather than scraping away the ground to expose lighter soil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352226715300052" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## Dating the different structures

One reason the mystery has endured is that the geoglyphs are unlikely to have been built at exactly the same time. They are spread across a large region, vary considerably in design and appear to have different construction histories.

The first systematic dating came through optically stimulated luminescence (OSL), a scientific method that estimates when buried sediments were last exposed to sunlight. Results published in 2016 suggested that at least the earliest phases of some monuments belong to the Early Iron Age, broadly around the first millennium BC. However, not every geoglyph has been dated, and some may have been modified or rebuilt long after their initial construction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352226715300052" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

This uncertainty matters because different periods imply different builders. An Early Iron Age date points towards steppe societies contemporary with the Saka and related nomadic cultures, while later modifications could reflect changing uses over centuries. The evidence therefore argues against treating every geoglyph as part of a single coordinated project.

## Why were they built?

The absence of written records or obvious artefacts directly associated with the earthworks means that archaeologists must infer their purpose from their design, location and surrounding landscape. Several explanations compete, each solving part of the puzzle while leaving important questions unanswered.

### Ritual and ceremonial monuments

Many researchers regard ceremonial use as the strongest possibility. Monumental construction requires organised labour, suggesting that these sites held social or symbolic importance beyond everyday practical needs.

The geometric layouts may have served as gathering places where dispersed pastoral communities met for seasonal ceremonies, political assemblies or religious rituals. Their large scale would also have made them visible across open steppe landscapes, reinforcing their role as landmarks of shared identity.

The weakness of this explanation is the limited archaeological evidence. Excavations have not uncovered the rich ritual deposits, burials or offerings that would conclusively identify the sites as ceremonial centres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352226715300052" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

### Markers on ancient routes

Another proposal links the geoglyphs to movement across the Eurasian steppe. Northern Kazakhstan has long been crossed by migration paths, seasonal grazing routes and later trade networks.

Because the monuments occupy elevated or strategically visible locations, they may have marked significant places within these routes, signalling meeting points, territorial boundaries or access to important resources such as water or pasture.

This theory fits the landscape but remains difficult to prove. No direct evidence connects the earthworks to a documented transport network from the relevant period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352226715300052" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

### Astronomical alignments

Some investigators have argued that particular geoglyphs may have been aligned with the rising Sun during key points in the year, functioning as horizon markers or observational aids rather than true observatories.

Such ideas gained public attention because other ancient cultures built monuments with astronomical orientations. However, specialists remain cautious. Demonstrating intentional celestial alignment requires consistent patterns across many sites, and the available evidence is still limited. At present, astronomy remains an interesting possibility rather than an established explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.education.nationalgeographic.org/2015/11/02/ancient-geoglyphs-discovered-in-kazakhstan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.education.nationalgeographic.org">[National Geographic Education Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.education.nationalgeographic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalgeographic.org.</span></span></span>

### Territorial symbols

The earthworks may also have expressed ownership or identity.

Building monumental geometric figures across the landscape could have advertised the presence of powerful groups, reinforced claims over grazing territories or commemorated alliances between communities. Similar large-scale landscape monuments elsewhere in Eurasia often combined practical, political and symbolic roles rather than serving a single function.

Again, the challenge is the absence of inscriptions or associated settlements that would identify the people responsible.

## What the evidence does not support

The mystery surrounding the Turgai geoglyphs has encouraged speculation, especially online. Several popular ideas have little archaeological support.

There is no credible evidence that the geoglyphs were created by extraterrestrials or represent messages intended for aircraft. Their builders could easily have planned large geometric designs using simple surveying methods without needing to see the finished pattern from high above.

Likewise, archaeologists have not found convincing evidence that the monuments were primarily burial mounds. Unlike many steppe kurgans, the investigated geoglyphs have generally not produced rich graves or funerary assemblages that would identify them as cemeteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.education.nationalgeographic.org/2015/11/02/ancient-geoglyphs-discovered-in-kazakhstan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.education.nationalgeographic.org">[National Geographic Education Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.education.nationalgeographic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalgeographic.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the purpose remains unresolved

Several factors continue to frustrate investigation.

First, only a minority of the known geoglyphs have been excavated in detail. Many remain documented mainly through satellite imagery.

Second, centuries of erosion, agriculture and natural weathering have altered the monuments. Low earth mounds are especially vulnerable to gradual flattening, making it difficult to reconstruct their original appearance.

Third, the builders left no known written explanation. Unlike ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia, the Iron Age steppe cultures responsible for these monuments did not leave inscriptions describing why they were created.

Finally, there may never have been a single purpose. The monuments differ in form, scale and apparent date, making it plausible that they served different functions across different communities and generations rather than belonging to one unified tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352226715300052" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OSL dates for the ancient geometric earthworks of Kazakhstan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## Why the Turgai geoglyphs matter

The Turgai geoglyphs demonstrate that the Eurasian steppe supported forms of monumental landscape architecture that remained almost invisible until satellite imagery transformed archaeological discovery. They challenge the assumption that large ceremonial earthworks belonged only to better-known civilisations and highlight how much of Kazakhstan's prehistoric landscape is still poorly understood.

Rather than representing a solved archaeological puzzle, they illustrate how new technology can reveal entirely new classes of ancient monuments while leaving their meaning unresolved.
