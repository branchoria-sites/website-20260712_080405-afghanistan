## Introduction

The graves at Anse Lascars on Silhouette Island are among the most frequently cited pieces of evidence in claims that people from the Arab world reached and even settled the [Seychelles]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-seychelles/' | relative_url }}) before Europeans. For many years, local tradition identified the burials as those of Arab sailors, and the beach itself took its name from that belief. Yet modern archaeological investigation has complicated the story. The strongest available evidence suggests that the individuals buried there probably died around the beginning of the nineteenth century, after European settlement had begun, rather than centuries earlier. The mystery therefore is not whether the graves exist—they do—but whether they record a forgotten pre-European community or simply an early chapter of the colonial era.

The debate remains important because it touches a larger historical question. Did Arab and Swahili sailors merely know of the Seychelles and occasionally visit the islands, or did they establish a lasting settlement before Europeans arrived?

## Who Was Buried at Anse Lascars?

Anse Lascars lies on the eastern side of Silhouette Island, away from the main settlement at La Passe. For generations, visitors were shown a group of old graves said to belong to Arab mariners. The name "Lascars" itself encouraged this interpretation, as the term became associated locally with Arabs or sailors from the western Indian Ocean. The graves became one of the island's most enduring historical curiosities and were repeatedly mentioned in travel accounts and local histories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5799/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Silhouette Island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSilhouette Island - UNESCO World Heritage CentreFebruary 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2013</span></span></span>

The mystery gained wider attention because the Seychelles had no indigenous population when Europeans began colonising the islands during the eighteenth century. If the burials genuinely pre-dated European settlement, they would represent rare physical evidence that people from the wider Indian Ocean had lived, even temporarily, on the islands before the French established a permanent colony.

For many years, however, the attribution rested largely on tradition rather than archaeological proof. No contemporary written record identifies the people buried there, and no inscriptions or grave markers securely date the burials.

## Arab and Swahili Navigation in the Indian Ocean

The idea that Arab sailors reached the Seychelles long before Europeans is not inherently implausible. By the medieval period, merchants from the Arabian Peninsula and the Swahili coast maintained extensive trading networks across the western Indian Ocean. Seasonal monsoon winds allowed experienced navigators to travel between East Africa, Arabia, India and beyond with remarkable regularity.

Historical evidence indicates that these sailors almost certainly knew of islands in the western Indian Ocean, even if surviving written descriptions are limited. Portuguese navigator Thomé Lopes recorded sighting what is widely believed to have been Silhouette Island during Vasco da Gama's 1503 voyage, demonstrating that the islands were already on major sailing routes before permanent European settlement. Conservation and heritage organisations likewise note that Silhouette was probably familiar to Arab navigators before the first documented European landings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.islandconservationseychelles.com/silhouette.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandconservationseychelles.com">[islandconservationseychelles.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandconservationseychelles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Island Conservation Society...</span></span></span>

Knowledge of an island, however, is not the same as establishing a settlement. Ships commonly stopped to obtain fresh water, timber or shelter without creating permanent communities. Throughout the Indian Ocean there are many examples of islands used as temporary anchorages that left little archaeological trace.

This distinction is central to the Silhouette debate. Evidence of occasional visits would fit well with known Indian Ocean navigation. Evidence of a resident pre-European population would require a much stronger archaeological record.

## Why the Dating Changed the Debate

The strongest challenge to the traditional interpretation came when human remains recovered from the Anse Lascars graves were examined scientifically. Rather than indicating medieval or early Islamic burials, the available dating placed the remains at approximately 1800, close to the period when Europeans had already begun settling the Seychelles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.islandconservationseychelles.com/silhouette.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandconservationseychelles.com">[islandconservationseychelles.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandconservationseychelles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Island Conservation Society...</span></span></span>

That result significantly weakens the claim that the graves belong to an unknown Arab colony. Instead, several more ordinary explanations become possible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* They may represent early colonial-era workers or sailors.
* The deceased could still have originated from the wider Indian Ocean, including Arab or Swahili communities, without implying that they settled the islands before Europeans.
* They may have been victims of shipwreck or maritime disease during the early colonial period.
* They could have been connected with the mixed labour force that accompanied the first decades of French settlement.

</div>

Importantly, the dating does not prove who the individuals were. It simply narrows the likely period in which they lived, removing one of the strongest arguments for a much earlier occupation.

## What the Archaeological Record Can—and Cannot—Show

The archaeological evidence for pre-European settlement in the Seychelles remains remarkably sparse. Unlike islands that preserve extensive ruins, pottery, harbour works or cemeteries from earlier civilisations, the Seychelles have produced no widely accepted body of material evidence demonstrating a permanent Arab or Swahili colony before the eighteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seychelles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

That absence should be interpreted carefully.

Several factors complicate the picture:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Tropical environments rapidly destroy organic materials.
* Small seasonal camps may leave almost no detectable remains.
* Later settlement and natural erosion can disturb earlier sites.
* Many islands have received only limited archaeological investigation compared with better-known Indian Ocean trading centres.

</div>

Even so, archaeologists generally expect a permanent settlement to leave multiple forms of evidence rather than a single disputed cemetery. The Anse Lascars graves therefore carry considerable symbolic importance precisely because there are so few comparable finds.

## Why the Mystery Persists

The story survives because it sits between two positions that are both partly reasonable.

One view holds that Arab and Swahili navigators almost certainly reached the Seychelles before Europeans, making it tempting to interpret the graves as surviving evidence of that earlier presence.

The other emphasises that historical claims require archaeological support. Since the available dating points towards about 1800 and no broader settlement has been identified, historians generally regard the graves as more consistent with the early colonial period than with a forgotten medieval community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.islandconservationseychelles.com/silhouette.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandconservationseychelles.com">[islandconservationseychelles.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandconservationseychelles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Island Conservation Society...</span></span></span>

The disagreement therefore concerns interpretation rather than the existence of the graves themselves. The burials are genuine; what remains uncertain is the identity and life stories of those buried there.

## What the Graves Tell Us Today

The Anse Lascars graves remain an important reminder that the western Indian Ocean was connected by maritime networks long before European empires expanded into the region. They encourage questions about navigation, migration and cultural contact that extend beyond the Seychelles alone.

At present, however, the balance of evidence does not support using the graves as proof of a permanent pre-European Arab settlement on Silhouette Island. Instead, they illustrate the limits of the archaeological record: a handful of burials can inspire enduring historical speculation, but without supporting artefacts, written records or securely dated settlement remains, the larger claim remains unproven.

Rather than resolving the question of who first reached the Seychelles, the graves highlight a more nuanced conclusion. Early sailors almost certainly knew of the islands and may have landed there repeatedly, yet the available evidence still points to permanent occupation beginning only with European colonisation in the late eighteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5799/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Silhouette Island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreSilhouette Island - UNESCO World Heritage CentreFebruary 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2013</span></span></span>
