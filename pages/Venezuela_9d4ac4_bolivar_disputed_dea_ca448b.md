## Introduction

What really killed Simón Bolívar remains one of the most enduring medical mysteries in Venezuelan history. The traditional answer is tuberculosis, based on the symptoms recorded during his final illness and the autopsy performed after his death on 17 December 1830 at the Quinta de San Pedro Alejandrino near Santa Marta. Yet nineteenth-century medicine could not identify infectious diseases with the precision available today, and later researchers have argued that the historical record also fits other natural illnesses. More recently, speculation about poisoning transformed an old diagnostic debate into a political controversy. The surviving evidence makes one point clear: Bolívar died after a prolonged, debilitating illness affecting his lungs, but no modern laboratory diagnosis can now settle the question beyond doubt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/1/78/405546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth: An Infectious Diseases Conundrum &#124; Clinical Infectious Diseases &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

## What the Illness Recorded in 1830 Actually Shows

The most important evidence comes from Alexandre Prospère Révérend, the French physician who attended Bolívar during the final weeks of his life. His detailed observations describe a man who had become progressively weaker over many months. Bolívar suffered persistent coughing, fever, severe weight loss, exhaustion and difficulty carrying out even basic activities. When he died at the age of forty-seven, the autopsy described lungs showing extensive chronic disease.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/1/78/405546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth: An Infectious Diseases Conundrum &#124; Clinical Infectious Diseases &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

For generations these records were accepted as convincing evidence of tuberculosis, then commonly known as consumption. That conclusion was understandable. Tuberculosis was widespread in the nineteenth century and often caused exactly the sort of gradual wasting illness seen in Bolívar.

The difficulty is that Révérend was working decades before the discovery of the bacterium responsible for tuberculosis. He could describe damaged organs but had no microscope capable of identifying the infection, no chest X-rays and no laboratory tests. His report therefore documents the appearance of the disease rather than its exact cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/1/78/405546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth: An Infectious Diseases Conundrum &#124; Clinical Infectious Diseases &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

## Tuberculosis, Fungal Disease and Arsenic

Modern historians and physicians generally agree that tuberculosis remains the leading explanation, but it is no longer the only medically plausible one.

A detailed reassessment published in *Clinical Infectious Diseases* argued that some features of Bolívar's illness do not fit classic end-stage pulmonary tuberculosis. The authors noted the absence of certain expected signs and suggested that another chronic disease could better explain the combination of symptoms and autopsy findings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/1/78/405546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth: An Infectious Diseases Conundrum &#124; Clinical Infectious Diseases &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

They proposed two principal alternatives:

* **Paracoccidioidomycosis**, a fungal infection native to parts of South America. It can remain dormant for years before producing chronic lung disease, weight loss and widespread illness resembling tuberculosis.
* **Bronchiectasis complicated by chronic arsenic exposure**, in which long-term damage to the airways, possibly worsened by repeated arsenic intake, could account for both respiratory decline and other features described in historical accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/1/78/405546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth: An Infectious Diseases Conundrum &#124; Clinical Infectious Diseases &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

The arsenic hypothesis is often misunderstood. It does **not** necessarily imply deliberate poisoning. During Bolívar's lifetime arsenic compounds were widely used in medicine to treat a variety of illnesses, and repeated therapeutic exposure could occur without criminal intent. Researchers therefore distinguish carefully between **arsenic exposure** and **assassination**. The former is historically plausible; the latter requires evidence that has never been produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/1/78/405546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[oup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth: An Infectious Diseases Conundrum &#124; Clinical Infectious Diseases &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

Some physicians continue to defend tuberculosis as the strongest diagnosis, arguing that the combination of wasting, chronic cough and the autopsy findings remains most consistent with the disease despite the limitations of nineteenth-century medicine. Others acknowledge uncertainties but consider the evidence insufficient to replace tuberculosis with another diagnosis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anmdecolombia.org.co/fue-una-meningitis-tuberculosa-terminal-la-causa-de-muerte-de-simon-bolivar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anmdecolombia.org.co">[Anm de Colombia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anmdecolombia.org.co</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia Nacional de Medicina de Colombia...</span></span></span>

## Why the Murder Theory Endures

The medical uncertainty became politically charged nearly two centuries after Bolívar's death.

Beginning in 2008, Venezuelan President Hugo Chávez argued publicly that Bolívar had been murdered by political enemies rather than dying naturally. This led to the exhumation of Bolívar's remains in 2010, accompanied by extensive forensic investigation intended both to confirm the identity of the remains and to examine the possibility of poisoning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2011/jul/26/hugo-chavez-liberation-hero-murdered" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

When the official findings were announced in 2011, investigators reported that they could **not** establish death by intentional poisoning or any other unnatural cause. Historical documents likewise failed to demonstrate murder. Officials acknowledged that exposure to arsenic-containing medicines or contaminated treatments remained possible, but this was very different from proving assassination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eluniversal.com.co/ciencia/2011/07/10/estudio-no-logra-determinar-causa-de-muerte-de-bolivar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eluniversal.com.co">[www.eluniversal.com.co]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eluniversal.com.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">www.eluniversal.com.co Estudio no logra determinar causa de muerte de Bolívar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">no logra determinar causa de muerte de BolívarJuly 10, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 2011</span></span></span>

An important irony is that the medical paper often cited in support of the murder theory did not actually conclude that Bolívar had been assassinated. Its authors proposed alternative natural diseases and discussed chronic arsenic exposure, but they did not argue that he had been deliberately poisoned. Paul Auwaerter, the study's lead author, later clarified publicly that historical medicinal use of arsenic seemed a more likely explanation than homicide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/1/78/405546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth: An Infectious Diseases Conundrum &#124; Clinical Infectious Diseases &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

The persistence of the murder theory owes as much to Bolívar's symbolic status as to the medical evidence. As the central figure in the independence of much of northern South America, debates about his death have become entwined with modern politics, national identity and competing interpretations of history.

## Why the Mystery Cannot Be Fully Solved

Several obstacles prevent a definitive answer.

First, the original medical records describe symptoms but cannot identify microorganisms or toxins with modern precision. Second, the autopsy itself was conducted using the standards of 1830, long before bacteriology transformed medical diagnosis. Third, even if chemical traces are found in preserved remains, they rarely reveal whether exposure occurred through treatment, environment or intentional poisoning, nor can they reliably establish dosage or timing after nearly two centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/1/78/405546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[oup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth: An Infectious Diseases Conundrum &#124; Clinical Infectious Diseases &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

Modern forensic science has therefore narrowed some possibilities while leaving others open. It can rule out certain simplistic explanations, but it cannot recreate the complete clinical picture lost with time.

## The Most Balanced Assessment

The evidence points to a measured conclusion rather than a dramatic revelation.

Bolívar unquestionably died following a prolonged, debilitating respiratory illness. Tuberculosis remains the most widely accepted explanation because it aligns with much of the historical record and the contemporary autopsy. However, the diagnosis was made before modern microbiology, meaning it can never be confirmed to today's medical standard. Alternative natural illnesses—particularly a chronic fungal infection or bronchiectasis associated with arsenic exposure—remain scientifically plausible, though none has been proven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/52/1/78/405546" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[oup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSimon Bolivar&#x27;s Medical Labyrinth: An Infectious Diseases Conundrum &#124; Clinical Infectious Diseases &#124; Oxford AcademicJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

The assassination theory has attracted enormous public attention but lacks convincing supporting evidence. Investigations have failed to demonstrate intentional poisoning, and the presence or possibility of arsenic exposure is compatible with ordinary nineteenth-century medical practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eluniversal.com.co/ciencia/2011/07/10/estudio-no-logra-determinar-causa-de-muerte-de-bolivar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eluniversal.com.co">[www.eluniversal.com.co]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eluniversal.com.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">www.eluniversal.com.co Estudio no logra determinar causa de muerte de Bolívar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">no logra determinar causa de muerte de BolívarJuly 10, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 2011</span></span></span>

That uncertainty explains why Bolívar's death remains one of [Venezuela]({{ 'venezuela/' | relative_url }})'s most discussed historical mysteries. The surviving documents are detailed enough to sustain informed medical debate, yet incomplete enough that no single diagnosis can command universal acceptance nearly two hundred years later.
