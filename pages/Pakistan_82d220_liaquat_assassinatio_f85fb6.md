## Introduction

The assassination of [Pakistan]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-pakistans/' | relative_url }})’s first prime minister, Liaquat Ali Khan, remains one of the country's defining unresolved political mysteries. On 16 October 1951, he was shot while addressing a public meeting at Company Bagh in Rawalpindi, later renamed Liaquat Bagh in his honour. The gunman, Said Akbar, was killed within moments by police, ensuring that the only person known to have fired the fatal shots could never be questioned. Although official inquiries identified him as the assassin, they never established who, if anyone, directed or financed him. The missing chain of command—the unanswered question of who ordered the murder—has kept the case alive in Pakistan's historical memory for more than seven decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk/items/00188d64-66d3-4e3c-b57b-328282b1b30f/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk">[digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Assassination of Mr. Liaquat Ali Khan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Assassination of Mr. Liaquat Ali Khan</span></span></span>

## What happened at Liaquat Bagh?

The public meeting was intended to be an ordinary political gathering. As Liaquat Ali Khan began his speech, Said Akbar fired two shots from close range. The prime minister collapsed and later died from his wounds. Almost immediately, police officers shot and killed Akbar before he could be arrested or interrogated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk/items/00188d64-66d3-4e3c-b57b-328282b1b30f/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk">[digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Assassination of Mr. Liaquat Ali Khan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Assassination of Mr. Liaquat Ali Khan</span></span></span>

That immediate killing transformed the assassination from a criminal investigation into a lasting historical puzzle. In many political assassinations, investigators reconstruct motives and networks through interviews with the accused. Here, that opportunity disappeared within seconds. From the beginning, investigators had to rely on witness statements, documents and circumstantial evidence rather than testimony from the assassin himself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk/items/00188d64-66d3-4e3c-b57b-328282b1b30f/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk">[digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Assassination of Mr. Liaquat Ali Khan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Assassination of Mr. Liaquat Ali Khan</span></span></span>

The government quickly appointed a Commission of Inquiry under Justice Mohammad Munir. The commission held dozens of hearings, examined many witnesses and reviewed the available evidence. Yet despite this extensive effort, it never produced a definitive explanation for who organised the attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nihcr.edu.pk/Latest_English_Journal/4.%20THE%20ASSASSINATION.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nihcr.edu.pk">[nihcr.edu.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nihcr.edu.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Generated by Foxit PDF Creator © Foxit Software</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Generated by Foxit PDF Creator © Foxit Software</span></span></span>

## Why the investigation never answered the central question

The greatest weakness of the investigation was that its principal suspect was dead before questioning could begin. That single fact left investigators trying to infer motives from fragments of evidence rather than direct testimony.

Other problems compounded the difficulty:

* Important leads depended on intelligence records that were incomplete or disputed.
* Witness testimony was sometimes contradictory.
* Pakistan's young state institutions had little experience handling a crime of this political magnitude.
* Some sections of the inquiry report were withheld or removed before publication on grounds of national security, preventing the public from seeing the commission's complete reasoning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nihcr.edu.pk/Latest_English_Journal/4.%20THE%20ASSASSINATION.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nihcr.edu.pk">[nihcr.edu.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nihcr.edu.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Generated by Foxit PDF Creator © Foxit Software</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Generated by Foxit PDF Creator © Foxit Software</span></span></span>

The result was an investigation that documented many facts about the shooting itself but failed to identify a proven organising network behind it.

## The rival theories and their weaknesses

Because no conclusive chain of command was established, numerous explanations emerged. None has been supported by evidence strong enough to achieve broad historical acceptance.

### Said Akbar acted alone

The simplest explanation is that Said Akbar independently decided to kill the prime minister.

This theory has the advantage of requiring no hidden conspiracy. However, the official inquiry itself found little support for the idea that the murder resulted merely from insanity or isolated fanaticism. Instead, commissioners regarded several aspects of the planning and circumstances as difficult to reconcile with a completely spontaneous attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nihcr.edu.pk/Latest_English_Journal/4.%20THE%20ASSASSINATION.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nihcr.edu.pk">[nihcr.edu.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nihcr.edu.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Generated by Foxit PDF Creator © Foxit Software</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Generated by Foxit PDF Creator © Foxit Software</span></span></span>

### Domestic political conspiracy

Another long-standing theory suggests that influential political or bureaucratic figures wished to remove Liaquat Ali Khan because of disagreements over the direction of the new state.

This idea gained attention partly because Pakistan was already experiencing severe political tensions. Earlier in 1951 the government had uncovered the Rawalpindi Conspiracy, an alleged coup plot involving military officers and civilians. Nevertheless, no reliable evidence has directly connected that conspiracy to the assassination itself. Historians generally treat the overlap in timing as suggestive rather than demonstrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journal.psc.edu.pk/index.php/pp/article/view/342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journal.psc.edu.pk">[journal.psc.edu.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journal.psc.edu.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 18, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 2020</span></span></span>

### Foreign involvement

Since Said Akbar had connections extending into Afghanistan's tribal frontier, suspicion quickly crossed Pakistan's borders. Diplomatic correspondence from 1951 shows both Pakistan and Afghanistan responding to rumours and accusations about Afghan involvement. Afghan officials denied responsibility, while American diplomatic records reveal concern that speculation could worsen regional tensions before any evidence had been established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951v06p2/d361" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the HistorianOctober 23, 1951...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 23, 1951</span></span></span>

Other theories have blamed British, American or Soviet intelligence services. These usually rely on Cold War geopolitics, Liaquat Ali Khan's foreign policy decisions or later political narratives rather than documentary proof. Despite their popularity in some political circles, no archival evidence has conclusively demonstrated that any foreign government ordered the assassination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951v06p2/d499" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianHistorical Documents - Office of the Historian...</span></span></span>

## What the inquiry actually concluded

A common misconception is that the Commission of Inquiry solved the case but that its findings were simply hidden. The historical record is more complicated.

Justice Munir's commission examined numerous possible motives and ultimately rejected several straightforward explanations, including claims that Said Akbar acted solely through mental instability or religious extremism. The commissioners concluded that the assassination appeared unlikely to have been purely an individual act. At the same time, they acknowledged that they could not identify the people behind any broader conspiracy with sufficient certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journal.psc.edu.pk/plugins/generic/pdfJsViewer/pdf.js/web/viewer.html?file=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.journal.psc.edu.pk%2Findex.php%2Fpp%2Farticle%2Fdownload%2F297%2F297%2F303" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journal.psc.edu.pk">[journal.psc.edu.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journal.psc.edu.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Feudal Tentacles: Is Feudalism Dead</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">admin,-S.M.Zulqurnain+Zidi.pdf...</span></span></span>

The inquiry also referred to indications of multiple conspiracies while noting that parts of its discussion could not be publicly disclosed because of national interest. Those omissions have themselves become part of the mystery, encouraging later speculation about what evidence was withheld and whether it would have materially changed public understanding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nihcr.edu.pk/Latest_English_Journal/4.%20THE%20ASSASSINATION.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nihcr.edu.pk">[nihcr.edu.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nihcr.edu.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Generated by Foxit PDF Creator © Foxit Software</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Generated by Foxit PDF Creator © Foxit Software</span></span></span>

## Why the missing chain of command still matters

The unresolved question is not whether Said Akbar fired the shots. That fact is well established. The mystery concerns whether he acted on behalf of someone else and, if so, who exercised command over the operation.

Without a surviving assassin to question and without documentary evidence identifying organisers, historians cannot reconstruct a complete chain linking the gunman to political sponsors. This missing link has had lasting consequences:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* It weakened public confidence in the state's ability to investigate attacks on senior leaders.
* It encouraged competing political narratives, each reflecting later ideological conflicts.
* It established a pattern in which later high-profile political killings in Pakistan were often interpreted through the lens of incomplete investigations and suspected hidden actors.

</div>

The assassination therefore occupies a distinctive place in Pakistan's history. It is remembered less because the identity of the shooter is disputed than because the authority behind him—if one existed—has never been securely identified.

## What can be said with confidence today

Modern historical scholarship draws a careful distinction between established facts and enduring speculation.

The established facts are clear: Liaquat Ali Khan was assassinated at a public meeting in Rawalpindi on 16 October 1951; Said Akbar fired the fatal shots; Akbar was immediately killed by police; an official commission investigated the crime but failed to identify a proven sponsor behind the attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk/items/00188d64-66d3-4e3c-b57b-328282b1b30f/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk">[punjab.gov.pk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.punjab.gov.pk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Assassination of Mr. Liaquat Ali Khan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Assassination of Mr. Liaquat Ali Khan</span></span></span>

What remains uncertain is the missing chain of command. No surviving primary evidence has demonstrated who, if anyone, recruited, directed or financed Said Akbar. That absence of conclusive proof—not the lack of theories—is what has preserved the assassination as one of Pakistan's most enduring historical mysteries.
