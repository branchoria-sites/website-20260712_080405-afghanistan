## Introduction

The strongest cases share a common difficulty: Spanish conquest disrupted institutions, destroyed records and displaced communities just as Europeans began describing the Andean world. Archaeologists must therefore combine colonial accounts, surviving objects, excavation, oral tradition and new technologies without assuming that any one source tells the whole story. What emerges is not a catalogue of supernatural secrets, but a history of incomplete evidence, disputed interpretation and discoveries that continue to change what is known about Peru’s past.

## Why were the Nazca Lines made?

The Nazca Lines are the most recognisable of Peru’s archaeological puzzles. Across the dry coastal plain around Nazca and Palpa, ancient communities removed dark surface stones to expose lighter ground beneath, producing long straight lines, geometric spaces and images of animals, plants and human-like figures. UNESCO dates the wider tradition broadly from about 500 BC to AD 500 and describes a protected landscape covering tens of thousands of hectares. The dry climate helped preserve markings that would have disappeared quickly in wetter terrain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/700/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreLines and Geoglyphs of Nasca and PalpaLocated in the arid Peruvian coastal plain, some 400 km south of Lima...</span></span></span>

There is little mystery about how they were constructed. Their makers did not need aircraft, advanced surveying equipment or outside assistance. Small designs could be enlarged using stakes, cords and measured points, while the surface stones were easily shifted by hand. The real unanswered question is why different generations created so many figures over such a large area.

Early twentieth-century interpretations often presented the lines as a giant astronomical calendar. The German-born researcher Maria Reiche devoted much of her life to documenting and protecting them and argued that some lines corresponded to celestial events. Later archaeoastronomical studies found that particular alignments could be identified, but not consistently enough to explain the entire complex. With hundreds of lines pointing in many directions, some apparent matches with stars or solstices are statistically inevitable.

A stronger modern interpretation links many of the lines to religious movement, water and pilgrimage. In one of the driest inhabited regions on Earth, water was not merely an economic resource but a central religious concern. Broad trapezoids may have accommodated gatherings, while straight lines could have acted as ceremonial paths leading towards mountains, river valleys or sacred places associated with water. Researchers Anthony Aveni and Johan Reinhard have argued that the lines make better sense as parts of a ritual landscape than as a single technical instrument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/nasca-lines" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicWhy the Nasca lines are among Peru&#x27;s greatest mysteries&quot;The rituals were likely involved with the ancient need to prop...</span></span></span>

Yet “ritual landscape” is not a complete translation of their meaning. The designs were created across centuries, and different types probably served different purposes. A 2024 study led by Masato Sakai used artificial intelligence to identify promising shapes in aerial imagery before checking them in the field. The team confirmed 303 previously undocumented figurative geoglyphs in six months. Many were small relief-style figures placed near ancient paths, unlike the enormous line-style animals traditionally associated with the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2407652121" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pnas.org">[pnas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pnas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AI-accelerated Nazca survey nearly doubles the number of...by M Sakai · 2024 · Cited by 29 — We report the deployment of an AI syste...</span></span></span>

Their locations suggest a useful distinction. The smaller figures may have been viewed at close range by people walking along paths, perhaps conveying information, stories or warnings. Larger figures and line systems appear better suited to collective ceremonies and movement across open space. That does not solve every image, but it replaces the idea of one secret code with a more plausible picture: a long-lived sacred landscape used in several related ways.

Claims that the lines were landing strips for extraterrestrials have no supporting archaeological evidence. They disregard the identifiable construction technique, the relationship between geoglyph imagery and local artistic traditions, and the gradual development of the landscape. The surviving uncertainty concerns the beliefs and social practices of ancient Peruvians, not whether humans made the lines.

## Can the Inca knotted records be read?

The Inca governed a vast empire without leaving books in the familiar alphabetic sense. Instead, officials used devices made from cords and knots, generally called khipus. Their colours, fibres, knot types, spacing, direction of twist and attachment to a main cord could all carry information. Spanish accounts describe trained specialists consulting them for censuses, taxation, storehouses, labour obligations and historical testimony. Archaeological finds at the Inca centre of Inkawasi include an archive of 34 khipus associated with state storage and administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/archaeology-of-measurement/recording-measurements-in-the-inka-khipu/14C2AD8E5EEA30CBABCDDF374F451389" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentRecording measure(ment)s in the Inka khipu (Chapter 6)5 Jun 2012 — The objective of this chapter i...</span></span></span>

Part of the system has been understood for a century. Many khipus record numbers through a decimal arrangement: knots in different positions represent units, tens, hundreds and higher values. Groups of cords can contain totals that correspond mathematically to their component entries. Matching sets appear to preserve duplicate accounts or records passed between different levels of administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/latin-american-antiquity/article/khipu-archives-duplicate-accounts-and-identity-labels-in-the-inka-knotted-string-records/019D70304BF1E88E4CE6F2EBBAAD597E" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

The unresolved problem is whether some khipus also encoded names, stories, laws or messages in a way comparable to writing. Colonial witnesses sometimes said they preserved histories, and not all surviving specimens behave like straightforward accounting records. However, most were removed from their original settings, often without reliable information about where they came from or what they recorded. Spanish authorities also destroyed many examples, particularly when they suspected an association with Indigenous religion or political resistance. The loss of both objects and trained readers broke the chain needed for easy decipherment.

Promising evidence comes from colonial-period khipus that can be compared with written documents. Research on a seventeenth-century record from coastal Peru has identified numerical and structural correspondences between a paper census and a set of cords. Elsewhere, Sabine Hyland’s study of eighteenth-century message khipus preserved at San Juan de Collata suggested that combinations of colour, animal fibre and cord structure might identify lineage names through sound-based associations. These findings show that cords could carry more than bare numbers, although it remains uncertain how closely late colonial systems resembled imperial Inca practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sapiens.org/language/khipu-andean-writing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sapiens.org">[sapiens.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sapiens.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">khipu andean writing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">khipu andean writing</span></span></span>

A 2025 study added another human dimension. Analysis of a khipu whose main cord was made from a person’s hair suggested that record-making knowledge may not have belonged exclusively to a small imperial elite. Chemical evidence from the hair indicated that its owner probably followed a modest diet, raising the possibility that people of lower social status also produced or handled complex records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/a-lock-of-braided-human-hair-could-change-how-we-think-about-inca-society-and-record-keeping-180987187/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

The khipu mystery therefore has two levels. Scholars can read much of the numerical structure, but there is no generally accepted key that converts most surviving examples into complete speech or narrative. A decisive breakthrough would probably require a secure match between a specific khipu and a detailed contemporary text describing the same information. Until then, claims that the entire system has been “decoded” go beyond the evidence.

## Was Peru’s lost Inca city really found?

Stories about a lost Inca city often merge two different places: Vilcabamba, the documented final capital of independent Inca resistance, and Paititi, a far less certain realm or settlement said to lie beyond the eastern Andes.

After Spanish forces seized Cusco, Manco Inca established a resistant state in the mountainous forests north-west of the former capital. Its rulers governed from Vilcabamba until Spanish troops captured the region in 1572. The settlement was then abandoned, and knowledge of its location faded outside nearby communities. This disappearance was historically real rather than legendary.

Hiram Bingham went to Peru in 1911 partly to search for Vilcabamba. Guided by local residents, he reached Machu Picchu and later promoted it as the lost Inca stronghold. The identification was wrong. Machu Picchu lies on a steep, relatively cool ridge and was most probably a royal estate associated with the Inca ruler Pachacuti. Radiocarbon research indicates occupation began earlier than the conventional chronology based solely on colonial royal lists had suggested, but nothing makes it the last forest capital described in conquest accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/new-ams-dates-for-machu-picchu-results-and-implications/323804857B6EE4DD85B5B337F9E3C933" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Bingham also visited Espíritu Pampa, a more extensive site in the warmer lowlands, but underestimated it. Later investigation showed that Espíritu Pampa matched historical descriptions of Vilcabamba far better: it lay beyond the earlier Inca centre of Vitcos, supported tropical crops and contained a settlement large enough to fit accounts of the final capital. Archaeological and documentary work in the later twentieth century established Espíritu Pampa as the accepted location of historical Vilcabamba.

The episode is instructive because the “lost city” was never wholly lost. Local people knew the ruins of Machu Picchu and cultivated nearby land before Bingham arrived. Residents also directed explorers towards Espíritu Pampa. What had been lost was the connection between known ruins and names preserved in colonial documents. The mystery was solved by taking local geography and archival descriptions more seriously, not by discovering an untouched city unknown to everyone.

Paititi remains much more elusive. Colonial writings refer variously to a rich eastern kingdom, a distant Indigenous ruler or a refuge beyond Spanish control. A much-publicised account attributed to the missionary Andrés López, dating from around 1600, describes a wealthy city called Paititi, but the report is indirect: López did not claim to have visited it himself. Other seventeenth-century references show that Spaniards believed important political centres existed east of the Andes, yet they do not produce a consistent map to one hidden Inca metropolis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The most plausible explanation is that traditions about real lowland societies, Inca frontier settlements and refugees from the conquest gradually combined into the idea of a single magnificent lost city. Archaeology increasingly demonstrates that Amazonian regions supported complex and substantial settlements, so the notion that important sites remain beneath Peru’s forest is reasonable. It does not follow that one of them will be the golden capital imagined in later expedition literature.

The search also has present-day consequences. Expeditions into protected parts of south-eastern Peru risk intruding on the territories of isolated Indigenous communities and exposing them to disease. The possibility of archaeological discovery does not justify treating inhabited forest as empty terrain waiting for outsiders to reveal it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2014/apr/07/search-inca-lost-city-amazon-peru-paititi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">search inca lost city amazon peru paititi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">search inca lost city amazon peru paititi</span></span></span>

## What happened to Atahualpa’s missing gold?

Atahualpa’s ransom begins with one of the best-documented episodes of the Spanish conquest. In November 1532, Francisco Pizarro’s force captured the Inca ruler at Cajamarca. Atahualpa offered an enormous payment: a room filled with gold objects and a larger quantity of silver in exchange for his release. Precious objects were gathered, melted and divided among the conquerors. Despite the payment, the Spaniards executed Atahualpa in July 1533. The stone chamber traditionally associated with his captivity survives in Cajamarca, although later rebuilding and legend complicate claims about every physical detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wmf.org/monuments/ransom-room" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wmf.org">[World Monuments Fund]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wmf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wmf.org.</span></span></span>

The mystery concerns reports that additional treasure was still travelling towards Cajamarca when news of Atahualpa’s death arrived. Later stories say the convoy was diverted and the gold hidden, sometimes in remote Andean lakes or caves. The best-known versions place it in the Llanganates mountains, which are now in Ecuador rather than Peru. This geographical shift reflects the size of Atahualpa’s political network and the later spread of the legend across modern borders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/lost-inca-gold" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalgeographic.com.</span></span></span>

It is entirely plausible that not all wealth ordered for the ransom reached the Spaniards. Transport across the Andes took time, communications were disrupted, and Inca commanders had strong reasons to withhold resources after Atahualpa was killed. But no securely documented cache has been identified as the missing ransom. Later treasure maps and accounts often appeared long after the conquest and contradict one another about locations, quantities and discoverers.

The popular image of a single cavern packed with untouched golden statues is also misleading. Inca wealth was distributed among temples, estates, storehouses, rulers and regional authorities. Precious metals had ceremonial and political importance rather than functioning simply as a central reserve of bullion. Spanish looting, unofficial concealment, later grave robbery and repeated melting could disperse objects without leaving one spectacular hoard.

Atahualpa’s “lost treasure” therefore occupies the boundary between reasonable inference and folklore. Some valuables probably failed to reach Cajamarca; there is no reliable evidence that they were all hidden together or that surviving directions lead to them. The legend endures because it gives material form to a larger historical loss: the destruction of an empire and the conversion of sacred objects into conquerors’ wealth.

## What were the thousands of holes at Monte Sierpe?

Monte Sierpe in Peru’s Pisco Valley presents a different kind of puzzle. Across a ridge runs a band containing roughly 5,200 human-made pits, arranged in blocks over about 1.5 kilometres. The holes vary in size and construction; some have stone linings, while empty spaces separate distinct groups. From ground level the overall pattern is difficult to recognise, helping explain why its purpose remained uncertain for so long.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/indigenous-accounting-and-exchange-at-monte-sierpe-band-of-holes-in-the-pisco-valley-peru/41D83389D230D99CFF18194CF274A9A3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Suggested explanations have included water collection, defensive works, storage, gardening and ritual activity. None fitted all of the site’s features. The pits do not form an obvious irrigation system, and the ridge is an inconvenient location for ordinary permanent storage. Their highly organised grouping nevertheless implies planned collective use rather than random digging.

Research published in 2025 offered the strongest explanation so far. Drone mapping revealed repeated numerical and spatial groupings. Sediment samples contained microscopic traces associated with crops and plant materials, including maize and reeds that may have been used in baskets or packaging. The researchers proposed that Monte Sierpe originally served the pre-Inca Chincha state as a periodic exchange place where goods were deposited in separate pits. After the Inca incorporated the region, the site may have been reorganised for counting, tribute collection or administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/indigenous-accounting-and-exchange-at-monte-sierpe-band-of-holes-in-the-pisco-valley-peru/41D83389D230D99CFF18194CF274A9A3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

The patterning has been compared with khipus because both divide quantities into structured groups. That resemblance does not mean the hillside is a literal stone version of a knotted record, but it supports the idea that the pits helped organise goods and obligations. A market and an accounting site are not mutually exclusive: exchange, taxation and state supervision could have occurred at the same place during different periods.

The case is not entirely closed. More dating evidence is needed to establish when separate sections were dug and how their use changed. Archaeologists must also determine whether plant traces represent goods placed deliberately in the pits or later contamination. Even so, the new evidence makes alien construction and other extravagant explanations unnecessary. Monte Sierpe is becoming less an inexplicable monument than a rare physical record of how Andean communities managed exchange without coins or written ledgers.

## Are the elongated Paracas skulls really unexplained?

Images of elongated skulls from the Paracas Peninsula are frequently presented online as evidence of an unknown human species or non-human visitors. The archaeological explanation is much less dramatic and much better supported: many ancient Peruvian societies deliberately shaped infants’ heads by applying cloth bindings, boards or other controlled pressure while the skull was still growing.

Intentional cranial modification occurred in different parts of the Andes and elsewhere in the world. Skull shape could mark community, ancestry, social standing or regional identity. Because communities used different techniques, the resulting forms vary considerably, including flattened, rounded and strongly lengthened profiles. Research treats these differences as evidence about identity and social relationships, not as proof of separate biological species.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X21004764" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

Claims that Paracas skulls possess impossible anatomy, abnormal numbers of skull plates or mysterious DNA generally originate in unsupervised sampling, private collections or reports that have not undergone credible scientific review. Context is crucial. Skulls taken from looted graves cannot always be linked reliably to a burial, date or population, and contamination can make genetic results especially difficult to interpret. Extraordinary conclusions drawn from a handful of poorly documented samples do not outweigh the broad archaeological record of intentional head shaping.

This does not mean every question about the Paracas population is settled. Researchers continue to study migration, diet, health, kinship and the social meanings of bodily modification. Ancient DNA and isotope analysis may clarify where individuals grew up and how communities interacted. The genuine mystery is historical and cultural: why particular forms became important, who was permitted or expected to wear them, and how they communicated identity.

The transformation of modified human remains into an “alien skull” story also raises an ethical problem. These were people whose bodies were buried within specific communities and later excavated, collected or looted. Treating them merely as bizarre exhibits removes the human and cultural context that archaeology is meant to recover.

## How to judge Peru’s mystery stories

Peru’s past contains real gaps, but the quality of a mystery depends on the evidence behind it. The most useful distinction is not between “solved” and “unsolved”, but between several different kinds of uncertainty.

**A documented puzzle** begins with secure material or written evidence. The Nazca geoglyphs exist, khipus demonstrably stored information, and Monte Sierpe contains thousands of deliberately arranged pits. Debate concerns function and meaning.

**A plausible historical inference** goes beyond what can be directly proved but remains consistent with known events. Some of Atahualpa’s intended ransom may never have reached Cajamarca, and significant archaeological settlements almost certainly remain undocumented beneath Peru’s forests.

**A disputed identification** arises when known places or objects are connected to historical names. The long argument over Machu Picchu and Vilcabamba was resolved by comparing geography, documents and archaeology. Paititi remains uncertain because the surviving descriptions are inconsistent and often indirect.

**A legend** may preserve fragments of historical memory while changing dramatically through retelling. Stories of a single hidden Inca treasury probably draw strength from real confiscation, concealment and colonial violence, but their maps and precise locations lack dependable support.

**A modern myth** often survives by omitting evidence already capable of explaining the supposed anomaly. Alien interpretations of the Nazca Lines and Paracas skulls depend on presenting ancient Peruvian engineering or cultural practices as impossible.

The distinction matters because Peru’s mysteries have often been told through an outsider’s assumption that Indigenous achievements require an exotic explanation. The better approach is almost the reverse. The most compelling puzzles arise from the complexity of Andean societies: administrative systems made from fibre, sacred landscapes created over generations, political centres linking mountains and rainforest, and economic practices that left unfamiliar marks on the land.

## Why the mysteries still matter

These cases are important not simply because something remains unknown, but because each uncertainty exposes how history is reconstructed. Colonial chroniclers recorded invaluable information while misunderstanding or suppressing the societies they described. Explorers sometimes publicised sites while overlooking the knowledge of local residents. Museums preserved objects but often separated them from the places and communities that could explain them.

New discoveries are steadily narrowing those gaps. Artificial intelligence and field inspection have expanded the known Nazca landscape. Drone mapping and microbotanical analysis have produced a credible interpretation of Monte Sierpe. Radiocarbon dating has challenged inherited chronologies at Machu Picchu. Digital khipu databases allow scholars to compare structural patterns across collections that were once isolated from one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2407652121" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pnas.org">[pnas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pnas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AI-accelerated Nazca survey nearly doubles the number of...by M Sakai · 2024 · Cited by 29 — We report the deployment of an AI syste...</span></span></span>

Yet better technology does not automatically produce certainty. A computer can locate a faint geoglyph without explaining its symbolism. Chemical analysis can identify where a person ate or grew up without revealing what their community believed. A mathematical pattern in a khipu may show how information was organised while leaving its language unread.

Peru’s enduring historical enigmas are therefore neither evidence of a vanished super-civilisation nor a set of puzzles awaiting one dramatic revelation. They are fragments of societies whose knowledge was disrupted, dispersed and partly silenced. Their investigation matters because each careful recovery—of a route, a cord pattern, a plant trace or a neglected archival description—restores part of a history that conquest and later mythmaking made harder to see.
