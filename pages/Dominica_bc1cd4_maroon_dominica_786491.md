## Introduction

[Dominica]({{ 'what-is-still-unsolved-in-dominicas-past/' | relative_url }})'s Maroon communities are remarkable not because they disappeared without trace, but because they survived for decades in one of the Caribbean's most rugged landscapes while leaving relatively little physical evidence behind. From the mid-eighteenth century into the early nineteenth century, escaped enslaved Africans established camps deep within the island's mountainous interior, creating organised networks that repeatedly frustrated British military expeditions. The enduring mystery is not whether these communities existed—the documentary record leaves little doubt—but exactly how they governed themselves, maintained communication, and sustained life in terrain that colonial authorities found almost impossible to control. Recent archaeological and geographical research, combined with surviving colonial records and Dominican oral traditions, has begun to reveal how these forest societies functioned, while also highlighting how much remains hidden beneath the island's rainforest.

## Why the mountains became a place of freedom

Dominica offered opportunities unlike many Caribbean plantation colonies. Much of the island consists of steep volcanic mountains, deep river valleys and dense tropical forest. Even after Britain formally acquired the island in 1763, colonial settlement remained concentrated around the coast, leaving large parts of the interior beyond effective government control.

Escaped enslaved people exploited this geography. Rather than hiding as isolated fugitives, many joined organised Maroon communities that developed permanent or semi-permanent camps in the interior. Colonial correspondence repeatedly describes military patrols struggling to locate or destroy these settlements, despite considerable effort. The Maroons also maintained contact with enslaved people on plantations, receiving intelligence, supplies and new recruits while encouraging further escapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of Defensibility and Accessibility of Maroon Settlem...</span></span></span>

Their success depended on far more than finding inaccessible hiding places. The camps formed a connected resistance landscape that combined defence, food production and communication across the mountains.

## Jacko and the Maroon wars

Among the best remembered Maroon leaders is Jacko, whose name survives in Dominican place names and oral tradition. Although colonial documents mention numerous Maroon captains—including leaders such as Balla, Pharcelle, Noel and Cicero—Jacko became the enduring symbol of organised resistance because of his ability to regroup after military defeats and maintain alliances across multiple camps.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of Defensibility and Accessibility of Maroon Settlem...</span></span></span>

During the conflicts often described as Dominica's Maroon Wars in the 1780s and the renewed campaigns of the 1790s and early nineteenth century, British forces launched repeated expeditions into the interior. These campaigns were expensive, exhausting and frequently unsuccessful. Soldiers unfamiliar with the terrain found themselves climbing narrow mountain tracks, crossing swollen rivers and searching forests where the defenders possessed every geographical advantage.

Contemporary accounts describe Jacko's principal stronghold as being reached only by an extremely difficult ascent. The famous "Jacko Steps"—stone steps cut into the mountainside—illustrate the sophisticated balance between accessibility and defence. They allowed Maroons to move efficiently while forcing attacking soldiers into narrow single-file formations that could easily be ambushed from above. According to eighteenth-century descriptions, sentries stationed at the summit could observe approaching enemies long before they reached the camp.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of Defensibility and Accessibility of Maroon Settlem...</span></span></span>

British expeditions occasionally destroyed camps, but leaders frequently escaped through alternative routes, rebuilt elsewhere or joined allied settlements, demonstrating that the resistance depended on a network rather than a single headquarters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of Defensibility and Accessibility of Maroon Settlem...</span></span></span>

## Camps, routes and the governor of the woods

Colonial officials often portrayed the Maroons simply as fugitives, yet the surviving evidence points towards organised communities with leadership, territorial knowledge and systems of communication.

Modern geographical analysis has transformed historians' understanding of these settlements. Using historical documents together with Geographic Information Systems (GIS), researchers have reconstructed the likely locations of several Maroon camps and examined how they related to one another across the landscape. The results suggest deliberate planning rather than random concealment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of Defensibility and Accessibility of Maroon Settlem...</span></span></span>

Several consistent patterns emerge:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Camps occupied elevated plateaus protected by cliffs, rivers and dense forest.
* Many settlements could see at least one neighbouring camp, allowing visual communication across the mountains.
* River valleys provided reliable water supplies while also acting as natural barriers.
* Hidden routes linked settlements to plantations, making intelligence gathering and rescue missions possible.
* Provision grounds lay beyond the main defensive perimeter, reducing the risk that a single attack would destroy food supplies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of Defensibility and Accessibility of Maroon Settlem...</span></span></span>

</div>

Dominican oral traditions sometimes refer to experienced Maroon leaders as effectively becoming "governors" of the forest—not through formal titles recognised by colonial authorities, but through practical authority over movement, defence and survival within territory the government could not truly dominate. Colonial officials controlled ports and plantations; the Maroons controlled knowledge of the interior.

This contrast explains why military expeditions repeatedly failed despite superior numbers and weapons. The British possessed maps of the coastline and settlements, whereas the Maroons possessed what archaeologists describe as mental maps: detailed knowledge of ridges, caves, river crossings, escape paths and observation points accumulated through years of movement within the forest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/black_studies_fac/46/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu">[PDXScholar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scholar&quot;Tracking Down The Marrons: Archaeo-Geography Of Marronage In The Carib&quot; by E. ## Survival depended on more than secrecy

The image of isolated fugitives living entirely apart from colonial society is increasingly regarded as misleading.

Evidence suggests the Maroons maintained extensive relationships beyond their camps. Enslaved workers on plantations warned them about troop movements, supplied food or information, and sometimes joined them permanently. In return, Maroon groups launched raids that liberated additional enslaved people or seized tools, weapons and provisions needed for survival. Colonial reports repeatedly express concern about these connections, demonstrating that officials recognised the interior communities as an organised threat rather than scattered individuals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of Defensibility and Accessibility of Maroon Settlem...</span></span></span>

Agriculture also played an essential role. Historical descriptions mention gardens within defended settlements and larger cultivation areas located at safer distances. These food-producing landscapes allowed communities to survive for prolonged periods instead of relying entirely on raids or hunting. Recent archaeological interpretation suggests camp locations balanced military security with access to fertile ground and freshwater.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of Defensibility and Accessibility of Maroon Settlem...</span></span></span>

## What archaeology could still uncover

One of the greatest mysteries is how little of Dominica's Maroon landscape has been excavated.

Unlike Jamaica or Suriname, where archaeological investigations have documented several Maroon settlements, none of Dominica's principal camps has yet undergone full archaeological excavation. Dense vegetation, difficult terrain and the limited scale of archaeological work on the island mean that much remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://revistas.usp.br/revmae/en/article/view/164882" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revistas.usp.br">[Portal de Revistas da USP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revistas.usp.br</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 21, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 2020</span></span></span>

Researchers believe future investigations could answer important questions, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* How large were individual settlements?
* Were camps occupied continuously or seasonally?
* What kinds of houses and defensive structures were built?
* Which crops supported long-term survival?
* How extensive were communication networks between different Maroon leaders?

</div>

The surviving documentary evidence comes overwhelmingly from colonial officials attempting to suppress the Maroons. Archaeological discoveries could therefore provide the first substantial material evidence created by the communities themselves rather than by their opponents.

Modern GIS modelling has already identified likely settlement zones, making future surveys considerably more targeted than earlier searches. ## Why the mystery still matters

The enduring puzzle surrounding Dominica's Maroons is not whether they resisted colonial rule, but how a relatively small population maintained autonomous communities for more than half a century despite repeated military campaigns.

The answer increasingly appears to lie in a combination of geography, organisation and social networks rather than in any single hidden refuge. Mountain strongholds, carefully engineered access routes, alliances between camps, intelligence from plantations and detailed environmental knowledge created a resilient system that colonial governments never fully mastered.

Yet important questions remain unresolved because the physical evidence is still largely concealed beneath Dominica's forests. Every newly identified route, campsite or archaeological feature has the potential to reshape understanding of how these communities governed themselves and how freedom was defended in one of the Caribbean's most formidable landscapes.
