## Introduction

Some of [Indonesia]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-indonesias/' | relative_url }})'s most important unresolved historical mysteries are not about identifying the immediate killers, but about establishing who gave the orders. Two cases stand out: the 1993 murder of labour activist Marsinah and the 2004 arsenic poisoning of human-rights lawyer Munir Said Thalib. In both investigations, courts convicted or prosecuted individuals connected to the crimes, yet neither case produced a definitive judicial account of the full chain of responsibility. That gap has made these killings enduring symbols of impunity—the failure to hold those who plan or direct political violence accountable even when evidence suggests a wider conspiracy. Their significance lies not only in the deaths themselves but in the continuing uncertainty over how powerful institutions may have influenced the investigations and why the complete truth has remained elusive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/amnesty/1994/en/91506" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: refworld.org">[Refworld]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">refworld.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Indonesia: Power and Impunity: Human Rights under the New Order &#124; Refworld</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Indonesia: Power and Impunity: Human Rights under the New Order &#124; RefworldSeptember 1, 1994...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 1994</span></span></span>

## Who Ordered Indonesia's Unsolved Political Killings?

Unlike ordinary criminal investigations, these cases raise two separate questions. The first is who physically carried out the killing. The second—and more controversial—is whether those individuals acted on behalf of more senior organisers who escaped prosecution.

Indonesia's courts have partially answered the first question in both cases. They have never conclusively answered the second. Human-rights organisations, legal scholars and Indonesia's own investigative bodies have repeatedly argued that evidence pointed beyond the individuals initially prosecuted, while judges themselves sometimes remarked that the known perpetrators could not have acted alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2006/09/04/indonesia-no-justice-two-years-after-munirs-death" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights WatchIndonesia: No Justice Two Years After Munir’s Death &#124; Human Rights WatchSeptember 4, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 4, 2006</span></span></span>

This distinction explains why both murders remain part of Indonesia's unresolved historical record. The mystery is not whether crimes occurred, but whether the full command structure behind them has ever been exposed.

## Marsinah's Murder After a Labour Dispute

Marsinah was a young factory worker and labour activist in East Java. In May 1993 she became involved in a dispute over wages and working conditions after workers at a watch factory organised industrial action. Shortly afterwards she [disappeared]({{ 'disappeared/' | relative_url }}). Several days later her body was discovered bearing signs of severe torture.

The broad outline of events is well established. The controversy concerns where she was held, who interrogated her and who authorised the violence.

Official investigations initially focused on civilian suspects connected with the factory. Independent inquiries by Indonesian legal aid organisations and the National Human Rights Commission, however, raised serious doubts about that account. Investigators identified inconsistencies in witness testimony, allegations that confessions had been extracted under torture and indications that military personnel may have played a far greater role than prosecutors acknowledged. Amnesty International reported that defendants retracted their statements in court, alleging coercion, while independent investigations suggested Marsinah was likely detained and killed inside a military facility rather than elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/amnesty/1994/en/91506" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: refworld.org">[Refworld]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">refworld.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Indonesia: Power and Impunity: Human Rights under the New Order &#124; Refworld</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Indonesia: Power and Impunity: Human Rights under the New Order &#124; RefworldSeptember 1, 1994...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 1994</span></span></span>

The legal proceedings therefore resolved remarkably little. Convictions against civilian defendants were later overturned, leaving no final judicial determination of who murdered Marsinah. More importantly, no senior official was ever held criminally responsible despite longstanding allegations that the investigation failed to examine potential military involvement fully. The result has been an enduring uncertainty over whether the case represented an isolated crime or reflected broader patterns of state-backed intimidation during the final years of President Suharto's New Order.

## The Poisoning of Human-Rights Lawyer Munir

Munir Said Thalib was Indonesia's best-known human-rights lawyer, recognised for investigating military abuses in East Timor, Aceh and elsewhere. On 7 September 2004, while travelling on a Garuda Indonesia flight from Jakarta to Amsterdam, he died after being poisoned with arsenic. A Dutch forensic examination confirmed poisoning as the cause of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2006/09/04/indonesia-no-justice-two-years-after-munirs-death" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights WatchIndonesia: No Justice Two Years After Munir’s Death &#124; Human Rights WatchSeptember 4, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 4, 2006</span></span></span>

The investigation rapidly established that the death was not accidental. A Garuda pilot, Pollycarpus Budihari Priyanto, who travelled on the same flight despite not being part of the operating crew, became the principal suspect. He was eventually convicted after complex legal proceedings. Former Garuda chief executive Indra Setiawan was also convicted for document-related offences connected with Pollycarpus's assignment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2010/02/11/indonesia-reopen-inquiry-activists-murder" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrw.org.</span></span></span>

Yet the convictions did not settle the larger question.

Indonesia's independent fact-finding team examined telephone records and other evidence that appeared to indicate contacts between Pollycarpus and officials within the State Intelligence Agency (BIN). The team's reported findings suggested possible links extending beyond the airline itself. Former intelligence official Muchdi Purwopranjono was subsequently prosecuted for allegedly helping organise the murder, but he was acquitted after a trial criticised by domestic and international observers for weaknesses in witness handling and evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2006/10/06/indonesia-acquittal-bolsters-impunity-munirs-murder" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights WatchIndonesia: Acquittal Bolsters Impunity for Munir’s Murder &#124; Human Rights WatchOctober 6, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 6, 2006</span></span></span>

Human Rights Watch noted that even judges involved in earlier proceedings stated that Pollycarpus had not acted alone and urged investigators to pursue those ultimately responsible. Despite repeated calls from Indonesia's National Human Rights Commission and civil-society organisations, no court has established a complete chain of command behind the assassination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2006/09/04/indonesia-no-justice-two-years-after-munirs-death" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights WatchIndonesia: No Justice Two Years After Munir’s Death &#124; Human Rights WatchSeptember 4, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 4, 2006</span></span></span>

## Why Convictions Failed to Reveal the Full Conspiracy

The two cases differ in detail but reveal similar institutional problems.

In both investigations:

* **Operational responsibility proved easier to investigate than command responsibility.** Individual participants could be identified, while evidence linking decisions to senior figures proved far harder to establish in court.
* **Witness reliability became a central issue.** Marsinah's case involved allegations of coerced confessions, while Munir's prosecution saw disputes over witness testimony, documentary evidence and investigative procedure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.refworld.org/reference/countryrep/amnesty/1994/en/91506" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: refworld.org">[Refworld]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">refworld.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Indonesia: Power and Impunity: Human Rights under the New Order &#124; Refworld</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Indonesia: Power and Impunity: Human Rights under the New Order &#124; RefworldSeptember 1, 1994...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 1994</span></span></span>
* **Independent inquiries reached broader conclusions than criminal courts.** Fact-finding bodies and human-rights organisations repeatedly suggested wider involvement than final verdicts reflected, but their findings did not automatically translate into criminal convictions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2006/10/06/indonesia-acquittal-bolsters-impunity-munirs-murder" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights WatchIndonesia: Acquittal Bolsters Impunity for Munir’s Murder &#124; Human Rights WatchOctober 6, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 6, 2006</span></span></span>
* **Political sensitivity complicated accountability.** Both cases touched institutions with significant political influence, making allegations of interference and incomplete investigations persistent features of public debate.

It is important to distinguish between suspicion and proof. Numerous observers argue that more senior organisers escaped accountability, but criminal courts require evidence meeting a much higher legal standard. That difference explains why public belief in a broader conspiracy has persisted even though definitive judicial findings remain absent.

## Why These Cases Still Matter

Marsinah and Munir have become reference points whenever Indonesians discuss accountability for politically sensitive crimes. Rather than simply representing two unsolved murders, they illustrate a wider historical problem: investigations that establish enough facts to demonstrate deliberate killing, yet fail to produce a complete and widely accepted account of who ultimately directed it.

For historians, the unresolved element is therefore quite specific. It is not uncertainty over whether the victims were murdered, nor whether the deaths were politically significant. The enduring mystery is whether Indonesia's justice system ever uncovered—and was able or willing to prosecute—the highest level of responsibility.

That unanswered question continues to shape debates about judicial independence, intelligence oversight and human-rights accountability. Decades after Marsinah's death and more than twenty years after Munir's assassination, both cases remain powerful reminders that identifying perpetrators is not always the same as uncovering the full truth.
