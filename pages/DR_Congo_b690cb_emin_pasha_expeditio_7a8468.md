## Introduction

The Emin Pasha Relief Expedition of 1886–1890 is remembered as one of the most controversial expeditions of the nineteenth century rather than one of its greatest rescues. Led by Henry Morton Stanley, the expedition crossed what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo before reaching Equatoria, where the governor known as Emin Pasha was believed to be stranded after the collapse of Egyptian rule in Sudan. What remains disputed is not whether the expedition reached Emin, but why it took the route it did, how much violence occurred along the way, who was responsible for some of its worst episodes, and whether the stated humanitarian mission concealed wider imperial ambitions. Contradictory memoirs, selective publication of journals and gaps in the surviving documentary record have ensured that historians continue to debate the expedition more than a century later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emin_Pasha_Relief_Expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span></span></span>

## What really happened on Stanley's Congo expedition?

The expedition was organised with the declared aim of relieving Emin Pasha, the governor of the isolated Equatoria Province on the Upper Nile. Emin had become cut off after the Mahdist revolt in Sudan severed communications with Egypt. Stanley accepted the mission with support from British backers, but instead of approaching from the east coast, he chose a route up the Congo River and through the dense Ituri rainforest, passing across territory that now lies within the Democratic Republic of the Congo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/76167/76167-h/76167-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Stanley’s Emin Pasha Expedition &#124; Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Stanley’s Emin Pasha Expedition &#124; Project Gutenberg</span></span></span>

The Congo route remains one of the expedition's most debated decisions. Stanley argued that it was the quickest and most practical means of reaching Emin. Critics, both at the time and later, questioned whether the choice also advanced King Leopold II's growing interests in the Congo Free State by mapping territory, strengthening transport routes and demonstrating European access across Central Africa. Modern historians generally conclude that both strategic and humanitarian motives probably influenced the expedition, rather than seeing the two explanations as mutually exclusive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emin_Pasha_Relief_Expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span></span></span>

The journey proved catastrophic. Disease, starvation, exhaustion and conflict devastated both the European officers and the hundreds of African porters recruited to carry supplies. Casualty estimates vary because surviving records are incomplete, but it is clear that a large proportion of the expedition's African personnel never completed the journey. The suffering of these carriers, long overshadowed in European accounts, has become central to more recent reassessments of the expedition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emin_Pasha_Relief_Expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span></span></span>

## Why are the expedition's accounts so contradictory?

The expedition generated an unusually large body of first-hand writing. Stanley published his own version of events, while other participants, including Arthur Jephson and William Bonny, produced memoirs, letters and diaries. These sources often agree on major events but differ sharply over responsibility, motives and behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nypl.org/research/research-catalog/browse/subjects/Emin%20Pasha%20Relief%20Expedition%20%281887-1889%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nypl.org">[nypl.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nypl.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New York Public Library Search &#124; Research Catalog &#124; NYPL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New York Public Library Search &#124; Research Catalog &#124; NYPL</span></span></span>

Several factors explain these contradictions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Participants were separated for long periods and often relied on rumour rather than direct observation.
* Many memoirs were written after the expedition, when reputations were already under attack.
* Editors sometimes revised or abridged journals before publication.
* Some private notebooks remained unpublished for decades, while other documents have been lost altogether.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.africamuseum.be/fr/research/collections_libraries/human_sciences/archives/stanley/journal?g=05&amp;p=06" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africamuseum.be">[africamuseum.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africamuseum.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Africa MuseumInventory of the Stanley Archives &#124; Musée royal de l&#x27;Afrique centrale - Tervuren - Belgique...</span></span></span>

</div>

Because no single contemporary narrative provides a complete picture, historians compare multiple accounts alongside official correspondence and archival material rather than accepting one memoir at face value.

### The problem of Stanley's own narrative

Stanley's published books presented the expedition as an extraordinary achievement against immense odds. His critics argued that he minimised mistakes, justified harsh discipline and shifted blame onto absent officers.

The later discovery and cataloguing of Stanley's personal journals and notebooks has allowed scholars to compare his private observations with his published works. These archives show that he recorded far than appeared in print, but they have not eliminated the central debates because even the private papers reflect Stanley's own perspective and were revised after the expedition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.africamuseum.be/fr/research/collections_libraries/human_sciences/archives/stanley/journal?g=05&amp;p=06" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africamuseum.be">[Africa Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africamuseum.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Africa MuseumInventory of the Stanley Archives &#124; Musée royal de l&#x27;Afrique centrale - Tervuren - Belgique...</span></span></span>

## The rear column remains the greatest mystery

The expedition's most disputed chapter concerns the so-called "rear column". Stanley divided his force at Yambuya on the Aruwimi River, leaving a substantial group under Major Edmund Barttelot to await additional carriers and supplies while he pushed eastwards through the forest.

Months passed without communication. When Stanley eventually returned in search of the rear column, he found chaos. Barttelot had been killed, several officers had died or departed, supplies had been wasted, discipline had collapsed and many African porters had deserted or died. The original logistical plan had effectively disintegrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emin_Pasha_Relief_Expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span></span></span>

Exactly why this happened remains contested.

Stanley blamed Barttelot's leadership, arguing that indecision, brutality and poor judgement destroyed the rear column before it could fulfil its role. Surviving officers, however, pointed to impossible circumstances: prolonged isolation, contradictory instructions, disease, dependence upon regional power brokers for carriers and the complete absence of reliable communication with Stanley for well over a year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emin_Pasha_Relief_Expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span></span></span>

The result is one of the enduring archival puzzles of nineteenth-century exploration. Historians possess multiple eyewitness accounts but no neutral record capable of settling every disputed decision.

## Violence, responsibility and changing historical judgement

Violence accompanied much of the expedition, but assigning responsibility is often difficult because accounts conflict and local African perspectives survive only in fragments.

European narratives describe repeated fighting with communities encountered in the rainforest, frequently portraying these as acts of self-defence. Later historians have questioned whether some confrontations resulted from aggressive expedition tactics, food seizures or misunderstandings created by the movement of a heavily armed foreign force through unfamiliar territory. The absence of equivalent African written testimony makes reconstruction particularly difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emin_Pasha_Relief_Expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span></span></span>

One notorious controversy centres on James Sligo Jameson, an officer in the rear column. Contemporary witnesses accused him of paying for a young enslaved girl to be handed to cannibals so he could observe and sketch her death. Jameson admitted paying for the girl but claimed he believed the event would not actually occur. Other eyewitnesses rejected that explanation, insisting he remained to watch what happened. The episode became an international scandal and continues to illustrate how difficult it is to reconcile conflicting testimony from the expedition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emin_Pasha_Relief_Expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Emin Pasha Relief Expedition</span></span></span>

Modern scholarship also gives greater weight to the experiences of African carriers and guides, whose losses far exceeded those of the European officers but received comparatively little attention in Victorian accounts. This shift has significantly changed how the expedition is understood.

## Was it really a rescue mission?

The expedition unquestionably reached Emin Pasha, but even the success of the "relief" mission proved ambiguous.

Emin did not immediately abandon his province. He hesitated, consulted his officers and weighed whether evacuation was preferable to remaining with his troops. Internal revolts among his own forces, rather than Stanley's arrival alone, shaped the final outcome. Stanley himself spent months retracing his route to recover the rear column before returning to Emin, turning what had been presented as a straightforward rescue into a prolonged and uncertain political negotiation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/76167/76167-h/76167-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Stanley’s Emin Pasha Expedition &#124; Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Stanley’s Emin Pasha Expedition &#124; Project Gutenberg</span></span></span>

This has encouraged historians to view the expedition as more than a humanitarian mission. It also served scientific, geographical and imperial purposes by gathering information, mapping routes and reinforcing European influence in Central Africa during the period known as the Scramble for Africa. The balance between these motives remains a matter of interpretation rather than settled fact.

## Why the expedition still matters in DR Congo's history

Within the history of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the Emin Pasha Relief Expedition occupies a distinctive place because many of its greatest controversies unfolded while crossing the Congo Basin rather than at its final destination.

The expedition illustrates several broader themes that recur throughout the country's colonial history:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the difficulty of reconstructing events from incomplete archives;
* the dominance of European voices over African experiences in surviving records;
* the overlap between exploration, humanitarian rhetoric and imperial expansion;
* the way later publication, editing and selective preservation of documents can reshape historical memory.

</div>

Rather than presenting a single unsolved mystery, the expedition is better understood as a collection of unresolved historical questions. New archival work has clarified aspects of its route, chronology and documentation, yet fundamental disputes over motive, responsibility and the scale of violence remain because the surviving evidence is incomplete, contradictory and filtered through competing personal narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.africamuseum.be/fr/research/collections_libraries/human_sciences/archives/stanley/journal?g=05&amp;p=06" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africamuseum.be">[africamuseum.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africamuseum.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Africa MuseumInventory of the Stanley Archives &#124; Musée royal de l&#x27;Afrique centrale - Tervuren - Belgique...</span></span></span>
