## Introduction

One of the deepest unresolved questions in modern Uruguayan history is not whether people were forcibly disappeared during the 1973–1985 civil–military dictatorship—that is well documented—but where many of them were buried and whether every victim can still be found. Families were denied bodies, death certificates and truthful official accounts, leaving decades of uncertainty that has continued long after the return to democracy. The mystery today lies in hidden burial sites, incomplete military records, contradictory testimony and the passage of time. Despite important forensic breakthroughs, investigators are still searching former military land, especially Battalion 14, where discoveries have confirmed that clandestine burials occurred but have also shown how much remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gub.uy/institucion-nacional-derechos-humanos-uruguay/comunicacion/publicaciones/desaparicion-forzada-informe-sobre-denuncias-casos-investigacion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gub.uy">[Gub.uy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gub.uy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Desaparición forzada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Informe sobre denuncias y casos en investigación &#124; Institución Nacional de Derechos Humanos y Defensoría del PuebloMay 14, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 14, 2026</span></span></span>

## How enforced disappearance worked

Enforced disappearance was designed to erase both a person and the evidence of what had happened to them. Political detainees were secretly arrested, held in unofficial or military detention centres, tortured and, in many cases, killed. Authorities then denied knowledge of their fate, preventing families from recovering bodies or obtaining reliable information.

This system created a lasting historical mystery because it attacked the historical record itself. False reports, destroyed documents and official denials meant that relatives often spent decades searching without knowing whether their loved one had died, been transferred abroad or been buried anonymously. In some cases, Uruguayan citizens disappeared inside the country; in others they were abducted elsewhere in South America through the cross-border intelligence network known as Operation Condor, making reconstruction even more difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gub.uy/institucion-nacional-derechos-humanos-uruguay/comunicacion/publicaciones/desaparicion-forzada-informe-sobre-denuncias-casos-investigacion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gub.uy">[Gub.uy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gub.uy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Desaparición forzada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Informe sobre denuncias y casos en investigación &#124; Institución Nacional de Derechos Humanos y Defensoría del PuebloMay 14, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 14, 2026</span></span></span>

Unlike an ordinary missing-person investigation, these cases involve deliberate concealment by state institutions. Even where historians can establish who was detained and approximately when they died, the precise locations of burial sites often remain uncertain.

## What searches at Battalion 14 have found

The clearest physical evidence has come from the grounds of the former Parachute Infantry Battalion No. 14 outside Montevideo. For years, investigators suspected that military land had been used for clandestine burials, but proving this required painstaking archaeological work rather than relying solely on witness testimony.

Excavation teams use methods more familiar from archaeology than criminal investigation. They compare historical maps with modern landscapes, examine soil disturbances, analyse aerial photographs, remove earth layer by layer and recover even tiny fragments of bone, clothing or personal objects. Every discovery is documented before DNA testing begins.

A major breakthrough came in June 2023 when human remains were uncovered at Battalion 14. After months of forensic analysis and international DNA comparisons, investigators confirmed in May 2024 that the remains belonged to Amelia Sanjurjo Casal, a Communist Party activist who disappeared in 1977 after being detained by security forces. The identification was achieved through cooperation between [Uruguay]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-uruguays/' | relative_url }})'s forensic investigators, the Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team and Sanjurjo's surviving relatives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gub.uy/fiscalia-general-nacion/comunicacion/noticias/identifican-restos-hallados-batallon-14" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gub.uy">[Gub.uy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gub.uy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Identifican restos hallados en el Batallón 14 &#124; Fiscalía General de la Nación</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 28, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 28, 2024</span></span></span>

The discovery carried wider significance than solving a single case. It confirmed that Battalion 14 had indeed been used as a clandestine burial site and demonstrated that decades-old disappearances can still be resolved through modern forensic science.

Searches did not stop there. In July 2024 investigators uncovered another buried skeleton at the same military installation, approximately 170 metres from the place where Sanjurjo's remains had been found. The burial showed a similarly concealed pattern, including layers of lime and construction material, suggesting deliberate efforts to hide the grave. At the time of recovery, forensic specialists determined that the remains probably belonged to an adult man, but identification required further genetic analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gub.uy/institucion-nacional-derechos-humanos-uruguay/comunicacion/noticias/nuevo-hallazgo-batallon-n-14" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gub.uy">[Gub.uy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gub.uy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 2, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 2, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why so many cases remain unresolved

Although several disappearances have now been solved, most have not. The reasons are practical as well as historical.

Large military properties must be searched metre by metre because witness testimony often identifies only a general area rather than an exact grave. Over several decades, construction work, vegetation growth and changes to the landscape have altered the original terrain, making former burial sites difficult to recognise.

Investigators also believe that some bodies were moved after burial. Evidence from multiple investigations indicates that efforts were made to conceal or destroy remains, including the use of lime and repeated disturbance of burial sites. Such actions complicate both archaeological recovery and DNA identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/901429b132cd3c5e2e6af8d201ca646c" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sanjurjo, a Communist party member and publishing house employee, was pregnant when she disappeared in 1977. Another obstacle is the limited documentary record. Military archives remain incomplete, and many official denials issued during and after the dictatorship have proved unreliable. As a result, forensic teams often have to combine fragmentary documents, survivor testimony, historical research and genetic evidence before reaching conclusions that can withstand legal scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gub.uy/institucion-nacional-derechos-humanos-uruguay/comunicacion/publicaciones/desaparicion-forzada-informe-sobre-denuncias-casos-investigacion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gub.uy">[Gub.uy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gub.uy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Desaparición forzada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Informe sobre denuncias y casos en investigación &#124; Institución Nacional de Derechos Humanos y Defensoría del PuebloMay 14, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 14, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why every identification matters

Each successful identification changes more than one family's story. It also tests historical claims against physical evidence.

For decades, some disappearances existed only in testimony and documentary records. Recovering remains from military land provides independent forensic confirmation of crimes that were once officially denied. It allows prosecutors to refine investigations, historians to reconstruct events more accurately and families to hold funerals that had been impossible for generations.

The identification of Amelia Sanjurjo illustrates this process. Her disappearance had long been documented by relatives and human rights organisations, but the recovery and DNA confirmation of her remains transformed a documented allegation into a verified forensic finding linked to a specific burial site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gub.uy/fiscalia-general-nacion/comunicacion/noticias/identifican-restos-hallados-batallon-14" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gub.uy">[Gub.uy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gub.uy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Identifican restos hallados en el Batallón 14 &#124; Fiscalía General de la Nación</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 28, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 28, 2024</span></span></span>

## The mystery that still remains

The central mystery today is no longer whether enforced disappearances occurred. That question has been answered by court proceedings, documentary research and repeated forensic discoveries.

Instead, the unanswered questions are intensely personal and specific. Where are the remaining victims buried? Were all clandestine graves located within known military sites, or were some moved elsewhere? Which official records still exist but have not been disclosed? Can every victim eventually be identified through advances in forensic science and DNA analysis?

Uruguay's search continues because every newly discovered grave demonstrates both progress and the scale of what remains unresolved. The discoveries at Battalion 14 have shown that truth can emerge even after nearly half a century, but they have also confirmed that many families are still waiting for the same answer: where their missing relatives were finally laid to rest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gub.uy/institucion-nacional-derechos-humanos-uruguay/comunicacion/publicaciones/desaparicion-forzada-informe-sobre-denuncias-casos-investigacion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gub.uy">[Gub.uy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gub.uy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Desaparición forzada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Informe sobre denuncias y casos en investigación &#124; Institución Nacional de Derechos Humanos y Defensoría del PuebloMay 14, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 14, 2026</span></span></span>
