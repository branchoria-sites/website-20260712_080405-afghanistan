## Introduction

[Malaysia]({{ 'malaysia/' | relative_url }}) Airlines Flight MH370 disappeared on 8 March 2014 while flying from Kuala Lumpur to Beijing with 239 people on board. More than a decade later, it remains one of aviation's greatest unresolved mysteries, not because there is no evidence, but because the single most important piece of evidence—the main wreckage on the seabed—has not been found. Investigators know far more about the aircraft's final journey than many people realise. Military radar, satellite communications, recovered debris and ocean-drift modelling all point towards a crash in the southern Indian Ocean. Yet those same sources cannot answer the central questions of who diverted the aircraft, why it left its planned route, or precisely how the flight ended. The continuing search is therefore not simply about finding an aeroplane; it is about recovering the flight recorders and physical evidence needed to explain one of the defining mysteries in Malaysia's modern history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-search-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atsb.gov.au">[ATSB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atsb.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mh370 search overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MH370 search overviewWith the publication of the final report The Operational Search for MH370 on 3 October 2017, the ATSB ceased to...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2017</span></span></span>

## The final confirmed movements of the aircraft

The last routine radio communication with air traffic control occurred shortly after the aircraft departed Malaysian airspace. Soon afterwards, its secondary surveillance radar transponder stopped transmitting. This did not make the aircraft invisible: Malaysian military radar continued tracking an unidentified aircraft that matched MH370 as it turned back across the Malay Peninsula, flew northwest past Penang and continued towards the Andaman Sea before disappearing beyond radar coverage.

Those radar observations established that the aircraft did not suffer an immediate catastrophic accident after communications were lost. Instead, it remained under controlled flight while making a series of deliberate changes in direction. The official Malaysian safety investigation concluded that these manoeuvres were most consistent with manual control of the aircraft, although it could not determine who was at the controls or why the route changed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-search-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atsb.gov.au">[ATSB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atsb.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mh370 search overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MH370 search overviewWith the publication of the final report The Operational Search for MH370 on 3 October 2017, the ATSB ceased to...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2017</span></span></span>

After radar contact ended, investigators relied on a different source of information: automated "handshakes" between the aircraft's satellite communications system and an Inmarsat satellite. These signals did not reveal the aircraft's exact position. Instead, they produced a sequence of distance measurements—known as satellite arcs—showing where the aircraft could have been at successive moments.

Combined with timing analysis and measurements of signal frequency changes caused by relative movement (known as burst frequency offset), these data strongly indicated that the aircraft continued flying south for several more hours before fuel exhaustion ended the flight somewhere in the southern Indian Ocean. This satellite analysis transformed the search by shifting it from Southeast Asia to one of the most remote stretches of ocean on Earth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-search-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atsb.gov.au">[ATSB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atsb.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mh370 search overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MH370 search overviewWith the publication of the final report The Operational Search for MH370 on 3 October 2017, the ATSB ceased to...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2017</span></span></span>

## What debris and satellite data establish

The absence of the main wreckage has sometimes led to the mistaken belief that no physical evidence exists. In reality, dozens of pieces of debris have been recovered from coastlines around the western Indian Ocean.

The most important discovery came in July 2015, when a flaperon—a movable section of the Boeing 777's wing—was found on Réunion Island. Investigators confirmed it originated from MH370. Other aircraft components later recovered from locations including Madagascar, Mozambique, Tanzania and Mauritius were either confirmed or judged highly likely to belong to the missing aircraft.

These finds establish several important facts.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* MH370 almost certainly ended in the Indian Ocean rather than elsewhere.
* The aircraft broke apart on impact with the sea.
* Ocean currents carried floating debris westwards over many months, broadly matching predictions from drift models.

</div>

However, the debris cannot identify the exact crash location. Ocean currents are complex, and although sophisticated modelling can estimate probable origin areas, small uncertainties grow substantially over months of drifting. Different modelling approaches therefore identify overlapping but not identical source regions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-operational-search-reports" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atsb.gov.au">[ATSB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atsb.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ATSBMH370 operational search reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This report details the results of a comprehensive attempt to use drift modelling to inform efforts to locate the missing Malaysia Ai...</span></span></span>

Researchers from Australia's Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation (CSIRO) refined these drift models by combining recovered debris locations with improved knowledge of Indian Ocean currents. Their work narrowed the range of plausible impact areas compared with earlier estimates but did not identify a unique point on the seabed. Instead, the modelling strengthened the case that the highest-probability search area lies close to the seventh satellite arc derived from the Inmarsat data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-operational-search-reports" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atsb.gov.au">[ATSB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atsb.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ATSBMH370 operational search reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This report details the results of a comprehensive attempt to use drift modelling to inform efforts to locate the missing Malaysia Ai...</span></span></span>

## Why repeated seabed searches have failed

Finding a large aircraft on land would normally be straightforward. Finding one beneath several kilometres of water across an immense, rugged seabed is a different problem altogether.

The multinational search coordinated largely by Australia surveyed roughly 120,000 square kilometres of ocean floor using sonar and autonomous underwater vehicles. This operation involved years of painstaking mapping in an area where underwater mountains, deep trenches and rough terrain complicated interpretation of sonar images. Despite being one of the largest underwater searches ever conducted, no confirmed wreckage was located.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-search-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atsb.gov.au">[ATSB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atsb.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mh370 search overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MH370 search overviewWith the publication of the final report The Operational Search for MH370 on 3 October 2017, the ATSB ceased to...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2017</span></span></span>

Private marine robotics company Ocean Infinity mounted another search in 2018 using improved autonomous technology but also failed to locate the aircraft. New analyses of satellite data, debris drift and possible flight-path refinements later prompted Malaysia to approve additional "no find, no fee" searches in newly defined priority zones.

The latest targeted campaign, conducted during 2025 and early 2026, again used autonomous underwater vehicles to investigate a carefully selected search area in the southern Indian Ocean. Although thousands of square kilometres of seabed were examined, the operation ended without locating confirmed wreckage. In mid-2026 Malaysia nevertheless extended its agreement with Ocean Infinity to allow further work in the remaining target area, reflecting official confidence that credible search locations still exist despite previous failures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oceaninfinity.com/news/conclusion-of-the-search-for-malaysian-airlines-flight-mh370/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oceaninfinity.com">[oceaninfinity.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oceaninfinity.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ocean InfinityConclusion of the search for Malaysian Airlines flight...8 Mar 2026 — On 23 January 2026, Ocean Infinity departed the sear...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 2026</span></span></span>

## Why the main wreckage still matters

The aircraft's disappearance has generated numerous theories, ranging from pilot action and unlawful interference to technical malfunction and more speculative conspiracy claims. The official investigation could not determine which explanation was correct because the decisive evidence remains missing.

Recovering the main wreckage would answer several crucial questions.

First, the cockpit voice recorder and flight data recorder could reveal the aircraft's systems, crew actions and conversations during the final hours, assuming they remained readable.

Second, examination of impact damage could clarify whether the aircraft descended under some degree of control or entered the sea in an uncontrolled dive.

Third, structural evidence could identify any pre-impact fire, explosion or mechanical failure that might have influenced events.

Without those findings, investigators can only judge competing explanations against the available indirect evidence rather than prove any single scenario. The Malaysian safety investigation therefore avoided assigning responsibility while noting that the aircraft's diversion was probably achieved through manual manipulation of the controls. It specifically did not conclude that pilot suicide, terrorism, mechanical failure or any other popular explanation had been demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-search-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atsb.gov.au">[ATSB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atsb.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mh370 search overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MH370 search overviewWith the publication of the final report The Operational Search for MH370 on 3 October 2017, the ATSB ceased to...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2017</span></span></span>

## What remains uncertain—and what is not

One reason MH370 continues to attract speculation is that certainty and uncertainty coexist unusually closely.

The strongest evidence supports several conclusions with considerable confidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The aircraft intentionally deviated from its planned route.
* It continued flying for several hours after communications ceased.
* Satellite analysis places its final journey on a southern track into the Indian Ocean.
* Confirmed debris demonstrates that the aircraft entered the ocean.

</div>

By contrast, the following questions remain unanswered:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Who altered the aircraft's course?
* What motive, if any, lay behind the diversion?
* What occurred during the final hours of flight?
* What was the aircraft's exact point of impact?
* Where are the main wreckage and flight recorders?

</div>

The mystery therefore lies less in where investigators should look than in the absence of the one body of evidence capable of resolving those remaining uncertainties.

## Why MH370 remains one of Malaysia's defining mysteries

MH370 occupies a unique place in Malaysia's history because it combines an unprecedented international search with an unusually large body of indirect evidence that still stops short of definitive explanation. Few historical mysteries have been investigated with comparable technical sophistication, involving radar reconstruction, satellite engineering, underwater robotics, oceanography and forensic examination of recovered aircraft parts.

Each new search narrows the range of possibilities rather than resetting the investigation. The evidence increasingly constrains where the aircraft almost certainly ended its journey, yet until the main wreckage is recovered, the fundamental human questions—who, why and exactly how—remain unanswered. That unresolved gap between extensive knowledge and missing proof is what continues to make MH370 one of the world's most enduring aviation mysteries and one of the most significant unresolved events in modern Malaysian history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-search-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atsb.gov.au">[ATSB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atsb.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mh370 search overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MH370 search overviewWith the publication of the final report The Operational Search for MH370 on 3 October 2017, the ATSB ceased to...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2017</span></span></span>
