## Why was Baku’s Maiden Tower built?

The Maiden Tower dominates Baku’s walled old city, but its original purpose remains disputed. UNESCO describes the visible monument principally as a 12th-century structure built over much earlier remains, possibly dating from the seventh or sixth centuries BC. That distinction is crucial: the tower seen today may combine several phases of construction rather than represent a single building created for one purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/958/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The most ancient monument of Icherisheher is the Maiden Tower – symbol of the city...Read more...</span></span></span>

The tower’s form does not settle the matter. It is a massive cylindrical structure with unusually thick walls and a projecting buttress-like section. Its restricted entrance, elevated position and commanding view are consistent with defence or observation. Yet its internal arrangement, wells, ventilation features and relationship to Baku’s ancient shoreline have encouraged alternatives, including use as a sanctuary, astronomical structure, beacon or fire-related religious monument.

A defensive interpretation is attractive because the tower eventually formed part of Baku’s fortified landscape. It could have watched the approaches to the harbour and provided a protected position during attack. The difficulty is that parts of its design are awkward for a conventional medieval fortress. The interior is divided into levels, but its capacity for housing a large garrison or mounting extensive defensive equipment appears limited.

The religious theory draws strength from the wider history of the Absheron Peninsula, where naturally escaping gas produced persistent flames. Baku’s region preserves evidence of pre-Islamic Iranian religious influence, including traditions associated with sacred fire. Nevertheless, resemblance to a ritual tower is not proof that the Maiden Tower itself was a Zoroastrian temple. Many confident identifications depend on symbolic readings made long after its construction rather than on securely dated ritual objects found inside it.

The most defensible conclusion is that the surviving tower probably acquired different uses over time. An earlier structure may have been incorporated into a major medieval rebuilding, after which the monument served defensive, observational and perhaps signalling functions. Its final medieval form need not reveal the purpose of everything beneath it.

The famous story of a maiden throwing herself from the tower belongs to legend rather than documented architectural history. Several versions exist, usually involving a woman escaping an unwanted marriage or an oppressive ruler. The tale explains the name emotionally, but it does not establish how the building was originally known or used. The tower became “mysterious” partly because folklore supplied a human drama where archaeology could offer only fragments.

## What happened to the fortress beneath Baku Bay?

Just offshore from Baku lie the remains of [Bayil Castle]({{ 'bayil-castle/' | relative_url }}), also called Sabayil Castle. Often promoted as the “Atlantis of the Caspian”, it is not a wholly lost city and does not require a legendary civilisation to explain it. It was a substantial medieval fortified complex associated with the rulers of Shirvan, built during the 13th century and later overwhelmed or isolated by changes in the Caspian shoreline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.advantour.com/azerbaijan/baku/bail-castle.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: advantour.com">[advantour.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">advantour.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bayil Castle near BakuAlong the entire upper part of the Bayil Castle ran a strip of inscriptions in Arabic and Farsi, together...</span></span></span>

The ruins attracted serious archaeological attention because hundreds of carved stone panels were recovered from the water. Together they once formed an extensive decorative and inscriptional band. Surviving portions include writing in Arabic and Persian, royal names, human heads, animals and imaginary creatures. Several fragments give a construction date in the 1230s and identify the architect, making the site far less mysterious than its modern nickname suggests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.advantour.com/azerbaijan/baku/bail-castle.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: advantour.com">[Advantour]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">advantour.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bayil Castle near BakuAlong the entire upper part of the Bayil Castle ran a strip of inscriptions in Arabic and Farsi, together...</span></span></span>

What remains uncertain is the complex’s precise role. It has been described as a sea fortress, royal residence, harbour installation, customs post or ceremonial structure. Its walls and towers clearly had defensive value, but the elaborate sculptural programme implies that it was also designed to proclaim dynastic authority. It may have guarded Baku’s maritime approach while serving as a visible statement of the Shirvanshahs’ power.

Its disappearance is also more complicated than a single dramatic sinking. Caspian Sea levels have varied greatly over historical time, repeatedly altering coastlines and exposing or covering low-lying structures. An earthquake has often been blamed for the castle’s destruction, while a medieval account has been interpreted as saying that one of Baku’s offshore fortifications was damaged during the Mongol period. These explanations are not necessarily exclusive: warfare, structural collapse, erosion and rising water could all have contributed at different stages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabayil_Castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sabayil Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sabayil Castle</span></span></span>

The genuinely unresolved part is not whether the fortress existed, but how it fitted into medieval Baku. Many recovered stones are weathered, the inscriptional sequence is incomplete and parts of the complex remain inaccessible or poorly understood. Reconstructing the full decorative text could clarify the building’s patrons, political message and intended audience.

## Why was a Roman centurion near the Caspian Sea?

At Gobustan, south-west of Baku, a Latin inscription records a man named Lucius Julius Maximus, a centurion of the Twelfth “Thunderbolt” Legion, under the emperor Domitian. Since Domitian ruled from AD 81 to 96, the carving provides firm evidence that Roman military personnel reached the western Caspian region in the late first century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AQobustan_inscription.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Qobustan inscription.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsLatin text: &quot;Imp(eratore) Domitiano / Caesare Aug(usto) / Germanic(o)...</span></span></span>

The inscription itself is not coded. The mystery lies in what the soldier was doing there. The legion’s principal base was far to the west, in the Roman Empire’s eastern frontier system. Gobustan was beyond the empire’s normally administered provinces, in or near the ancient kingdom known as Caucasian Albania. A Roman centurion’s presence so far east therefore requires explanation.

Several possibilities have been proposed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A military expedition:** Roman forces may have moved through the Caucasus during operations connected with client kingdoms and frontier security.
* **A diplomatic or reconnaissance mission:** A small detachment could have travelled without any attempt to conquer or occupy the region permanently.
* **Protection of a route:** Roman personnel may have been concerned with communications or movement between the Black Sea, Caucasian passes and Caspian shore.
* **An isolated posting:** The inscription may record a temporary visit by a limited group rather than a full legionary advance.

</div>

There is no corresponding Roman fort, settlement or large body of military material at Gobustan that demonstrates long-term occupation. The carving should therefore not be treated as evidence that Rome annexed Azerbaijan or established a permanent Caspian province. It proves presence, not the scale or purpose of that presence.

The most plausible interpretation is a limited mission connected with Rome’s involvement in the kingdoms of the South Caucasus. The region mattered strategically because influence there could affect routes around the Black Sea and Caspian, relations with Parthia, and access through the Caucasus. But with only a brief inscription and little supporting Roman material, the centurion’s exact orders remain unknown.

## What do Gobustan’s prehistoric boats really show?

Gobustan contains thousands of rock carvings produced over a very long period. They depict people, animals, hunting, dancing and boats, preserving traces of changing landscapes and ways of life around the Caspian. UNESCO recognised the area as a World Heritage landscape because of both the quantity of its images and the long cultural sequence they represent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1076rev.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centregobustan rock art cultural landscape</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Heritage Centregobustan rock art cultural landscape</span></span></span>

The boat carvings are particularly intriguing because the surrounding country is now semi-arid. Their presence is best understood against major prehistoric changes in Caspian water levels and local ecology. Research indicates that boat images were carved across an extended period rather than in one episode, suggesting a lasting relationship between communities and nearby aquatic environments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0140196316302385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

Norwegian explorer Thor Heyerdahl popularised a much more ambitious theory. He believed similarities between Gobustan boats and Scandinavian rock art might point to ancient movements of people from the Caucasus towards northern Europe. He also connected Azerbaijan with traditions concerning the Norse god Odin.

These claims gained attention because Heyerdahl was a celebrated adventurer and because visual comparisons between ancient boats can appear striking. They have not, however, produced convincing archaeological proof of a Caucasus-to-Scandinavia migration. Similar boat forms can arise because early watercraft faced comparable practical constraints. A long-distance migration theory would require converging evidence from securely dated artefacts, settlement patterns, ancient DNA and historical linguistics, not resemblance alone.

Gobustan’s carvings still present real research problems. Rock art is notoriously difficult to date directly, and later carvings can overlap or imitate earlier ones. The boats may represent different vessel types, periods and symbolic meanings. Some could record ordinary travel or fishing; others may have held ritual significance. The evidence supports prehistoric boating on or near the Caspian far more strongly than it supports journeys connecting Azerbaijan with Bronze Age Scandinavia.

Other markings at Gobustan have been reinterpreted as gaming boards rather than calendars or ritual diagrams. Archaeologist Walter Crist identified patterns resembling the ancient game usually called Fifty-eight Holes, known across Egypt and western Asia during the second millennium BC. The identification is plausible but depends heavily on shape and archaeological context because no rules or playing pieces survive beside the carvings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/culture/culture-desk/what-we-learn-from-one-of-the-worlds-oldest-board-games" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker What We Learn from One of the World&#x27;s Oldest Board Games</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker What We Learn from One of the World&#x27;s Oldest Board Games</span></span></span>

## Who built Baku’s fire temple?

The fire temple at Surakhani, outside Baku, is frequently presented as an ancient Zoroastrian sanctuary. The reality is more interesting and more complicated. The surviving complex was largely constructed in the early modern period and contains inscriptions associated with Indian Hindu and Sikh visitors and merchants. Its central flame was fuelled by natural gas emerging from the ground, one of the geological phenomena behind Azerbaijan’s enduring “land of fire” image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/370398211_KTSSaraoTemple_of_Eternal_Fiire_at_Baku" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) KTS.Sarao.Temple of Eternal Fiire at Baku</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) KTS.Sarao.Temple of Eternal Fiire at Baku</span></span></span>

The confusion arises because sacred fire was important in several traditions. The Absheron Peninsula had longstanding Iranian religious connections, while merchants from the Indian subcontinent travelled through the Caspian trade network and established places of worship abroad. The architecture at Surakhani resembles a fortified inn or religious residence surrounding a central altar, and its inscriptions invoke Hindu deities, sacred flame and Sikh religious language.

It is possible that the site had pre-existing local sanctity before the current buildings were erected. Natural flames would have attracted attention long before the surviving inscriptions were carved. Yet the available physical and written evidence does not securely establish an uninterrupted ancient Zoroastrian temple continuing unchanged into modern times.

The strongest interpretation is that Surakhani became a shared or successively used sacred landscape. Indian worshippers constructed and maintained much of the visible complex in the 17th and 18th centuries, possibly at a place already associated with fire. Zoroastrians later visited it and incorporated it into their own cultural memory. Calling it simply “Hindu” or simply “Zoroastrian” conceals the movement of merchants, pilgrims and ideas along the trade routes linking India, Iran, the Caucasus and Russia.

The flames themselves also became part of the mystery. Natural gas once reached the surface without modern equipment, but industrial exploitation altered underground pressure and the original fire eventually ceased. The flames displayed at the monument today are supplied rather than an unbroken survival of the ancient natural phenomenon. This does not make the site fraudulent; it shows how a geological wonder became a historic religious monument and then a curated symbol of national heritage.

## How was a lost Caucasian language recovered?

One of Azerbaijan’s most substantial manuscript mysteries concerns Caucasian Albania, an ancient and medieval kingdom that covered much of the eastern South Caucasus. It was unrelated to the modern Balkan state of Albania. Early accounts indicated that its Christian communities possessed a distinct alphabet, but for centuries almost no readable literature in that script was known.

A list of its letters was identified in a medieval manuscript in the 1930s. Excavations at Mingachevir in Azerbaijan later uncovered short inscriptions resembling the alphabet, but the texts were too brief to permit confident reconstruction of the language. The breakthrough came from two manuscripts held at Saint Catherine’s Monastery on Mount Sinai. They were palimpsests: pages whose original writing had been erased and overwritten, while traces of the lower text remained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/personal/jg/pdf/jg2007g.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: titus.uni-frankfurt.de">[uni-frankfurt.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">titus.uni-frankfurt.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Titus Achtung!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Titus Achtung!</span></span></span>

Multispectral photography and detailed linguistic comparison allowed scholars to recover substantial portions of a Christian lectionary and the Gospel of John in Caucasian Albanian. Roughly 120 pages of underlying text provided enough material to identify grammar, vocabulary and relationships with surviving Caucasian languages. Research links the language closely to Udi, still spoken by small communities in Azerbaijan and elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/personal/jg/pdf/jg2007g.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: titus.uni-frankfurt.de">[uni-frankfurt.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">titus.uni-frankfurt.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Titus Achtung!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Titus Achtung!</span></span></span>

The decipherment resolved the basic question of whether a literary language had existed. It also transformed isolated finds at Mingachevir from mysterious symbols into evidence belonging to a wider written tradition. But major uncertainties remain. Only a fraction of the literature once produced in the language has survived, and scholars cannot yet reconstruct the full history of its use, regional variation or disappearance.

The subject is also politically sensitive. The heritage of Caucasian Albania is invoked in modern disputes about churches, inscriptions and cultural ownership in the South Caucasus. Linguistic decipherment can identify a text’s language, but it cannot by itself validate sweeping modern territorial claims. Medieval communities changed language, religious allegiance and political affiliation over time. A church might pass between communities, be rebuilt repeatedly or preserve art from more than one tradition.

The responsible mystery is therefore narrower than nationalist retellings suggest: how much Caucasian Albanian literature once existed, where it circulated and why so little survived. Further palimpsests or inscriptions could answer those questions. Until then, the Sinai pages remain a rare case in which modern technology restored a voice that had been physically erased from parchment.

## Which explanations deserve the most confidence?

Azerbaijan’s historical mysteries fall into three broad categories.

**Problems likely to yield to further evidence** include the complete reconstruction of Bayil Castle’s inscriptions, the chronology of individual Gobustan carvings and the recovery of additional Caucasian Albanian texts. Better underwater archaeology, imaging and dating methods could materially change what is known.

**Questions that may remain permanently open** include the Maiden Tower’s earliest function and the purpose of the Roman mission recorded at Gobustan. The relevant buildings and inscriptions survive, but the explanatory records may never have existed or may have been destroyed centuries ago.

**Stories created mainly by later interpretation** include the Maiden Tower suicide legend, the description of Bayil Castle as a Caspian Atlantis and claims that Gobustan proves a prehistoric migration to Scandinavia. These stories form part of public memory, but they are not equal in evidential status to excavated masonry, dated inscriptions or deciphered manuscripts.

The best-supported picture is not of Azerbaijan as a storehouse of inexplicable wonders. It is of a region repeatedly crossed by armies, traders, religions and languages. Its enduring enigmas arise from that complexity. Towers were rebuilt over earlier foundations, shorelines shifted, manuscripts were scraped clean, and monuments were adopted by new communities. The missing pieces remain fascinating precisely because enough evidence survives to test the stories told about them.
