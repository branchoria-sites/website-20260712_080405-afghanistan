## Introduction

These cases remain puzzling for different reasons. Some were investigated under an authoritarian government that controlled courts, police and public information. Others suffer from contradictory testimony, missing records or physical evidence that lies underwater or has deteriorated. In archaeology, the mystery comes not from concealment but from the difficulty of reconstructing ancient behaviour from disturbed deposits. Taken together, they show that Malawi’s unresolved past is often less about a complete absence of evidence than about evidence distorted by power, poor preservation and the limits of later investigation.

## Who carried out the Chilobwe murders?

Between 1968 and 1970, a succession of killings terrified Chilobwe and other communities around Blantyre. Victims were attacked at night, often inside poorly secured homes, and some bodies were mutilated. At least 31 deaths were eventually attributed to the same campaign, although the precise total and whether every killing was connected remain uncertain. The apparent absence of a clear motive, combined with the police’s failure to stop the attacks, created an atmosphere in which rumour became almost as influential as evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/523638" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Chilobwe Murders Trialby P Brietzke · 1974 · Cited by 20 — In the first confession, Kawisa alleged that he and twelve other peop...</span></span></span>

One persistent story claimed that blood or body parts were being collected for South Africa as part of a secret arrangement involving Hastings Banda’s government. There is no credible evidence for that theory. Its importance lies instead in what it reveals about public distrust. Banda maintained unusually close relations with apartheid South Africa, while political dissent inside Malawi was heavily restricted. In that setting, an unexplained series of mutilation murders could easily be interpreted as a government conspiracy.

President Banda offered a political theory of his own, blaming opponents associated with the 1964 Cabinet Crisis. Former minister Henry Chipembere and other government critics were drawn into allegations that the killings were intended to destabilise the country. Yet these accusations were never supported by a transparent investigation capable of separating criminal evidence from the regime’s broader campaign against dissent.

The authorities eventually charged Walla Laini Kawisa. Physical evidence connected him to an attempted break-in and possibly to a small number of murder scenes, but the prosecution’s larger case depended heavily on confessions made during approximately 19 months in detention. Those statements contradicted one another. At different times Kawisa claimed to have acted with a group, implicated political figures and described the use of substances supposedly capable of silencing victims. The prosecutor reportedly accepted that, without the confessions, the evidence connected him to only three of the murder incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/523638" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Chilobwe Murders Trialby P Brietzke · 1974 · Cited by 20 — In the first confession, Kawisa alleged that he and twelve other peop...</span></span></span>

In 1971, a Southern Regional Traditional Court nevertheless found Kawisa solely responsible for 31 murders and 15 attempted murders. He was sentenced to death and denied a normal appeal because he had pleaded guilty. A reviewing court upheld the conviction but simultaneously expressed doubt that one person could have committed all the crimes. Kawisa was reportedly executed in 1972, although no clear public announcement settled even that final detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilobwe_murders" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Chilobwe murders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chilobwe murders</span></span></span>

The best-supported conclusion is that Kawisa probably had some involvement, but the claim that he alone committed the entire series is doubtful. Unidentified fingerprints, the scale of the attacks, contradictory confessions and the courts’ own reservations all leave open the possibility of accomplices or of several unrelated killers being combined into one official case. Claims of South African blood collection or elaborate opposition plots belong to political folklore, not demonstrated fact. The genuine mystery is narrower and more disturbing: how many crimes Kawisa actually committed, who else may have participated and whether political convenience ended the investigation prematurely.

## The Mwanza Four: murder proved, responsibility unresolved

On 18 May 1983, four prominent political figures died in Mwanza District: Malawi Congress Party secretary-general Dick Matenje, ministers Aaron Gadama and David Chiwanga, and Member of Parliament Twaibu Sangala. The Banda government announced that they had died in a road accident. Because all four had recently fallen under political suspicion, many Malawians doubted that account from the beginning. Human-rights reporting later noted claims that the men had been arrested and that witnesses had observed injuries inconsistent with an ordinary crash.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/worldreports/world.89/malawi.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human Rights WatchMalawiIn 1983, Attati Mpakati, the leader of a dissident group, was killed in Zimbabwe, A previous attempt had resulted...</span></span></span>

The case could not be openly re-examined while Banda remained in power. After Malawi’s transition to multiparty democracy, an independent commission was appointed in 1994. Reporting in January 1995, it concluded that the four men had been murdered rather than killed accidentally. The finding transformed a long-suppressed suspicion into an official conclusion and led to criminal proceedings against Banda, senior politician John Tembo and others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Independent_Commission_of_Inquiry_Into_t.html?id=_oVR--QaK94C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[google.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Books Malawi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Commission of Inquiry into the Mwanza Accident25 Nov 2024 — Independent Commission of Inquiry Into the 1983 Mwanza Incident: Chaired by M...</span></span></span>

The later court case established an important distinction between historical probability and proof beyond reasonable doubt. Malawi’s Supreme Court accepted that there was very strong evidence that the victims had been brutally murdered at Thambani. It did not, however, find sufficiently reliable evidence to uphold criminal responsibility against the accused. Testimony concerning meetings, orders and the movement of the victims contained weaknesses, inconsistencies or gaps that made it unsafe to convert the commission’s broader reconstruction into murder convictions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sdnp.org.mw/judiciary/criminal/MWANZA%20MURDER%20CASE%20SC.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sdnp.org.mw">[SDNP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sdnp.org.mw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">strict of Mwanza on the 18t May 1983. That outcome is sometimes misleadingly described as proving that no state operation occurred. It did no such thing. The judicial position was that murder was strongly established, while the prosecution had failed to prove the guilt of particular defendants to the criminal standard. A commission of inquiry can assess patterns, probabilities and political context; a criminal court must decide whether admissible evidence proves each accused person’s participation beyond reasonable doubt.

The remaining questions are therefore about command and execution. Who ordered the killings? Which security personnel transported and killed the men? How far did responsibility extend through the political hierarchy? The passage of more than a decade before a serious inquiry began allowed memories to fade, records to disappear and possible participants to coordinate or revise their accounts.

The Mwanza case matters because it became a public test of whether post-authoritarian Malawi could expose crimes committed under one-party rule. The commission supplied a persuasive historical verdict, but the failed prosecutions denied families and the wider public a final legal allocation of responsibility. Commemorations of the [Mwanza Four]({{ 'mwanza-four/' | relative_url }}) consequently serve not only as memorials to four victims but as reminders of the difference between knowing that a political crime occurred and being able to prove who ordered it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://search.worldcat.org/title/The-Mwanza-accident-documentary/oclc/55657541" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: search.worldcat.org">[WorldCat]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">search.worldcat.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on worldcat.org.</span></span></span>

## Why did the *MV Vipya* sink?

On 30 July 1946, the passenger and cargo vessel *MV Vipya* capsized during a storm on Lake Malawi, then usually called Lake Nyasa in colonial records. About 145 passengers and crew died, making it the deadliest known shipping disaster in the country’s history. The ship had been built in Scotland, transported in sections to south-east Africa and reassembled for service on the lake. It had completed only a few scheduled journeys before it sank near the northern lakeshore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Vipya" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MV Vipya</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MV Vipya</span></span></span>

The broad mechanism is not mysterious: the vessel encountered severe weather, took on water, overturned and sank. The uncertainty concerns why a relatively new ship was so vulnerable and which decisions turned dangerous conditions into catastrophe.

An initial inquiry criticised the captain’s seamanship. Accounts indicated that crew members and passengers had warned of deteriorating weather, but the vessel continued. Questions were also raised about the opening of a cargo hatch and whether water could enter through inadequately protected openings. Survivors further described concerns about the ship’s stability and construction. An added upper structure fitted during reassembly may have increased its top weight, while hatches and companionways were alleged not to have been sufficiently watertight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Vipya" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MV Vipya</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MV Vipya</span></span></span>

The inquiry’s findings did not settle the matter. The captain’s representatives and the Nyasaland Railways Company challenged the conclusions, and the East African Court of Appeal later cleared the captain of negligent seamanship. Accounts of the proceedings indicate that testimony from surviving African crew members changed, but the available public record does not provide a satisfactory explanation of why. The reversal left several possible causes competing with one another: navigational error, poor weather judgement, alterations made during assembly, basic design weaknesses, or a combination of all four.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Vipya" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MV Vipya</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MV Vipya</span></span></span>

Much of the physical evidence remains beyond easy reach. The wreck has not been comprehensively documented through a modern published underwater investigation, and its precise position is commonly reported as uncertain. No bodies were recovered. Without a detailed survey of the hull, later investigators cannot readily establish how quickly water entered, whether structural failure occurred or how the added upper works affected stability.

The most plausible explanation is therefore cumulative rather than conspiratorial. A powerful lake storm exposed deficiencies in operating decisions and perhaps in the ship’s configuration. Colonial inequalities also shaped the surviving record: passengers travelled in segregated accommodation, and African survivor testimony did not always carry the same institutional authority as that of company officials and European officers.

The disaster entered Malawian public memory through survivor accounts, memorials and literature, including work by writer Steve Chimombo. A memorial in Mangochi records the 145 lives lost. The continuing mystery is not whether a storm sank the vessel, but why official investigations produced no durable agreement about responsibility and why such a major wreck has never yielded a definitive modern reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Vipya" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MV Vipya</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MV Vipya</span></span></span>

## Was Mwanganda’s Village really an elephant-kill site?

Mwanganda’s Village, near Karonga in northern Malawi, offers a different kind of mystery. Excavations beginning in the 1960s uncovered stone artefacts near the remains of an elephant. Researchers initially interpreted the association as evidence that Middle Stone Age people had killed or butchered the animal. The site became an important example in debates about early human hunting and the exploitation of very large animals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/african-archaeology/bibliographic-references/F173FC0E74F38CDDFC6B244AEA087E26" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment Bibliographic references</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press &amp; Assessment Bibliographic references</span></span></span>

That interpretation depended on a seemingly simple assumption: because the tools and elephant bones were found together, the people who made the tools must have processed the carcass. Archaeological deposits are rarely so straightforward. Water, erosion, sediment movement and repeated occupation can bring materials from different events into the same layer. Bones may also be broken by natural pressure or animal activity rather than stone tools.

Renewed investigations used improved mapping, sediment analysis and dating methods to reconstruct how the deposits formed. The research suggested that Mwanganda was not best understood as a single, neatly preserved butchery episode. Instead, the area appears to contain evidence of repeated human activity along changing watercourses, with stone artefacts and animal remains accumulated or rearranged through complex geological processes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260280763_Renewed_Geoarchaeological_Investigations_of_Mwanganda%27s_Village_Elephant_Butchery_Site_Karonga_Malawi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Later work places Mwanganda within a wider Late Pleistocene landscape occupied at different times between roughly 58,000 and 15,000 years ago. The locality was attractive because rivers, wetlands and other resources persisted there while regional climates changed. The central historical question has consequently shifted. Researchers are now less concerned with whether one hunting party killed one elephant and more interested in how successive communities used a favourable corridor over thousands of years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277379122002694" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

This is not a case in which a sensational theory was exposed as a hoax. The original interpretation was a reasonable inference based on the methods and evidence then available. Later geoarchaeology showed that the association was less secure than it appeared. Mwanganda is valuable precisely because it demonstrates how archaeological “mysteries” are often solved: not by discovering a dramatic missing object, but by learning that objects found close together need not represent the same moment.

## Why Malawi’s archive leaves hard questions open

The uncertainty surrounding these cases is reinforced by the history of record-keeping itself. Malawi’s National Archives preserves government papers, manuscripts, newspapers and other documentary material, but the national record is uneven. Archivist Paul Lihoma has described “silences” created by colonial administration, institutional disruption, poor records management, natural deterioration and the political conditions of the one-party era. Some records were never created, some were removed or destroyed, and others survive in overseas repositories rather than Malawi.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.taylorfrancis.com/content/chapters/oa-edit/download?identifierName=doi&amp;identifierValue=10.4324%2F9781003003618-8&amp;type=chapterpdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.taylorfrancis.com">[taylorfrancis.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.taylorfrancis.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Taylor &amp; Francis APIsilences in malawi&#x27;s archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis APIsilences in malawi&#x27;s archives</span></span></span>

An archive is therefore not a neutral store containing everything that happened. Governments usually preserve the records of their own institutions more effectively than the experiences of poor residents, political prisoners or communities subjected to official suspicion. Under an authoritarian system, sensitive files may be classified, altered, deliberately kept informal or never written down. Later investigators can find themselves trying to reconstruct decisions from fragments produced by the very institutions whose conduct is in question.

This helps explain why oral testimony is so important in Malawi’s historical mysteries. Survivors of the *Vipya*, residents frightened by the Chilobwe murders and relatives of political victims preserved details that official accounts omitted. Yet oral evidence also changes with time. Memory can be influenced by later political developments, public rumours and repeated retelling. Neither the written archive nor personal recollection is automatically reliable; the strongest historical reconstruction emerges when they can be checked against each other.

## What is genuinely unresolved?

Malawi’s major historical enigmas should not all be treated alike.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The Chilobwe murders remain substantially unsolved.** Kawisa was probably connected to some crimes, but the evidence that he committed the entire series alone is weak.
* **The Mwanza Four were murdered.** The central uncertainty is who ordered and carried out the killings, not whether the deaths were an ordinary road accident.
* **The *MV Vipya* was lost in a storm.** What remains disputed is the balance between human error, design or assembly problems, and institutional responsibility.
* **Mwanganda’s Village is an archaeological reinterpretation, not an unsolved crime.** The old elephant-butchery model has given way to a more complex account of repeated occupation and disturbed deposits.
* **Grand conspiracy theories are unnecessary.** The best explanations involve authoritarian secrecy, unreliable prosecutions, incomplete archives, hazardous conditions and the ordinary difficulty of recovering past events.

</div>

## Why these mysteries still matter

These cases endure because each concerns more than an unanswered factual question. Chilobwe records the fear that grows when public safety collapses and official explanations cannot be trusted. Mwanza represents the unfinished reckoning with political violence under Hastings Banda. The *Vipya* embodies both a national tragedy and the unequal structures of colonial transport and inquiry. Mwanganda shows how scientific knowledge changes when better methods challenge an attractive earlier story.

They also warn against two opposite mistakes. One is to accept official conclusions simply because they appear in court judgments or government reports. The other is to fill every gap with rumours of ritual murder, foreign plots or omnipotent secret services. Malawi’s unresolved history is most revealing when fact, probability and speculation remain clearly separated.

The surviving evidence rarely offers the satisfaction of a final culprit, a recovered wreck or a perfectly reconstructed ancient event. It does, however, establish meaningful boundaries. Some explanations are well supported, others remain possible, and several popular stories have little evidence at all. That disciplined uncertainty is not a failure of history. It is the most honest account the record presently allows.
