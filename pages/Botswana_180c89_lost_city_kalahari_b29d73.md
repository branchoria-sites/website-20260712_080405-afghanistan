## Introduction

The Lost City of the Kalahari is one of southern Africa’s best-known exploration legends, but the mystery today is not whether a forgotten civilisation lies beneath the sands of [Botswana]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-botswanas/' | relative_url }}). The real question is whether the Victorian explorer Guillermo Antonio Farini genuinely saw ancient ruins or misinterpreted an extraordinary natural rock formation. More than a century of expeditions has failed to uncover archaeological evidence of a buried city, yet uncertainty over Farini’s exact route across the wider Kalahari has prevented the story from being settled beyond all doubt. As a result, the legend survives as a fascinating example of how exploration, geology and myth became intertwined across the borderlands of modern Botswana, Namibia and South Africa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.co.za/doi/10.10520/AJA00382353_1297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.co.za">[Journals.co.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.co.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 1, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 1965</span></span></span>

## Did Farini Really Find a Lost Kalahari City?

The legend began in 1885 when the Canadian showman William Leonard Hunt, travelling under the name Guillermo Antonio Farini, crossed part of the Kalahari with his adopted son, Lulu. After returning to Europe, he published *Through the Kalahari Desert*, describing what he believed were the remains of an ancient stone-built settlement partly buried beneath drifting sand. He compared the feature to a ruined city, noting wall-like structures, flat-sided blocks and what appeared to be mortar between them. His photographs and a paper presented to the Royal Geographical Society gave the account considerable publicity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_City_of_the_Kalahari" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lost City of the Kalahari</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lost City of the Kalahari</span></span></span>

Farini never claimed to have excavated buildings or uncovered artefacts. Instead, his conclusions rested almost entirely on what he observed on the surface. Even in his own account, there were no inscriptions, pottery, tools, graves or other objects that archaeologists would normally expect from a substantial settlement. That absence of supporting evidence became increasingly important as later expeditions failed to relocate the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_City_of_the_Kalahari" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lost City of the Kalahari</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lost City of the Kalahari</span></span></span>

The geographical setting also became part of the mystery. Farini's journey crossed regions that today lie within Botswana, Namibia and South Africa. His maps and travel descriptions contain inconsistencies, making it difficult for historians to reconstruct his precise route. Because no one can identify his path with complete confidence, later searchers have disagreed over where the supposed ruins should even be sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.co.za/doi/10.10520/AJA00382353_1297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.co.za">[Journals.co.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.co.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 1, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 1965</span></span></span>

## Why Searchers Never Found a Buried City

Farini's account inspired decades of expeditions. During the twentieth century, explorers, surveyors and amateur researchers repeatedly crossed the Kalahari looking for the lost city. Aircraft, improved mapping and later satellite imagery all failed to reveal convincing traces of an extensive ancient settlement.

Despite numerous searches, investigators never discovered:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* foundations belonging to large stone buildings;
* occupation layers showing long-term human settlement;
* pottery, metal objects or carved stone;
* cemeteries or ritual structures;
* inscriptions identifying an unknown civilisation.

</div>

The failure to recover even basic archaeological evidence steadily weakened the case for a buried city. Modern archaeology in southern Africa has documented many genuine precolonial settlements, including impressive stone-built centres elsewhere in the region, demonstrating that ancient architecture certainly existed—but not where Farini claimed to have found it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_City_of_the_Kalahari" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lost City of the Kalahari</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lost City of the Kalahari</span></span></span>

## The Geological Explanation

The strongest explanation emerged in the 1960s through the work of South African researcher A. J. Clement. Rather than searching randomly across the desert, Clement carefully reconstructed what he believed to be Farini's actual route. His conclusion was that Farini had probably travelled along a different path from the one generally assumed by later explorers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.co.za/doi/10.10520/AJA00382353_1297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.co.za">[Journals.co.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.co.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 1, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 1965</span></span></span>

Near Rietfontein, close to today's Botswana–Namibia–South Africa border region, Clement identified remarkable formations of weathered dolerite—an igneous rock that fractures into straight-edged blocks. Over millions of years, erosion exposed these rock walls while removing softer surrounding material. The result is a landscape where long, angular ridges resemble collapsed masonry, complete with stacked blocks and apparent "walls".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.co.za/doi/10.10520/AJA00382353_1297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.co.za">[Journals.co.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.co.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 1, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 1965</span></span></span>

Several details closely matched Farini's description:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* long linear rock formations extending for hundreds of metres;
* block-like stones resembling cut masonry;
* natural fractures that can look like mortar joints;
* weathering patterns creating the illusion of collapsed structures.

</div>

Clement argued that an experienced geologist would recognise the formations as natural, but a nineteenth-century traveller encountering them unexpectedly could reasonably mistake them for ruins. His interpretation remains the most widely accepted explanation among historians and geologists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.co.za/doi/10.10520/AJA00382353_1297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.co.za">[Journals.co.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.co.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 1, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 1965</span></span></span>

## Why the Legend Still Survives

Although the geological explanation is persuasive, it does not answer every question. The principal uncertainty concerns Farini's route rather than the rocks themselves. Because his travel records contain contradictions, historians cannot prove beyond doubt that Clement's dolerite outcrops are the exact formations Farini observed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.co.za/doi/10.10520/AJA00382353_1297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.co.za">[Journals.co.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.co.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 1, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 1965</span></span></span>

This small uncertainty leaves just enough room for speculation. Some enthusiasts continue to argue that Farini discovered genuine ruins that have since been buried by shifting sands or remain hidden elsewhere in the Kalahari. However, no expedition has produced archaeological evidence supporting those claims despite more than a century of searching.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_City_of_the_Kalahari" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lost City of the Kalahari</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lost City of the Kalahari</span></span></span>

The legend also reflects the intellectual climate of the late nineteenth century. European audiences were captivated by stories of "lost civilisations" hidden in Africa, Asia and South America. Discoveries such as Great Zimbabwe encouraged speculation that other monumental settlements might await discovery. In that atmosphere, Farini's dramatic description attracted far more attention than a simple geological curiosity would have received.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_City_of_the_Kalahari" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lost City of the Kalahari</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lost City of the Kalahari</span></span></span>

## What the Best Evidence Suggests

Today, the evidence points towards a much narrower mystery than popular retellings often suggest.

The well-supported conclusions are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Farini almost certainly encountered an unusual landscape feature during his 1885 journey.
* He sincerely believed it resembled the remains of an ancient city.
* No convincing archaeological evidence has ever been found for a buried settlement matching his description.
* Weathered dolerite formations provide a credible and well-documented natural explanation.
* The precise location of Farini's observation remains uncertain because his recorded route cannot be reconstructed with complete confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.co.za/doi/10.10520/AJA00382353_1297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.co.za">[journals.co.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.co.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 1, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 1965</span></span></span>

</div>

Rather than representing a lost civilisation beneath Botswana's sands, the Lost City of the Kalahari is now best understood as an enduring exploration mystery shaped by ambiguous observation, imperfect Victorian mapping and the remarkable ability of unusual geology to resemble human construction. That combination of genuine uncertainty and convincing natural explanation explains why the story continues to occupy a distinctive place in the historical folklore of the wider Kalahari.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.co.za/doi/10.10520/AJA00382353_1297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.co.za">[Journals.co.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.co.za</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 1, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 1965</span></span></span>
