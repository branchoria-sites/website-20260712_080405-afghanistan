## Introduction

Was ancient Punt located in modern [Somalia]({{ 'what-secrets-still-shape-somalias-past/' | relative_url }})? The most accurate answer is that **Somalia was probably part of the wider commercial world associated with Punt, but no surviving evidence proves that Punt lay entirely within Somalia's modern borders**. Egyptian inscriptions describe Punt as a wealthy source of incense, ebony, ivory, gold and exotic animals, yet they never define its borders in a way that can be matched to a modern map. Over the past two decades, archaeological science, environmental studies and renewed analysis of Egyptian records have narrowed the possibilities towards the southern Red Sea and the Horn of Africa. Even so, scholars continue to debate whether Punt was a single kingdom, a federation of coastal communities, or a broader trading region extending across parts of present-day Eritrea, Djibouti, Ethiopia and Somalia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33319742/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Mummified baboons reveal the far reach of early Egyptian mariners</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mummified baboons reveal the far reach of early Egyptian mariners - PubMedDecember 15, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2020</span></span></span>

## What Egyptian records actually say

The mystery begins with Egyptian texts rather than local inscriptions. Punt appears in records from the Old Kingdom onwards, becoming especially famous through the reliefs commissioned by Queen Hatshepsut in the fifteenth century BCE at her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahri.

These scenes show Egyptian ships arriving at Punt and returning with valuable cargoes including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Living myrrh trees with their roots intact.
* Frankincense and aromatic resins.
* Ebony and other hardwoods.
* Ivory.
* Gold.
* Leopard skins.
* Monkeys and baboons.
* Exotic livestock.

</div>

The reliefs also depict local houses raised on stilts, distinctive vegetation and the rulers of Punt, but they contain **no geographical coordinates or political boundaries**. Egyptian writers clearly regarded Punt as a real trading partner reached by sea, yet their descriptions are too general to identify a single modern country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heiup.uni-heidelberg.de/catalog/view/355/494/110740" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heiup.uni-heidelberg.de">[heiup.uni-heidelberg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heiup.uni-heidelberg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Entre mers—Outre-mer &#124; Heidelberg University Publishing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Entre mers—Outre-mer &#124; Heidelberg University Publishing</span></span></span>

This absence of precise geographical information explains why the debate has continued for well over a century. The Egyptian evidence establishes the existence of a prosperous Red Sea trading partner but leaves historians to reconstruct its location indirectly.

## How trade goods and baboons narrow the search

The strongest arguments linking Somalia with Punt come from environmental evidence rather than from written records.

Northern Somalia has been one of the world's major natural sources of **frankincense** and **myrrh**, aromatic resins that were among Egypt's most prized imports. Egyptian demand for incense was enormous because it played a central role in temple rituals, embalming and elite ceremonies. The distribution of these trees makes the Horn of Africa a much stronger candidate than regions lacking such resources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335329912_The_African_Incense_Trade_and_Its_Impacts_in_Pharaonic_Egypt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Another important clue comes from baboons.

In 2020, researchers analysed oxygen and strontium isotope ratios preserved in mummified baboons imported into ancient Egypt. These chemical signatures act rather like geological fingerprints, reflecting where an animal spent its early life. The study concluded that New Kingdom baboons most likely originated from an area encompassing modern Ethiopia, Eritrea, Djibouti and parts of Somalia and Yemen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33319742/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Mummified baboons reveal the far reach of early Egyptian mariners</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mummified baboons reveal the far reach of early Egyptian mariners - PubMedDecember 15, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2020</span></span></span>

This was an important advance because it excluded many earlier theories placing Punt much farther away. However, it did **not** identify a single location. Instead, it showed that the animals came from a broad region surrounding the southern Red Sea.

The baboon evidence therefore supports the idea that Somalia participated in Punt's trading network, but it does not demonstrate that the political centre of Punt lay within Somalia itself.

## Why Somalia remains a serious candidate

Several features make northern Somalia a credible component of Punt.

First, its coastline occupies a strategic position on ancient Red Sea and Indian Ocean shipping routes. Long before the Classical period, sailors moving between Egypt, Arabia and eastern Africa would naturally have encountered ports along the Somali coast.

Second, Somalia possessed products repeatedly associated with Punt:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Frankincense.
* Myrrh.
* Ivory obtained through inland trade.
* Exotic animals.
* Access to African hardwoods and other luxury goods through regional exchange.

</div>

Third, later historical sources demonstrate that the Somali coast supported prosperous international trading settlements. Although these belong to a much later period than the Egyptian expeditions, they show that the region had the environmental and maritime characteristics expected of an ancient trading centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335329912_The_African_Incense_Trade_and_Its_Impacts_in_Pharaonic_Egypt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

These arguments do not prove that Punt was Somalia, but they explain why Somalia consistently appears in serious scholarly discussions.

## Why no modern country can claim Punt alone

The greatest obstacle is the assumption that Punt must correspond to a single modern state.

Ancient political geography rarely worked that way. Trade networks often crossed today's national borders, and Egyptian names for foreign lands sometimes referred to economic regions rather than tightly defined kingdoms.

Current scholarly interpretations generally fall into three broad possibilities:

* **A single kingdom centred in Eritrea or neighbouring regions.** Some archaeological and environmental evidence has strengthened this interpretation in recent years.
* **A wider Horn of Africa trading zone** extending across parts of Eritrea, Djibouti, Ethiopia and Somalia.
* **A changing commercial network** whose principal ports shifted over centuries while Egyptians continued using the same name for their trading partner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholar.sun.ac.za/handle/10019.1/1564" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholar.sun.ac.za">[sun.ac.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholar.sun.ac.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scholar SUNProfiling Punt: using trade relations to locate &#x27;God&#x27;s Land</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 1, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2008</span></span></span>

These explanations are not mutually exclusive. Punt may have referred to a political authority at one time and a broader commercial sphere at another.

Modern national identities also complicate public discussion. Somalia's Puntland region deliberately takes its name from the ancient land, reflecting a regional historical tradition rather than archaeological proof. Likewise, neighbouring countries sometimes emphasise evidence supporting their own connections with Punt. The archaeological record itself does not justify exclusive ownership by any present-day nation.

## What evidence would solve the mystery?

The decisive discovery has never been made.

Researchers would like to find one or more of the following:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* An inscription from Punt identifying its rulers and location.
* A securely excavated port directly linked with Egyptian expeditions.
* Egyptian artefacts discovered in an unquestionable Punt context.
* Additional scientific analyses of traded animals, plants or timber from known archaeological sites around the Horn of Africa.

</div>

Unfortunately, political instability, limited archaeological excavation and the difficulty of investigating coastal sites that have changed dramatically over three thousand years mean that such evidence remains elusive.

## The most balanced judgement

The question is no longer whether Somalia had connections with the world described in Egyptian records. The archaeological, environmental and scientific evidence makes that highly plausible. The real mystery is whether Punt was a kingdom centred on one location or a broader commercial landscape extending around the southern Red Sea.

Current evidence supports three well-founded conclusions:

* Somalia almost certainly formed part of the wider economic region from which Egypt obtained incense and other luxury goods.
* Scientific studies of traded baboons are consistent with a source area that includes parts of Somalia, but they do not isolate Somalia as the unique homeland of Punt.
* No inscription, archaeological site or scientific discovery has yet demonstrated that Punt belonged exclusively to modern Somalia, or to any other single present-day country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33319742/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Mummified baboons reveal the far reach of early Egyptian mariners</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mummified baboons reveal the far reach of early Egyptian mariners - PubMedDecember 15, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2020</span></span></span>
