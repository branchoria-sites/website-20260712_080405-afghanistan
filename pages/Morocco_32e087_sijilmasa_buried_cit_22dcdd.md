## Introduction

Sijilmasa is often described as a "lost city", but that description is misleading. The real mystery is not that the city vanished overnight, but that centuries of rebuilding, abandonment and reuse have left several different Sijilmasas layered across the same landscape. Near modern Rissani in [Morocco]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-moroccos/' | relative_url }})'s Tafilalt oasis, archaeologists have found that the famous caravan centre changed shape repeatedly as political rulers, water systems and trade routes evolved. Instead of a single buried city waiting to be uncovered, the site preserves overlapping urban phases that are difficult to separate. This changing urban form explains why medieval written accounts, visible ruins and archaeological evidence sometimes appear to contradict one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/fr/sijilmasa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[gouv.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

## The caravan city at the edge of the Sahara

From the eighth century onwards, Sijilmasa became one of the western Sahara's most important trading centres. Caravans arriving from West Africa brought gold, slaves and other goods northwards, while textiles, metalwork and manufactured products travelled south. The city's position on the edge of the Tafilalt oasis made it both a commercial gateway and an agricultural settlement sustained by an elaborate irrigation network rather than by desert trade alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/fr/sijilmasa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[Archeologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

Yet the famous trading city was never static. Medieval dynasties repeatedly fought for control because whoever governed Sijilmasa controlled a strategic point in trans-Saharan commerce. Midrarids, Fatimids, Almoravids, Almohads and Marinids all left political and physical marks on the settlement. Instead of each regime constructing an entirely new city elsewhere, rebuilding commonly occurred within or beside earlier districts, creating successive layers of occupation that now overlap beneath the surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/fr/sijilmasa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[gouv.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

This continuous transformation explains why historical descriptions differ. Travellers writing centuries apart were often observing different versions of Sijilmasa rather than describing a single unchanging place.

## Why archaeologists cannot point to one buried city

The puzzle facing archaeologists is not simply locating Sijilmasa. Its approximate position has long been known. The challenge is determining which surviving walls, streets and buildings belong to which period.

Several factors make the site's urban history unusually difficult to reconstruct:

* **Earth-built architecture decays quickly.** Much of Sijilmasa was built from mud brick and rammed earth. When abandoned, these materials gradually collapse back into the ground, blurring the outlines of buildings and streets.
* **Repeated rebuilding obscured earlier layouts.** New walls, houses and religious buildings were frequently erected directly on top of older foundations rather than beside them.
* **Agricultural reuse altered the landscape.** Fields, irrigation channels and later villages reshaped parts of the former city over many centuries.
* **Later occupation continued after the medieval city's decline.** Mosques, religious schools, shrines and Alaouite structures occupied parts of the site, meaning that medieval remains sit beneath early modern construction rather than in an untouched archaeological layer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/fr/sijilmasa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[gouv.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

Because of these processes, excavation rarely reveals complete neighbourhoods. Instead, archaeologists recover fragments that must be combined with medieval chronicles, aerial imagery and studies of ancient water management to reconstruct the city's changing plan. The Moroccan-American Project at Sijilmasa in the late twentieth century, followed by the Moroccan-French archaeological programme begun in 2012, significantly improved understanding by examining the settlement as an evolving landscape rather than a single ruined monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/fr/sijilmasa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[gouv.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

### Reading an earthen city

Unlike stone-built Roman cities whose walls often survive to roof height, an abandoned earthen settlement gradually melts into the surrounding terrain. Wind erosion, seasonal floods and centuries of cultivation smooth the remains until streets become low ridges and collapsed buildings resemble natural mounds.

For that reason, researchers increasingly combine excavation with environmental evidence. Ancient canals, irrigation systems and agricultural plots reveal as much about Sijilmasa's urban growth as surviving buildings do. Studies suggest that expansion of the oasis and management of water were central to the city's prosperity and influenced how districts developed over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8306.1996.tb01746.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineSijilmassa: The Rise and Fall of a Walled Oasis in Medieval Morocco: Annals of the Association of American Geograp...</span></span></span>

## Trade decline, rebuilding and the myth of sudden disappearance

Popular accounts sometimes suggest that Sijilmasa mysteriously vanished beneath the desert. The evidence points to a slower and more complex process.

By the late fourteenth century the city's political importance had weakened amid repeated conflicts and changing regional power. At the same time, major trans-Saharan trade routes shifted, reducing Sijilmasa's position as the dominant northern terminus of western Saharan commerce. The decline was gradual rather than catastrophic. Medieval and early modern written sources describe a landscape increasingly organised around fortified villages across the oasis instead of one dominant urban centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/fr/sijilmasa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[gouv.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

Archaeological work supports this picture. Rather than complete abandonment after one destructive event, parts of the city continued to be occupied for religious and local purposes. Shrines, schools and other institutions appeared within sections of the former urban area, while neighbouring settlementsincluding the area around modern Rissaniabsorbed many of the region's economic and administrative functions. Later conflicts, including attacks in the early nineteenth century, contributed to the destruction of buildings still standing on the medieval site, but these occurred long after the city's principal commercial role had faded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/fr/sijilmasa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[Archeologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

The mystery therefore lies less in *where* Sijilmasa went than in understanding *which* Sijilmasa survives beneath today's landscape.

## What remains uncertain

Although archaeology has greatly clarified the site's history, several questions remain open.

Researchers continue to debate:

* the precise layout of different urban phases;
* how extensive the city's walls were during successive dynasties;
* which surviving structures belong to particular rebuilding campaigns;
* how residential, commercial and religious districts shifted as irrigation systems evolved;
* the extent to which medieval written descriptions represent the city at different moments rather than conflicting observations of the same settlement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8306.1996.tb01746.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[tandfonline.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineSijilmassa: The Rise and Fall of a Walled Oasis in Medieval Morocco: Annals of the Association of American Geograp...</span></span></span>

These uncertainties arise because the archaeological record is incomplete rather than because the city itself is mythical. Every excavation uncovers another stage in a settlement that was repeatedly transformed over more than six centuries.

## Why the changing city still matters

Sijilmasa demonstrates that some historical mysteries are created by gradual change rather than dramatic disappearance. The city's reputation as a "buried city" reflects the way earthen architecture, shifting trade networks and repeated rebuilding can erase clear boundaries between successive generations of urban life.

Its ruins preserve evidence of how medieval desert cities adapted to political upheaval, environmental constraints and long-distance commerce. Instead of searching for one lost metropolis beneath the sands, archaeologists are piecing together several overlapping cities, each representing a different chapter in the history of Morocco's greatest Saharan trading centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/fr/sijilmasa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[gouv.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archeologie Sijilmsa &#124; Archologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>
