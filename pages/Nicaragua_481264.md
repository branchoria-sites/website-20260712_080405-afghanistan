## Introduction

Four cases stand out because they combine genuine unresolved questions with unusually strong historical evidence. They also show why caution matters. [Sandino]({{ 'sandino/' | relative_url }})’s missing body has encouraged legends that go well beyond the surviving documents. La Penca produced rival accusations shaped by the Cold War. Acahualinca’s footprints were long misdated and romanticised as a flight from disaster. Zapatera’s statues are mysterious largely because removal, looting and limited excavation destroyed much of their original context.

## Sandino’s death is known, but his grave is not

The murder of Augusto César Sandino on 21 February 1934 is Nicaragua’s most politically important historical enigma. Sandino had led a long guerrilla resistance against the United States military presence and the US-organised Nicaraguan National Guard. After American forces withdrew in 1933, he reached a peace agreement with President Juan Bautista Sacasa but remained deeply mistrusted by the Guard and its director, Anastasio Somoza García.

Sandino travelled to Managua in February 1934 for negotiations with Sacasa. As he left the presidential residence, National Guardsmen detained him, his brother Sócrates and two senior followers. They were taken away and killed. Contemporary United States diplomatic reports recorded the immediate crisis between Sacasa and Somoza and later referred plainly to Somoza’s responsibility. One 1934 dispatch reported that Somoza had promised not to act against Sandino without the American minister’s consent; another recorded Sacasa’s embarrassment after Somoza effectively admitted responsibility. A US diplomatic discussion in 1935 described Somoza as “known to have been responsible” for Sandino’s death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d524" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">on February 21) that he would take no action against Sandino without my consent. The principal mystery is therefore not whether Sandino was assassinated or whether Somoza’s Guard carried out the killing. The surviving diplomatic record makes those points difficult to dispute. The unresolved question is what happened to Sandino’s body and the remains of the men killed with him.

Accounts commonly place the execution and initial burial around the Larreynaga district of Managua. Later stories claim that the bodies were secretly exhumed, moved or destroyed. One version says Sandino’s followers recovered him and hid the grave. Another says the Guard removed the remains years later, burnt them near Tiscapa and scattered the ashes. A still more dramatic tradition alleges that Sandino was decapitated and his head sent to the United States. His remains have never been conclusively identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusto_C%C3%A9sar_Sandino" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Augusto César Sandino</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Augusto César Sandino</span></span></span>

The evidence is not equal across these claims. A secret burial followed by removal or destruction is plausible because the Somoza regime had an obvious interest in preventing Sandino’s grave from becoming a place of pilgrimage. The story of a severed head sent abroad is much weaker. It survives principally as political lore rather than as a claim supported by a documented chain of custody, a verified witness account or an identified foreign archive.

The absence of a body became politically powerful. Sandino could not be commemorated at a conventional tomb, so his image, hat, silhouette and name became substitute relics. The organisation that overthrew the Somoza dynasty in 1979 called itself the Sandinista National Liberation Front, turning a concealed killing into the founding memory of a later revolution. A digital archive devoted to the Sandino rebellion now brings together thousands of military reports, letters, maps, photographs and oral histories, but even this extensive record has not produced a verified grave.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oxfordre.com/latinamericanhistory/view/10.1093/acrefore/9780199366439.001.0001/acrefore-9780199366439-e-77?rskey=ToSecI%26result%3D1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oxfordre.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oxfordre.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on oxfordre.com.</span></span></span>

The most responsible conclusion is narrow but important: the political authorship of Sandino’s murder is far less mysterious than popular retellings sometimes suggest. The true unresolved matter is the disposal of his remains. Unless a credible burial site, forensic identification or new archival document emerges, the exact sequence after the shooting will remain uncertain.

## La Penca and the bomber disguised as a journalist

On 30 May 1984, journalists gathered in a wooden structure at La Penca, a remote camp on the Nicaraguan side of the San Juan River. They were attending a press conference held by Edén Pastora, a former Sandinista commander who had broken with the revolutionary government and organised an armed opposition movement.

A man presenting himself as a Danish photographer named Per Anker Hansen entered with camera equipment. During the press conference, a bomb concealed in or near an aluminium camera case exploded. Seven people eventually died, including three journalists, and many others were wounded. Pastora, the likely target, survived. The supposed photographer disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Penca_bombing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Penca bombing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Penca bombing</span></span></span>

The use of a false journalistic identity made the attack particularly disturbing. It endangered not only those in the room but also reporters elsewhere in the region, because armed groups could thereafter treat cameras and press credentials as possible weapons. The bombing became both a murder investigation and an intelligence puzzle during one of the most secretive phases of the Nicaraguan conflict.

Early suspicion fell heavily on the United States Central Intelligence Agency and figures associated with the Contra war. Pastora was resisting pressure to merge his southern force with the larger, US-backed northern Contra organisation. Journalists Martha Honey and Tony Avirgan, who was injured in the blast, investigated the bombing and argued that people linked to the CIA-supported network were responsible. Costa Rican proceedings later focused on several individuals alleged to have had intelligence or Contra connections, but no prosecution delivered a final judicial account of the attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ticotimes.net/2025/06/08/la-penca-bombing-1984-nicaragua-mystery-unraveled" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ticotimes.net">[ticotimes.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ticotimes.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">la penca bombing 1984 nicaragua mystery unraveled</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">la penca bombing 1984 nicaragua mystery unraveled</span></span></span>

A competing explanation gained force in the 1990s. Photographs of the false journalist were said to resemble Roberto Vital Gaguine, an Argentine left-wing militant known by an alias meaning “Martin the Englishman”. This identification suggested that the bomber might have been connected not to the Contra network but to Sandinista or allied intelligence services. The identification was persuasive to some investigators, but it did not by itself prove who recruited him, who supplied the bomb or who selected the press conference as the place of attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalrepository.unm.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=12108&amp;context=notisur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalrepository.unm.edu">[UNM Digital Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalrepository.unm.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unm.edu.</span></span></span>

The Sandinista-linked theory received further support in 2009 when Swedish journalist Peter Torbiörnsson told Nicaraguan police that he had unwittingly helped the suspected bomber travel through the region. Torbiörnsson accused senior Sandinista and Cuban intelligence figures of organising the plot. His testimony was potentially significant because he described direct contact with the man posing as a photographer. Those accused did not provide a public account that resolved the allegation, and the case still did not progress to a conclusive trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ticotimes.net/2009/01/30/bombing-survivor-seeks-truth-closure" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ticotimes.net">[Tico Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ticotimes.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">bombing survivor seeks truth closure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">bombing survivor seeks truth closure</span></span></span>

Pastora himself offered changing or blended explanations. He initially suspected retaliation connected to CIA pressure, but later argued that interests on both sides of the conflict may have overlapped. Honey likewise acknowledged that the trail appeared to point in more than one direction. Such claims are not proof of a joint operation, but they reveal why the case resisted a simple Cold War division between a Sandinista culprit and a Contra culprit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ticotimes.net/2004/05/28/20-years-after-la-penca-bombing-more-questions-than-answers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ticotimes.net">[Tico Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ticotimes.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tico Times The Unsolved Mystery of La Penca: 20 Years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tico Times The Unsolved Mystery of La Penca: 20 Years</span></span></span>

Several propositions can be separated with reasonable confidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The attacker entered under a stolen or false journalistic identity.
* Pastora was the apparent target, although the choice of a crowded press conference showed disregard for civilian lives.
* The man photographed as “Hansen” is widely believed to have been Roberto Vital Gaguine, but this identification has not produced a complete legal finding.
* Evidence and testimony have implicated Sandinista intelligence, CIA-connected Contra networks, Cuban personnel and intermediaries moving between those worlds.
* No court has established a full command chain from the person who ordered the attack to the bomber who carried it out.

</div>

La Penca remains unresolved not because nothing is known, but because the available evidence stops at different points. There is a probable operative, several plausible networks and a clear political motive to remove Pastora. What is missing is the decisive link showing who authorised the operation and which organisation controlled it at the moment of the bombing.

## What the Acahualinca footprints really record

The human footprints preserved at Acahualinca in Managua present a very different kind of mystery. Construction workers uncovered them in 1874 in layers of volcanic mud and ash near Lake Managua. The trackway includes numerous human impressions, together with animal prints, and appears to record a group moving across soft ground before another deposit sealed the surface.

For many years the footprints were described as evidence of people fleeing a volcanic eruption. It is an unforgettable scene: families running while ash falls behind them. The problem is that the footprints themselves do not support the most dramatic version. Measurements of the spacing and form of the tracks indicate ordinary walking rather than a panicked sprint. Animal tracks cross the human trail, but they do not demonstrate that people and animals were escaping together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iugs-geoheritage.org/geoheritage_sites/the-human-footprints-of-acahualinca/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iugs-geoheritage.org">[iugs-geoheritage.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iugs-geoheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the human footprints of acahualinca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the human footprints of acahualinca</span></span></span>

Their age was also disputed. An earlier radiocarbon estimate, based on buried soil beneath the track layer, encouraged claims that the prints were roughly 5,000 or even 6,000 years old. Later geological work dated the volcanic sequence more directly and placed the footprints at about 2,100 years ago. A recent assessment gives a best estimate of roughly 2,000 to 2,200 years before the present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374643201_Acahualinca_Nicaragua_a_scientifically_significant_site_of_ancient_human_footprints_in_the_New_World" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

That revision does not make Acahualinca less valuable. The site contains an unusually large, accessible and well-preserved group of ancient human tracks. Unlike a pot or stone tool, a footprint records a brief physical act: a person placed a foot on wet ground, shifted weight and moved forward. Variations in size and stride preserve information about a group rather than an isolated individual.

The unanswered questions concern the circumstances. Researchers cannot identify the walkers, their destination or their relationship to nearby settlements. Nor can they prove whether volcanic activity had influenced their journey. Wet volcanic sediment may have resulted from an eruptive event or from ordinary environmental conditions following earlier deposition. The lack of running impressions weakens the popular escape story, but it does not show that the walkers were unaware of volcanic danger.

Acahualinca is therefore a useful warning about how historical mysteries are created. The genuine puzzle is modest: who crossed the ground, and why were they moving through that particular landscape? The grander story of terrified refugees appears to be an interpretation added later because it makes the footprints easier to narrate. Geological dating and gait analysis have solved part of the mystery while leaving the human moment intact.

## Zapatera’s statues lost their archaeological setting

Zapatera Island, a volcanic island in Lake Nicaragua, contains one of the country’s most important pre-Columbian archaeological landscapes. Nineteenth-century visitors recorded monumental basalt figures, petroglyphs, pottery and earthworks. The quantity and arrangement of the material suggest that parts of the island served as major ceremonial spaces, especially between approximately 800 and 1350 CE.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitanicaragua.com/en/petroglyphs-of-zapatera-island-granada/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitanicaragua.com">[Visita Nicaragua]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitanicaragua.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visita Nicaragua Petroglyphs of Zapatera Island – Granada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visita Nicaragua Petroglyphs of Zapatera Island – Granada</span></span></span>

The stone figures often combine human and animal forms. Some have been interpreted as gods, ancestors, rulers or ritual specialists. Their original placement around mounds and ceremonial areas may have formed part of a carefully organised sacred landscape. Yet confident identification is difficult because the people who made them left no surviving written explanation and because later researchers frequently tried to match the figures to better-documented religious traditions from Mexico.

That comparison is not unreasonable. Pacific Nicaragua participated in long-distance cultural networks and experienced migration from Mesoamerican-speaking populations. However, visual resemblance alone cannot establish the name, identity or precise function of a sculpture. A figure with animal features might represent a deity, a transformed human, an ancestor, a mythic being or a statement of political authority. Archaeological context is needed to distinguish among those possibilities.

Much of that context was lost. Statues were removed from Zapatera and placed in museums, government buildings or private collections. Smaller objects were dispersed, while looting disturbed sites before they could be excavated systematically. Even where a sculpture survives in good condition, its exact original position, associated offerings and relationship to nearby burials may be poorly recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zapatera_%28archaeological_site%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Zapatera (archaeological site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zapatera (archaeological site</span></span></span>

This creates an archival mystery as much as an artistic one. Nineteenth-century drawings and descriptions sometimes preserve information no longer visible on the island, but early explorers did not document sites according to modern archaeological standards. Researchers must reconstruct the ceremonial landscape from incomplete maps, old illustrations, displaced objects and limited later excavation.

Zapatera should not be presented as the work of a vanished unknown civilisation. Archaeologists can place its monuments within the broader pre-Columbian history of Nicaragua and neighbouring regions. The uncertainty lies in the details: which communities commissioned particular figures, what ceremonies occurred around them, how the monuments related to political power and whether later groups reused older sacred sites.

The damage caused by removal also explains why spectacular interpretations should be treated carefully. Claims about sacrifice, specific named gods or a single unified “statue cult” may be plausible in some cases, but they exceed what can be demonstrated for the whole island. The most enduring mystery is not how ancient people could carve the stone. It is how the sculptures functioned together before collectors separated them from the places that gave them meaning.

## Chamorro’s murder and the missing command chain

The assassination of newspaper editor Pedro Joaquín Chamorro Cardenal on 10 January 1978 helped bring the Somoza dictatorship towards its final crisis. Chamorro, director of the opposition newspaper *La Prensa*, was shot while driving in Managua. His death prompted an enormous funeral procession, unrest and a general strike. An Inter-American Commission on Human Rights report recorded that roughly 50,000 people accompanied his body and that serious disturbances followed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.divergentes.com/la-prensa-nicaragua-chamorro-director/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: divergentes.com">[Divergentes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">divergentes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Directors of La Prensa in the face of dictatorships</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Directors of La Prensa in the face of dictatorships</span></span></span>

The immediate killers and intermediaries were investigated, and the Somoza government promoted the explanation that the murder resulted from a private dispute involving Cuban-American businessman Pedro Ramos. Within days, however, the official inquiry was effectively closed after one accused participant claimed that Ramos had paid for the killing. Contemporary reporting noted both the speed of the closure and widespread public distrust.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1978/01/15/nicaragua-closes-probe-of-editors-assassination/9e1a68db-e52d-42bf-871d-231b63f609bd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Washington Post Nicaragua Closes Probe Of Editor&#x27;s Assassination</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington Post Nicaragua Closes Probe Of Editor&#x27;s Assassination</span></span></span>

The enduring question is whether the murder was merely a privately commissioned crime or whether members of the Somoza family, the National Guard or allied business interests authorised or protected it. Chamorro’s family and much of the opposition believed the dictatorship was responsible. The political circumstances made that suspicion understandable: Chamorro was the regime’s most prominent civilian critic, censorship and political violence were common, and the authorities had both motive and control over the investigation.

Suspicion, however strong, is not identical to documentary proof. The available public record establishes that the murder benefited Somoza’s opponents politically, not the regime. It united previously divided sectors against the dictatorship and turned Chamorro into a symbol of civil resistance. That disastrous outcome does not rule out regime involvement; authoritarian systems have often misjudged the consequences of political killings. It does mean that arguments based only on who benefited afterwards are unreliable.

The case differs from Sandino’s assassination. In Sandino’s case, contemporary diplomatic documents explicitly connect Somoza to responsibility. In Chamorro’s case, the disputed issue is the command chain above the convicted or accused participants. The official version was compromised by an investigation conducted under the very government suspected of involvement, while later revolutionary justice also operated in a highly politicised environment.

Chamorro’s murder remains historically unresolved at its most important level: not who fired the weapons, but who ordered the attack and whether state power shielded the organisers. Its larger significance is not uncertain. The killing destroyed much of the remaining confidence that peaceful political reform under Somoza was possible.

## Why Nicaragua’s mysteries remain difficult to settle

These cases survive because Nicaragua’s historical record has repeatedly been damaged by disaster, dictatorship, war and political upheaval. The Managua earthquake and fire of 1931 destroyed much of the national archive, including important colonial land records. Later conflicts scattered documents among government offices, private collections and foreign archives. United States repositories now preserve extensive diplomatic and military material relating to Nicaragua, while projects such as the Sandino Rebellion archive and the National Security Archive have assembled thousands of declassified documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nsa/publications/nicaragua/nicaragua.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[gwu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gwu.edu.</span></span></span>

Foreign archives are invaluable, but they have limits. American diplomats, Marines and intelligence officials recorded what they observed or were told, often through their own political assumptions. Their documents can reveal hidden conversations and contradict later propaganda, yet they cannot automatically recover unrecorded orders, clandestine burials or decisions made inside another state’s security apparatus.

Archaeology faces a parallel problem. Acahualinca survives because volcanic deposits preserved a moment, but footprints cannot explain themselves. Zapatera’s monuments survive physically, yet the removal of objects has erased relationships among sculpture, landscape and ritual activity. In both cases, modern methods can narrow the possibilities without restoring all the lost context.

The most useful distinction is between different levels of uncertainty:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Well established:** Sandino was killed by National Guardsmen; La Penca was a deliberate bombing; Acahualinca preserves ancient human tracks; Zapatera was an important ceremonial landscape; Chamorro was assassinated by hired gunmen.
* **Strongly supported but incomplete:** Somoza directed or accepted responsibility for Sandino’s killing; the La Penca bomber used a false identity and was probably an Argentine operative; the Acahualinca walkers moved at an ordinary pace; Zapatera’s statues were integral to organised ritual spaces.
* **Genuinely disputed:** the location of Sandino’s remains; the authority that ordered La Penca; the precise purpose of Zapatera’s figures; the ultimate sponsors of Chamorro’s murder.
* **Legend or weak speculation:** Sandino’s head being delivered abroad without documentary proof; Acahualinca’s entire group fleeing in panic from an eruption; confident identifications of every Zapatera figure as a named foreign deity; pirate-treasure stories unsupported by contemporary records.

</div>

Nicaragua’s historical mysteries matter because they concern ownership of the past. A missing grave can become the symbolic centre of a revolution. An unsolved bombing can preserve competing accusations from a secret war. A misread footprint can turn ordinary travel into catastrophe. A displaced statue can appear more enigmatic after collectors have removed the evidence that once explained it.

The best accounts do not try to make these mysteries larger than the evidence allows. They show precisely where knowledge ends. In Nicaragua, that boundary is often the most revealing part of the story: it exposes not only what has been lost, but also who had the power to hide, destroy, relocate or reinterpret the record.
