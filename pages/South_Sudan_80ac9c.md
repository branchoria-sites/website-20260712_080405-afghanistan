## Introduction

In each case, the central problem is not a complete absence of evidence. It is that the available evidence is fragmented, inaccessible or mistrusted. Official explanations compete with lived memory; oral traditions survive where written records have been lost; and objects or documents that might settle arguments are often beyond public reach. That makes South Sudan’s mysteries inseparable from its unfinished struggle over authority, justice and national identity.

## Why John Garang’s death still attracts suspicion

John Garang de Mabior led the Sudan People’s Liberation Movement and Army through much of the long civil war against the government in Khartoum. The 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement ended that conflict, created an autonomous southern government and established a route towards the referendum that eventually produced South Sudanese independence. Garang became Sudan’s first vice-president and appeared to many supporters to embody a radically different political future.

On 30 July 2005, after meeting Ugandan President Yoweri Museveni, Garang travelled towards southern Sudan aboard a Ugandan presidential Mi-172 helicopter. The aircraft crashed in mountainous country near New Cush in bad weather, killing Garang and everyone else aboard. The sudden death of the southern movement’s dominant leader, so soon after the peace agreement, immediately provoked rumours of assassination or sabotage. Violence erupted after the announcement, particularly in Khartoum, Juba and other towns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/atf/cf/%7B65BFCF9B-6D27-4E9C-8CD3-CF6E4FF96FF9%7D/Sudan%20NS%20S2005579.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: securitycouncilreport.org">[Security Council Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">securitycouncilreport.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sudan NS S2005579</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Security Council ReportS/2005/579September 15, 2005 — 12 Sept 2005 — severe test when the First Vice-President, John Garang, died on 30 J...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 15, 2005</span></span></span>

The official investigation concluded that the pilot had continued under visual-flight conditions as the weather deteriorated and had lost awareness of the aircraft’s position relative to the surrounding terrain. Ugandan officials described the primary cause as pilot error, compounded by poor weather and darkness. Flight and cockpit recorders recovered from the wreckage were sent for technical examination in Russia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/report/58764/sudan-uganda-garang-crash-resulted-pilot-error-says-report" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenewhumanitarian.org">[thenewhumanitarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenewhumanitarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Humanitarian Garang crash resulted from pilot error, says report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New HumanitarianGarang crash resulted from pilot error, says reportApril 18, 2006 — 18 Apr 2006 — Pilot error caused the helicopter c...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 18, 2006</span></span></span>

That explanation is plausible and consistent with a recognised form of aviation accident known as controlled flight into terrain: a functioning aircraft is unintentionally flown into the ground because its crew loses reliable situational awareness. There is no publicly established evidence that explosives, mechanical tampering or hostile action brought the helicopter down.

Yet the case remains contested for several reasons. Garang’s death transformed the balance of power at an exceptionally sensitive moment. The journey involved a foreign presidential aircraft, remote terrain and difficult weather. Early information was confused, and publication of the investigative findings was delayed, allowing suspicion to harden before the technical explanation became widely understood. Amnesty International called for independent third-party participation, warning that the inquiry had to be seen as credible as well as technically competent; it also stated that it had no indication at the time that the crash was anything other than accidental.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/afr540702005en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amnesty International...</span></span></span>

The distinction matters. There are legitimate questions about transparency, but questions are not proof of a plot. The strongest available evidence supports an accident caused by weather, navigational judgement and loss of terrain awareness. The continuing mystery is less “Who killed Garang?” than “Why did the official finding fail to produce public closure?”

Garang’s death also acquired significance because no one could know how South Sudan’s history might have unfolded had he lived. Supporters have imagined that he might have held the liberation movement together, prevented the later rivalry between Salva Kiir and Riek Machar, or pursued his original vision of a transformed and united Sudan rather than separation. These are counterfactual possibilities, not demonstrable facts. They nevertheless ensure that the crash remains a national turning point onto which later disappointment is projected.

## The missing people whose cases were never properly closed

Some South Sudanese mysteries concern people whose probable fate is grimly evident but whose families have never received bodies, official truth or legal accountability. These should not be treated as entertaining puzzles. Enforced disappearance is a human-rights violation in which state agents arrest or abduct someone and then conceal the person’s location or fate.

During the early 1990s, government forces responded to rebel attacks around Juba and elsewhere in Equatoria with arrests, killings and [disappearances]({{ 'disappearances-c24ce4/' | relative_url }}). A later study based on interviews with survivors and witnesses found that these cases continued to shape family life and public institutions decades later. Relatives were left without confirmation of death, burial places or the ability to complete mourning. Fear spread beyond the immediate families because friends and associates worried that asking questions might expose them to the same danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kpsrl.org/sites/kpsrl/files/publications/files/800_legacies_of_enforced_disappearances_in_south_sudan_ndash_briefing_paper.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kpsrl.org">[kpsrl.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kpsrl.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">800 legacies of enforced disappearances in south sudan ndash briefing paper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">800 legacies of enforced disappearances in south sudan ndash briefing paper</span></span></span>

The historical puzzle is therefore both individual and institutional. Exactly who ordered particular arrests? Where were detainees taken? Were they killed immediately, transferred elsewhere or buried in unmarked locations? In many cases there is no intact documentary trail. Some officials connected to earlier systems of coercion remained influential after the 2005 peace agreement and independence, while successive peace processes did little to investigate older abuses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kpsrl.org/sites/kpsrl/files/publications/files/800_legacies_of_enforced_disappearances_in_south_sudan_ndash_briefing_paper.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kpsrl.org">[kpsrl.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kpsrl.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">800 legacies of enforced disappearances in south sudan ndash briefing paper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">800 legacies of enforced disappearances in south sudan ndash briefing paper</span></span></span>

A more recent case shows how this pattern continued. Dong Samuel Luak, a lawyer and government critic, and Aggrey Idri, an opposition official, disappeared separately in Nairobi in January 2017. Amnesty International reported that the men were taken to a South Sudanese National Security Service facility in Juba before being removed again, after which their whereabouts were unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/AFR6562982017ENGLISH.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Dong Samuel Luak and Aggrey Idri</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Dong Samuel Luak and Aggrey Idri</span></span></span>

In 2019, a United Nations panel said it had corroborated evidence strongly suggesting that South Sudanese security personnel abducted the men in Kenya, transported them to South Sudan and executed them at a security facility in Luri. Human Rights Watch called for an independent investigation and access to the relevant detention sites. The British government likewise described the UN findings as deeply disturbing and urged South Sudanese authorities to respond.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africandefenders.org/it-is-time-for-accountability-for-the-enforced-disappearance-of-samuel-dong-and-aggrey-idri-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africandefenders.org">[africandefenders.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africandefenders.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on africandefenders.org.</span></span></span>

This leaves an important distinction between a legal mystery and an evidential one. The available evidence points strongly towards abduction and killing by security agents. What remains unresolved is the full chain of command, the role of any Kenyan officials, the location of the men’s remains and whether anyone will be held accountable.

Such cases persist in public memory because disappearance denies families even the limited certainty that follows an acknowledged death. It also turns record-keeping into a political battleground: the state’s refusal to produce a prisoner, register or burial record becomes part of the violence itself.

## The sacred staff that became a political object

One of South Sudan’s most unusual historical enigmas centres on a wooden staff associated with the nineteenth-century Nuer prophet Ngundeng Bong. The object, commonly described as Ngundeng’s *dang* or rod, is not mysterious because its existence is doubtful. Its documented movements can be traced with reasonable confidence. The uncertainty lies in what it means, who should control it and how later political actors have used traditions connected to it.

Ngundeng, who died in 1906, became a major religious and social figure among the Nuer. Accounts collected by historians describe him as preaching peace between communities, condemning destructive raiding and speaking through prayers and songs interpreted as prophecy. He established a large earthen shrine near Waat that attracted visitors from several communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://riftvalley.net/news/sudan-and-south-sudan/fate-ngungdengs-dang/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: riftvalley.net">[Rift Valley Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">riftvalley.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on riftvalley.net.</span></span></span>

The rod was made from tamarind root and bound with copper wire. Tradition associates it with an 1878 battle in which Ngundeng’s followers were said to have defeated an enemy amid a violent storm. Believers understood the event as evidence of spiritual power; historians cannot verify supernatural causation and instead treat such accounts as oral and religious tradition shaped by later retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e2300925846a694731e7627bb7a2f04e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The rivalry between Kiir and Machar, rooted in ethnic divisions, sparked a civil war in 2013 that killed an estimated 400,000 people. Alt...</span></span></span>

After Ngundeng’s death, the rod passed to his son Guek. Colonial forces killed Guek during the suppression of resistance in the late 1920s, and the object was carried away as a trophy. For decades its whereabouts were uncertain. Historian Douglas H. Johnson eventually located what he concluded was the rod in Bournemouth, bought it and returned it to southern Sudan in 2009. Riek Machar, then the most senior Nuer political leader in government, received it during a public ceremony in Juba.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://riftvalley.net/news/sudan-and-south-sudan/fate-ngungdengs-dang/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: riftvalley.net">[Rift Valley Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">riftvalley.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on riftvalley.net.</span></span></span>

The return solved one mystery but created others. Was the object now a national heritage item, a specifically Nuer religious possession or something held personally in trust by Machar? South Sudan had no established national museum capable of receiving it. Machar kept it at his residence and displayed it to visitors, according to Johnson. President Salva Kiir welcomed the repatriation but warned that the rod should not become a symbol for renewed war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e2300925846a694731e7627bb7a2f04e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The rivalry between Kiir and Machar, rooted in ethnic divisions, sparked a civil war in 2013 that killed an estimated 400,000 people. Alt...</span></span></span>

Its political importance grew because sayings attributed to Ngundeng were interpreted by some followers as predicting a future leader with personal features resembling Machar. Prophetic songs, however, survive through performance, translation and reinterpretation rather than through one authoritative text. Historians warn that their meaning is flexible, and political circumstances can alter how listeners understand them. A description applied to a leader after his rise may look more exact than it did beforehand.

The rod’s present location has again become unclear. Recent reporting indicates that archivists believe it may still be associated with Machar’s household, but it is not held by the national archive and has not been available for normal public examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e2300925846a694731e7627bb7a2f04e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The rivalry between Kiir and Machar, rooted in ethnic divisions, sparked a civil war in 2013 that killed an estimated 400,000 people. Alt...</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusions are limited:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The rod is a genuine historical object with a credible connection to Ngundeng’s family and colonial conquest.
* Claims that it produces supernatural effects belong to religious belief and oral tradition, not verifiable historical evidence.
* Its modern influence comes from what people believe it represents: authority, resistance, prophecy and political legitimacy.
* Unclear ownership and restricted access have allowed an important heritage object to become entangled in personal and factional politics.

</div>

The case is therefore not simply a legend about a magical staff. It is a dispute over whether sacred heritage can ever be separated from power.

## What lies beneath South Sudan’s underexplored landscape?

South Sudan’s archaeology presents a different kind of mystery: not a sensational discovery hidden from the world, but a vast historical record that has barely been investigated. War, poor infrastructure, seasonal flooding and limited funding have repeatedly interrupted fieldwork. As a result, large gaps remain between oral traditions, colonial descriptions and the material evidence beneath the ground.

Archaeological surveys have recorded prehistoric stone tools, rock shelters, pottery, iron-smelting remains, settlement mounds, fortified enclosures and traces of nineteenth-century trade and military activity. Around Wun Rok, researchers documented mounds that earlier observers had called Dinka “city mounds”. Excavations elsewhere examined homestead and cattle-camp sites whose repeated rebuilding produced raised occupation areas over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10090610/1/Davies_Kay%20et%20al%20final%20submission.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.ucl.ac.uk">[UCL Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCL Discovery</span></span></span>

A 2009 survey along the Nile corridor between Juba and Nimule recorded roughly 110 sites or groups of features. These included semi-fortified villages, terraces, stone walls, cairns, standing stones, pottery scatters and iron-working debris. The researchers also located a substantial late nineteenth-century Turco-Egyptian garrison at Laboré.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10090610/1/Davies_Kay%20et%20al%20final%20submission.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.ucl.ac.uk">[UCL Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCL Discovery</span></span></span>

These finds complicate the outdated image of South Sudan’s precolonial past as historically blank or almost entirely mobile. They point instead towards changing mixtures of pastoralism, farming, iron production, river travel, defensive settlement and long-distance exchange.

The unanswered questions are fundamental. How old are many of the mound settlements? Were some occupied continuously or rebuilt intermittently? What political organisations constructed fortified sites? How did communities respond to changes in the Nile, the Sudd wetlands and regional climate? What can iron furnaces and imported beads reveal about trade networks before formal colonial rule?

Claims of “lost cities” should be treated cautiously. The recorded remains do not establish a forgotten urban civilisation comparable to ancient Nubia farther north. Many sites may represent substantial villages, cattle settlements, trading posts or defensive centres rather than cities in the usual archaeological sense. The real mystery is more valuable than the inflated one: South Sudan contains a long and complex archaeological history whose basic chronology is still being assembled.

## Why so much of the past remains difficult to verify

All these cases share a practical obstacle: fragile and incomplete records. South Sudan’s national archive contains administrative documents dating mainly from the early twentieth century to the 1980s, including some of the only surviving detailed records of local government in the south. Approximately 5,000 provincial files were gathered from Upper Nile and Equatoria shortly before renewed civil war began in 1983.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://riftvalley.net/projects/sudan-and-south-sudan/national-archive-south-sudan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: riftvalley.net">[Rift Valley Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">riftvalley.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">national archive south sudan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">national archive south sudan</span></span></span>

The collection subsequently endured dispersal, heat, damp, insects, conflict and neglect. Conservation and digitisation projects have rescued and catalogued important material, but access and preservation remain uneven. UNESCO has described the archive as central to nation-building because it provides evidence from which South Sudanese history can be investigated rather than merely asserted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sudanmemory.org/cms/102/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sudanmemory.org">[sudanmemory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sudanmemory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sudanmemory.org.</span></span></span>

Archives are not neutral, complete memories. Colonial officials recorded what mattered to colonial government; military and security agencies often concealed abuses; rural communities preserved history orally; and civil war destroyed both documents and witnesses. Even a well-preserved file may present only the state’s view of an event.

This is why South Sudan’s historical enigmas demand several kinds of evidence at once: technical accident findings, survivor testimony, oral traditions, archaeological remains, diplomatic records and government files. None should automatically outrank all others. A crash recorder may explain an aircraft’s last minutes but not why citizens distrust the result. An oral tradition may preserve memories absent from official papers but change across generations. A security report may identify likely perpetrators without revealing the full command structure.

The most honest verdict is that South Sudan’s past is not mysterious because it lacks history. It is mysterious because so much history has been violently interrupted, privately held or incompletely preserved. The country’s enduring enigmas are therefore also questions about public authority: who is permitted to investigate, who controls the evidence, whose memories are believed and whether truth can emerge without accountability.
