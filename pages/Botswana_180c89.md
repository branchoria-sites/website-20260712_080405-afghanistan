## Introduction

These cases differ sharply in character and evidential strength. The “Lost City of the Kalahari” is probably an explorer’s error enlarged by later storytelling. Tsodilo’s so-called [python shrine]({{ 'python-shrine/' | relative_url }}) is a serious archaeological hypothesis, but its spectacular age claim remains disputed. The man buried in Gaborone after being repatriated from Spain was real, yet his name, homeland and even the appropriateness of his burial place are uncertain. Segametsi’s death, by contrast, is a documented crime whose unanswered questions damaged public confidence in Botswana’s institutions. Together, the mysteries show how uncertainty grows from incomplete records, contested interpretation, colonial collecting and official silence.

## Was there really a lost city in the Kalahari?

The Lost City of the Kalahari legend began with William Leonard Hunt, a Canadian showman and tightrope performer who travelled under the name Guillermo Antonio Farini. In 1885, Farini crossed part of the Kalahari with his adopted son, Lulu, later publishing *Through the Kalahari Desert*. His account described what appeared to be substantial ruins emerging from the sand: stone-like walls, passages and the remains of a vanished settlement. The book, photographs and a paper presented to the Royal Geographical Society transformed an ambiguous observation into one of southern Africa’s most persistent exploration stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.si.edu/digital-library/book/throughkalaharid00fari" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.si.edu">[Smithsonian Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian LibrariesThrough the Kalahari DesertThrough the Kalahari Desert; Creator: Farini, G Antonio...</span></span></span>

The mystery belongs to the wider Kalahari rather than neatly within Botswana’s modern borders. Farini travelled through territory associated with present-day Botswana, Namibia and South Africa, and later attempts to reconstruct his route have produced conflicting locations. That geographical uncertainty is central to the story. Searchers were not merely looking for ruins; they were trying to determine where Farini had actually been.

No expedition has produced convincing archaeological evidence of a buried city matching his description. There are no securely documented foundations, occupation layers, ceramics, graves, inscriptions or other material remains that would normally establish the existence of a major settlement. Later retellings sometimes introduce temples, unknown civilisations or an African “Atlantis”, but these additions go far beyond the evidence in Farini’s original account.

The most influential proposed solution came from geologist A. J. Clement, who retraced what he believed was Farini’s true route in the 1960s. Clement argued that the traveller had not penetrated as deeply into the Kalahari as later readers assumed. Near the Botswana–Namibia–South Africa border region, he identified weathered dolerite formations whose straight edges and stacked blocks could resemble ruined masonry. His conclusion was that Farini had seen a striking natural formation and interpreted it through the romantic expectations of nineteenth-century exploration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.co.za/doi/pdf/10.10520/AJA00382353_1297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.co.za">[Journals.co.za]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.co.za</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AJA00382353 1297</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Farini covers the long trek between. Lehututu and Ngamiland in a matter of 16 pages. After another 23 pages he is...Read more...</span></span></span>

That explanation is persuasive but not mathematically final. Farini’s route remains difficult to reconstruct, and no single rock formation has been proved beyond doubt to be the exact feature he described. The surviving mystery is therefore narrower than the legend suggests:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Farini almost certainly saw something unusual.
* There is no reliable evidence that it was an ancient city.
* Natural geology provides a credible explanation.
* Uncertainty about his route prevents an absolute identification.

</div>

The story endured partly because it fitted a colonial-era appetite for “lost civilisations”. Such narratives often treated monumental architecture as inexplicable unless it came from an unknown or supposedly vanished people. In reality, southern Africa has a rich and well-documented history of complex settlements and long-distance trade, including the stone-building traditions associated with Great Zimbabwe, Khami and sites such as Domboshaba in Botswana. The irony is that fascination with an imaginary Kalahari metropolis can distract from the region’s genuine archaeological record.

## Did people worship a stone python at Tsodilo 70,000 years ago?

Tsodilo Hills in north-western Botswana is unquestionably one of Africa’s great cultural landscapes. UNESCO records more than 4,500 rock paintings within roughly ten square kilometres, while archaeological deposits document human activity and environmental change over at least 100,000 years. The hills also remain spiritually important to local communities, whose traditions connect them with ancestors and sacred power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1021/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreTsodiloWith one of the highest concentrations of rock art in the world, Tsodilo has been called the &#x27;&#x27;Louvre...</span></span></span>

The controversy centres on a small chamber commonly called Rhino Cave. In 2006, archaeologist Sheila Coulson announced that a large rock inside the cave resembled the head of a python. Its surface contained numerous carved or hammered depressions. Stone points found nearby showed evidence of heating or burning, and fragments had apparently been deliberately broken. Coulson interpreted the setting as a ritual place associated with a python and suggested that some activity there could date back about 70,000 years. If correct, this would place organised ritual behaviour far earlier than many previously accepted examples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uio.no/english/research/research-news/apollon/articles/2006/python-english.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uio.no">[University of Oslo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uio.no</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Oslo World&#x27;s oldest ritual discovered</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Worshipped the python...1 Feb 2012 — A new archaeological find in Botswana shows that our ancestors in Africa engaged in ritual practice...</span></span></span>

The claim was memorable because several elements appeared to reinforce one another: the snake-like shape of the rock, the altered stone tools, the cave’s restricted position and the importance of powerful animals in later regional traditions. It offered a vivid picture of Middle Stone Age people gathering in a hidden chamber, striking the rock so that firelight animated its patterned surface.

Yet the evidence does not prove that complete story. Archaeologists familiar with earlier work at the site challenged both the dating and the interpretation. Among the principal objections were that the depressions may have been made at different times, that similar markings occur on other nearby rocks without forming recognisable animals, and that the excavated deposits do not securely connect every feature to a single 70,000-year-old ritual phase. A stone may resemble a python to modern observers without having been intended as one by its makers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.africanistarchaeology.net/s/Nyame-Akuma-Issue-067-Robbins.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanistarchaeology.net">[Society of Africanist Archaeologists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanistarchaeology.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Society of Africanist Archaeologistsbotswana</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of Africanist Archaeologistsbotswana</span></span></span>

Dating also requires care. A tool or sediment layer can be ancient without proving that a visible carving, a fire episode and an inferred ceremony are all equally old. Archaeological deposits are frequently disturbed, reused or accumulated over long periods. Establishing a ritual interpretation therefore requires more than demonstrating that humans visited the cave during the Middle Stone Age.

The fairest assessment is that Rhino Cave was an unusual and possibly symbolically important place, but the phrase “the world’s oldest ritual site” is stronger than the evidence permits. The finds support serious questions about early symbolic behaviour. They do not conclusively establish a continuous python cult or a precisely dated ceremony.

This distinction matters because Tsodilo does not need an archaeological superlative to be important. Its significance rests on an exceptionally long record of human presence, dense rock art, living traditions and relationships between landscape, memory and belief. The controversy is valuable precisely because it shows how archaeology moves from objects to interpretation—and how easily an attractive interpretation can become a public “fact” before specialists have reached agreement.

## Who was the man returned from the Banyoles museum?

In October 2000, human remains arrived at Sir Seretse Khama International Airport from Spain. They belonged to a man who had spent much of the twentieth century displayed in the Darder Museum in Banyoles as a taxidermied racial specimen. Museum visitors knew him only by a degrading nickname meaning “the Black man”. Botswana accepted the remains and buried them in Gaborone’s Tsholofelo Park.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://s3-euw1-ap-pe-df-pch-content-store-p.s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/9780203165775/49e6086f-758b-48cd-a010-b0ab7a15ee54/relatedobjects/chapters/chapter027/9780203165775-27.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&amp;X-Amz-Credential=REDACTED&amp;X-Amz-Date=20260605T091740Z&amp;X-Amz-Expires=172800&amp;X-Amz-Signature=REDACTED&amp;X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&amp;response-content-disposition=attachment%3B+filename%3D%2210.4324_9780203165775-27_chapterpdf.pdf%22" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: s3-euw1-ap-pe-df-pch-content-store-p.s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com">[Amazon S3]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">s3-euw1-ap-pe-df-pch-content-store-p.s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on amazonaws.com.</span></span></span>

The repatriation appeared to correct an obvious historical wrong. The man’s corpse had reportedly been taken in southern Africa during the early nineteenth century, preserved by French taxidermists and transported to Europe. After passing through private collections, it became a museum attraction. By the late twentieth century, campaigners argued that displaying a dead African person as a curiosity was incompatible with human dignity.

However, the return created new mysteries rather than resolving the old ones. No reliable record preserved the man’s name. Labels and published descriptions shifted over time, identifying him variously through broad and often inaccurate racial or ethnic categories. Even his precise place of origin was uncertain.

Historical research indicates that the body was probably obtained in the Cape frontier region in about 1830–31, beyond the borders of present-day Botswana. Neil Parsons and Alinah Kelo Segobye, who examined the case and its repatriation, showed how fragmentary documentation allowed the man’s identity to be repeatedly reconstructed according to European collecting practices and later political needs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://d.lib.msu.edu/pula/343/OBJ/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: d.lib.msu.edu">[d.lib.msu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">d.lib.msu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on msu.edu.</span></span></span>

The repatriation itself was also troubling. Botswana did not receive the intact preserved body once displayed in Banyoles. Spanish specialists removed artificial components and returned principally skeletal material. Scholars and commentators criticised the process as poorly communicated and insufficiently attentive to professional standards, community consultation and the dignity of the deceased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/28641975_El_Negro_El_Nino_Witchcraft_and_the_absence_of_rain_in_Botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) El Negro, El Niño, Witchcraft and the absence of rain</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) El Negro, El Niño, Witchcraft and the absence of rain</span></span></span>

A further controversy concerned destination. Botswana provided a state willing to receive and bury him, and the return carried powerful continental symbolism. But later research strengthened the argument that he had been taken from what is now South Africa, not Botswana. The burial therefore gave him dignity while possibly placing him in a country where he had never lived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2019/mar/10/final-indignity-for-man-stuffed-as-a-museum-exhibit-el-negro-botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian The final indignity for &#x27;El Negro&#x27;, laid to rest in wrong country</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian The final indignity for &#x27;El Negro&#x27;, laid to rest in wrong country</span></span></span>

Claims that the man has now been conclusively identified by a personal name should be treated cautiously. Proposed identities rely on attempts to connect incomplete expedition accounts, local leadership histories, physical descriptions and places whose names changed over time. None has achieved secure documentary confirmation.

What is firmly established is more sobering than any speculative identification. A man died in southern Africa; his body was removed, altered and exhibited in Europe; records preserving his own identity were either never made or did not survive; and later institutions spoke for him without being able to determine who he was. His namelessness is not an exotic puzzle. It is evidence of the way colonial collecting reduced a person to an object.

The Gaborone grave has consequently become both a memorial and an unresolved archival problem. It asks whether repatriation can fully repair harm when the destination, identity and wishes of the deceased remain unknown.

## Why does Segametsi Mogomotsi’s murder remain unresolved?

On 5 November 1994, fourteen-year-old Segametsi Mogomotsi disappeared in Mochudi, north-east of Gaborone. Her body was found the following day with severe injuries and missing tissue. The circumstances led police, journalists and members of the public to describe the case as a suspected medicine or ritual killing. No one has been convicted of her murder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.proquest.com/scholarly-journals/they-cut-segametsi-into-parts-ritual-murder-youth/docview/216475832/se-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: proquest.com">[ProQuest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">proquest.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on proquest.com.</span></span></span>

The case became a national crisis because the killing was followed by allegations that influential people were being protected. Suspects were detained and released, while rumours spread faster than verified information. Segametsi’s fellow pupils and other young people organised protests. Demonstrations escalated in Mochudi and Gaborone, bringing confrontations with police, damage to property and wider student unrest. The murder thus became inseparable from questions about power, inequality and whether the justice system treated well-connected suspects differently from ordinary citizens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259910097_The_Discourse_of_%27Ritual_Murder%27_Popular_Reaction_to_Political_Leaders_in_Botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) The Discourse of &#x27;Ritual Murder</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) The Discourse of &#x27;Ritual Murder</span></span></span>

British investigators from Scotland Yard were asked to review aspects of the case. Their involvement raised hopes that forensic or procedural problems would be corrected, but no prosecution followed. Accounts of the investigation repeatedly note that the resulting report was not made fully available to the public. That absence created an enduring vacuum in which allegations, partial recollections and conspiracy theories could thrive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259910097_The_Discourse_of_%27Ritual_Murder%27_Popular_Reaction_to_Political_Leaders_in_Botswana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) The Discourse of &#x27;Ritual Murder</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) The Discourse of &#x27;Ritual Murder</span></span></span>

Several points must be separated carefully.

**The murder and mutilation are documented facts.** Segametsi was a real child, not a figure from folklore. Her family’s loss should not be overshadowed by the mythology that later surrounded the case.

**The motive was never judicially established.** The condition of her body prompted the description “ritual murder”, and such an interpretation was widely accepted. Nevertheless, without a completed prosecution, it is not possible to state with legal certainty who killed her, who ordered the crime or what the perpetrators believed they were doing.

**Rumours about prominent beneficiaries remain allegations.** Public suspicion focused on business and political elites, but suspicion is not proof. Repeating names without firm evidence risks turning the failure of the investigation into fresh injustice.

**Some people suffered through wrongful suspicion.** At least one former suspect pursued legal action after detention, illustrating that an investigation can harm innocent people while still failing to identify the guilty. Reports years later continued to describe former suspects seeking recognition or closure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaystandard.info/men-wrongly-accused-of-segametsiocos-murder-want-to-meet-khama/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaystandard.info">[Sunday Standard]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaystandard.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">men wrongly accused of segametsiocos murder want to meet khama</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">men wrongly accused of segametsiocos murder want to meet khama</span></span></span>

The unanswered questions are therefore not confined to the identity of the killer. They include whether the original scene was handled properly, whether forensic evidence was lost or inadequately examined, why investigative findings were not made public, whether witnesses were intimidated or ignored, and whether political considerations affected decision-making. Available public sources do not settle those issues.

Segametsi’s case remains historically important because it altered public debate in Botswana. It became a reference point for distrust of authority, fear surrounding alleged ritual killings and youth demands for accountability. Memorial events and dramatic works have kept the case in public memory, but remembrance has not produced a definitive evidential breakthrough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mmegi.bw/news/segametsi-unveiling-reminder-of-uncompleted-investigations/news" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mmegi.bw">[Mmegi Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mmegi.bw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mmegi.bw.</span></span></span>

The responsible conclusion is stark: Segametsi was murdered, the investigation generated profound public controversy, and the available record does not justify accusing any particular living or deceased individual of the crime. The deepest mystery is not supernatural. It is why a case that mobilised a nation failed to deliver a transparent and convincing account of what happened.

## What these mysteries reveal about Botswana’s past

Botswana’s historical mysteries are especially revealing when their different kinds of uncertainty are kept separate.

The Lost City of the Kalahari is largely a problem of travel writing, geography and misidentified geology. Its popular form is probably myth, although Farini’s exact route and the feature he saw remain debatable. The Tsodilo python claim is an archaeological interpretation built on genuine ancient evidence but extended beyond what dating and context can presently demonstrate. The [Banyoles man]({{ 'banyoles-man/' | relative_url }}) is an archival and ethical mystery created by colonial dehumanisation: the individual is real, while the records needed to restore his identity are inadequate. Segametsi Mogomotsi’s murder is a modern criminal and institutional mystery in which missing transparency has prolonged grief and suspicion.

None is improved by exaggeration. There is no good evidence for a technologically advanced city buried beneath Botswana’s sands. Archaeology has not conclusively proved a 70,000-year-old python religion. The man buried in Gaborone has not been securely identified. The allegations surrounding Segametsi’s death have not been established in court.

Yet uncertainty does not make these stories unimportant. Each one exposes a historical process: the expectations colonial explorers carried into African landscapes; the difficulty of interpreting prehistoric symbolism; the destruction of identity through museum collecting; and the social consequences of an investigation that fails to command public trust.

The most enduring national enigmas are often not puzzles with a concealed, dramatic answer. They survive because evidence was never recorded, was removed from its context, was interpreted too confidently or was withheld when public confidence depended upon its release. Botswana’s mysteries matter because the gaps themselves have become part of the country’s history.
