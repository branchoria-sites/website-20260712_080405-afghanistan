## Introduction

On the night of 21 August 1986, Lake Nyos in north-western [Cameroon]({{ 'what-still-haunts-cameroons-historical/' | relative_url }}) released a huge cloud of carbon dioxide that flowed silently through surrounding valleys, killing about 1,700 people and thousands of livestock. The central scientific mystery today is no longer what killed the victims. Researchers overwhelmingly agree that the deaths were caused by a rare **limnic eruption**—the sudden release of carbon dioxide (CO₂) that had accumulated in the lake's deep waters over many years. What remains uncertain is **what set that release in motion**. Despite decades of study, no single triggering event has been proved. Instead, scientists have narrowed the possibilities to several competing mechanisms, each supported by some evidence but none conclusively demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1987/0097/report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubs.usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubs.usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological SurveyDEPARTMENT OF THE INTERIORApril 15, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 15, 2026</span></span></span>

## How carbon dioxide built up in the lake

The trigger debate only makes sense once the lake's unusual chemistry is understood. Lake Nyos occupies a young volcanic crater, known as a maar, along the Cameroon Volcanic Line. Deep beneath the lake, carbon dioxide from magmatic sources slowly rises through fractures in the Earth's crust and dissolves into the cold, high-pressure water at the bottom of the lake. Over many decades, this created a large reservoir of dissolved CO₂.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/origin-and-age-lake-nyos-maar-cameroon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[USGS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Origin and age of the Lake Nyos maar, Cameroon &#124; U.S. Geological Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Origin and age of the Lake Nyos maar, Cameroon &#124; U.S. Geological SurveyJanuary 1, 1989...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1989</span></span></span>

Normally, the lake remained stable because its water was strongly layered. Warm, fresher water stayed near the surface while colder, denser water rich in dissolved gas remained trapped below. The pressure at depth allowed enormous quantities of carbon dioxide to stay dissolved, much like gas inside an unopened bottle of fizzy drink. As long as the layers remained undisturbed, the lake could quietly store this gas.

Scientists examining the lake after the disaster found clear chemical evidence that carbon dioxide had accumulated long before August 1986. They also concluded that the gas originated from deep geological sources rather than from decaying vegetation or direct volcanic eruption at the surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/evolution-thermal-structure-and-water-chemistry-lake-nyos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[USGS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The evolution of thermal structure and water chemistry in Lake Nyos &#124; U.S. Geological SurveyJanuary 1, 1989...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1989</span></span></span>

## Why the gas cloud killed so quickly

Once the deep water began to rise, pressure decreased and dissolved carbon dioxide escaped as bubbles. Those bubbles made the water more buoyant, pulling even more gas-rich water upward in a self-reinforcing chain reaction. Within a short time, an enormous quantity of CO₂ erupted from the lake.

Because carbon dioxide is denser than air, it did not disperse upwards like smoke. Instead, it poured downhill through nearby valleys as an invisible cloud, displacing oxygen close to the ground. People and animals lost consciousness rapidly from oxygen deprivation and high CO₂ concentrations. The disaster was especially deadly because many victims were asleep and had little or no warning. Investigators found no convincing evidence that poisonous volcanic gases or high temperatures were responsible; carbon dioxide alone explains the pattern of deaths.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1987/0097/report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubs.usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubs.usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological SurveyDEPARTMENT OF THE INTERIORApril 15, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 15, 2026</span></span></span>

## Competing theories for the initial trigger

The unresolved question is not why carbon dioxide escaped once the process had begun, but what disturbed the stable deep water enough to start the chain reaction.

### Landslide into the lake

One of the earliest explanations proposed that part of the crater wall collapsed into the lake. A landslide could have displaced deep water, allowing it to rise until dissolved gas began escaping.

This theory gained support because investigators observed disturbed shorelines and evidence of slope failures around the lake after the disaster. However, it has never been established whether these landslides occurred **before** the gas release and caused it, or whether they happened **because** the violent eruption of water destabilised the surrounding slopes. The sequence remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1987/0097/report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubs.usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubs.usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological SurveyDEPARTMENT OF THE INTERIORApril 15, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 15, 2026</span></span></span>

### Sudden release from a deep gas-rich spring

More recent studies have suggested that an underwater spring carrying carbon dioxide-rich water may have played a more active role than previously recognised. Instead of requiring a large landslide, an increase in flow from a deep vent could have lifted gas-rich bottom water until bubbling began.

This idea has received renewed attention after detailed mapping of nearby Lake Monoun, where underwater vents appear capable of disturbing deep water directly. Although the evidence comes primarily from Lake Monoun rather than Lake Nyos itself, it has prompted scientists to reconsider whether submerged gas-rich springs alone might initiate a limnic eruption.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0012825223002921" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evidence of “Lake Nyos-type” behavior in the geological record: A review - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

### Weather and seasonal mixing

Another possibility is that unusual weather disturbed the lake's natural layering. Heavy rainfall, cooling surface temperatures or strong winds can sometimes mix lake waters.

Most researchers now consider ordinary weather an unlikely explanation by itself. Measurements made after the disaster showed that Lake Nyos possessed exceptionally strong chemical and thermal layering, making it resistant to normal seasonal overturn. In other words, routine meteorological conditions probably lacked the energy needed to mix the deep, gas-rich water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/evolution-thermal-structure-and-water-chemistry-lake-nyos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[USGS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The evolution of thermal structure and water chemistry in Lake Nyos &#124; U.S. Geological SurveyJanuary 1, 1989...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1989</span></span></span>

### A small volcanic event

Immediately after the disaster, some observers suspected that a volcanic eruption beneath the lake had suddenly injected fresh carbon dioxide or triggered the explosion.

Subsequent geological, chemical and isotopic studies have largely rejected this explanation. Carbon dioxide clearly originated from magmatic sources deep underground, but investigators found no convincing evidence of a contemporaneous volcanic eruption, lava, volcanic ash or other signs of fresh volcanic activity on 21 August 1986. The consensus today is that the gas had accumulated gradually over many years rather than being released directly by an erupting volcano.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1987/0097/report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubs.usgs.gov">[usgs.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubs.usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological SurveyDEPARTMENT OF THE INTERIORApril 15, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 15, 2026</span></span></span>

### Could it have happened spontaneously?

Some researchers argue that the lake may eventually have reached a critical point where no external trigger was required. If carbon dioxide concentrations became high enough, a relatively small disturbance—or even internal instability within the gas-rich layer—might have initiated runaway degassing.

Support for this idea comes partly from studies of gas accumulation rates and from comparisons with Lake Monoun, where calculations suggest deep waters can approach saturation. Under this model, the distinction between "trigger" and "inevitable release" becomes blurred: almost any minor disturbance could have been sufficient because the lake was already close to failure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/evolution-co2-lakes-monoun-and-nyos-cameroon-and-during-controlled-degassing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[USGS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evolution of CO2 in Lakes Monoun and Nyos, Cameroon, before and during controlled degassing &#124; U.S. Geological Survey...</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery remains unresolved

Unlike earthquakes or volcanic eruptions that leave clear geological records, the exact sequence of events at Lake Nyos lasted only minutes and occurred at night without direct scientific observation. By the time researchers reached the site, the gas had dispersed and the lake had already settled into a new state.

Many of the clues that might have identified the first disturbance—wave patterns, slope movements or underwater currents—had already been altered by the eruption itself. Computer models can reproduce several plausible scenarios, but multiple trigger mechanisms can produce similar final results, making it difficult to distinguish among them decades later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1987/0097/report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubs.usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubs.usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological SurveyDEPARTMENT OF THE INTERIORApril 15, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 15, 2026</span></span></span>

## What scientists agree on today

Although debate continues over the precise trigger, several conclusions are now widely accepted:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Carbon dioxide accumulated gradually in the lake's deep waters over many years.
* The gas came from deep magmatic sources beneath the volcanic region.
* A limnic eruption released that stored gas in a rapid chain reaction.
* The victims died because dense carbon dioxide displaced breathable oxygen.
* No convincing evidence supports a major volcanic eruption occurring at the time of the disaster.

## Why the trigger still matters

Understanding the initial trigger is more than an academic puzzle. It determines how scientists assess the risk posed by other gas-rich volcanic lakes.

If landslides are the main danger, monitoring unstable crater walls becomes a priority. If underwater gas-rich springs can trigger eruptions, continuous observation of deep-water circulation is more important. If lakes can erupt spontaneously once carbon dioxide reaches a critical concentration, preventing gas build-up through controlled degassing becomes essential regardless of external conditions.

These questions shaped the engineering response at Lake Nyos, where degassing pipes were installed to release carbon dioxide gradually rather than allowing pressure to accumulate again. The disaster transformed scientific understanding of limnic eruptions and remains one of the clearest examples of how a largely solved natural hazard can still contain one crucial unresolved mechanism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/evolution-co2-lakes-monoun-and-nyos-cameroon-and-during-controlled-degassing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[usgs.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evolution of CO2 in Lakes Monoun and Nyos, Cameroon, before and during controlled degassing &#124; U.S. Geological Survey...</span></span></span>
