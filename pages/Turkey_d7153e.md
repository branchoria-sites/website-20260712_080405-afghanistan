## Introduction

Some cases remain genuinely unresolved because the decisive evidence has disappeared. Others are mysteries only in a narrower sense: scholars understand the broad explanation, but gaps in the record still invite argument. A third group survives largely because modern mythmaking has proved more attractive than the evidence. The [Piri Reis map]({{ 'piri-reis-map/' | relative_url }}) does not reveal an ice-free Antarctica, and the boat-shaped Durupınar formation is not accepted by geologists as Noah’s Ark. Yet both remain important examples of how Turkish sites and artefacts acquire new meanings long after their creation.

Together, these cases show why Turkey’s past is unusually fertile ground for historical investigation. Anatolia has been a crossroads of prehistoric communities, empires, religions, armies and collectors, leaving an archaeological and archival record that is exceptionally rich but often fragmented.

## What was Göbekli Tepe built for?

[Göbekli Tepe]({{ 'göbekli-tepe/' | relative_url }}), in south-eastern Anatolia, contains monumental stone structures erected between roughly 9600 and 8200 BC. Its large T-shaped pillars, some carved with wild animals and human features, were raised by communities living before fully developed farming societies appeared in the region. That description is cautious because archaeologists can date the buildings, study their construction and identify the animals eaten there, but cannot recover the beliefs of people who left no writing. The pillars may represent stylised human figures. Images of snakes, boars, vultures and predators may refer to myths, social identities, dangers or relationships with the dead. None of these interpretations can yet be proved.

Early accounts portrayed Göbekli Tepe as an isolated sanctuary built by roaming hunter-gatherers who periodically assembled for ceremonies and feasts. Large quantities of animal bone supported the idea that groups gathered there for communal consumption. The apparent absence of ordinary homes also encouraged the dramatic claim that organised religion had preceded settled village life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2011/12/19/the-sanctuary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker The Sanctuary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Believed to be the birthplace of the prophet Abraham, Urfa attracts many pilgrims. The writer explores Göbekli Tepe, a site with megalith...</span></span></span>

Later excavation has complicated that picture. Evidence of domestic activity and more ordinary buildings suggests that people may have lived at or near the site for longer periods. Göbekli Tepe is therefore increasingly understood not as a temple standing alone in an empty landscape, but as part of a wider network of early settlements with both everyday and special-purpose architecture. Even specialists who defend a primarily ceremonial interpretation acknowledge that distinctions between “house”, “shrine” and “public building” may not have existed in modern form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dainst.blog/the-tepe-telegrams/2017/01/24/a-sanctuary-or-so-fair-a-house/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dainst.blog">[DAI Newsblog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dainst.blog</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAI Newsblog A Sanctuary … or so fair a House?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tepe Telegrams24 Jan 2017 — I agree that the best interpretation of PPNA Tepe Gobekli is that it did not serve a domestic function, and...</span></span></span>

Another puzzle is why several monumental structures were filled in. This was once described as deliberate burial, as though the community had carefully entombed obsolete sacred buildings. Some filling was certainly caused by human action, but erosion, rebuilding, collapse and later disturbance may also have contributed. Research into earthquake damage has further shown that the buildings experienced seismic stress and repairs, making a single ceremonial “burial event” less certain than popular accounts imply.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352226724000618" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shaking up the Neolithic - Tracing seismic impact at...by M Kinzel · 2024 · Cited by 3 — The Neolithic architecture at Göbe...</span></span></span>

The best-supported conclusion is modest. Göbekli Tepe was a socially important place where early communities invested enormous labour in monumental buildings, imagery, gatherings and probably ritual. Whether it was mainly a settlement, sanctuary, meeting centre, mortuary site or combination of these remains open. Claims that its carvings record a prehistoric comet strike, an advanced zodiac or knowledge inherited from a vanished civilisation are speculative interpretations, not archaeological consensus.

## Does the Piri Reis map show forbidden ancient knowledge?

The surviving fragment of the world map drawn by the Ottoman admiral Piri Reis in 1513 is genuinely remarkable. It combines information from earlier European, Islamic and Portuguese charts at a moment when knowledge of the Atlantic and the Americas was changing rapidly. Its own inscriptions state that it was compiled from numerous earlier sources, including a chart associated with Christopher Columbus.

The true archival mystery concerns those lost source maps. It is possible that Piri Reis preserved geographical information from charts that no longer survive, perhaps including an early Columbian representation of the Caribbean. Scholars continue to debate precisely how he combined his materials and how particular islands and coastlines should be identified. Research also shows that the map belongs within both Mediterranean navigational cartography and Islamic visual traditions rather than standing outside the knowledge of its period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cupola.gettysburg.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1010&amp;context=histfac" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cupola.gettysburg.edu">[Cupola]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cupola.gettysburg.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Searchin&#x27; His Eyes, Lookin&#x27; for Traces: Piri Reis&#x27; World Map...by KC Pinto · 2012 · Cited by 14 — This paper presents hitherto unn...</span></span></span>

Its fame outside cartographic history rests mainly on a different claim: that the southern portion depicts Antarctica without its ice sheet, proving that an unknown ancient civilisation had mapped the continent thousands of years ago.

That interpretation is not supported by the map’s geography or historical setting. The supposed Antarctic coastline is more plausibly a distorted continuation of South America or an imagined southern continent of the sort commonly included in early modern maps. Cartographers inherited the classical idea that a great landmass must exist in the south to “balance” the known northern continents. Coastlines were also stretched, rotated or combined when information from incompatible charts was copied into one image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piri_Reis_map" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Piri Reis map</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Piri Reis map</span></span></span>

The Antarctic theory became widely known only in the twentieth century. Its advocates repositioned parts of the map, rejected labels that contradicted their reading and compared selected curves with modern coastlines beneath the Antarctic ice. Such resemblance is subjective: irregular lines on old maps can be matched to many real coastlines if they are rotated, resized and interpreted selectively.

The map remains mysterious, but not because it proves impossible knowledge. Its real importance lies in the vanished documents behind it. It offers a rare glimpse of how Ottoman navigators assembled intelligence from rival seafaring powers during the first decades of sustained European contact with the Americas.

## Was Sultan Abdülaziz murdered?

Sultan Abdülaziz was removed from the Ottoman throne on 30 May 1876 during a political and financial crisis. On 4 June he was found dead at Feriye Palace in Istanbul with wounds to his arms. The immediate official finding was suicide. Within days, however, rumours of murder were spreading through the court, diplomatic community and press.

The physical circumstances made both interpretations possible. Abdülaziz had been deposed, confined and separated from much of his household. His state of mind was described differently by witnesses with political interests of their own. Reports about the wounds, the position of the body, bloodstained objects and access to his rooms were inconsistent. The original examination also fell far short of a modern independent forensic investigation.

The case became more confused after Sultan Abdülhamid II came to power. In 1881, a special court tried former senior officials, including the reforming statesman Midhat Pasha, for involvement in the alleged killing. The court treated Abdülaziz’s death as murder, but historians have long questioned whether the proceedings were designed partly to remove political enemies. A major modern study of the trial describes it as both a legal proceeding and a political drama whose verdict did not settle whether the former sultan had been killed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cmes.fas.harvard.edu/publications/ottoman-rule-law-and-modern-political-trial-yildiz-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cmes.fas.harvard.edu">[cmes.fas.harvard.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cmes.fas.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

Supporters of the murder theory point to the wounds on both arms, the political convenience of the death and later testimony alleging that men entered the palace to kill him. They also argue that evidence was controlled by figures involved in the coup.

The suicide interpretation has its own strengths. Contemporary doctors initially accepted it, Abdülaziz had suffered a catastrophic loss of power, and later murder testimony emerged in a deeply politicised setting. Cutting both arms is difficult but not medically impossible, and arguments based solely on that detail cannot establish homicide.

The central problem is that nearly every important source was produced by someone with a stake in the outcome: the coup leaders, palace factions, the new regime, foreign diplomats or later dynastic writers. Even recent historical literature continues to frame the death as disputed rather than demonstrably solved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/4144604" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dergipark.org.tr">[DergiPark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dergipark.org.tr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dergipark.org.tr.</span></span></span>

A firm verdict is therefore unsafe. Murder was possible, and the rapid political changes surrounding the death justify suspicion. Suicide was also plausible, and the later trial cannot be treated as neutral proof. The enduring mystery matters because Abdülaziz’s death became part of a larger struggle over legitimacy, constitutional government and palace power during the Ottoman Empire’s turbulent final decades.

## Where is the skull of Mimar Sinan?

Mimar Sinan, the sixteenth-century chief imperial architect associated with monuments including the Süleymaniye Mosque in Istanbul, was buried in a tomb of his own design. In 1935, his grave was opened by researchers connected with the Turkish Historical Society. His skull was removed for anthropological study during an era when racial classification was treated by many governments and scholars as legitimate science.

Contemporary reports claimed that examination of the skull would help establish Sinan’s supposed racial identity. The political purpose was clear: the celebrated Ottoman architect was being incorporated into a modern nationalist account of Turkish history.

The planned anthropological museum was not created, and Sinan’s skull subsequently disappeared. A study published by the Turkish Historical Society’s journal *Belleten*, drawing on period newspapers and articles, confirms both the excavation and the later loss of the skull.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://belleten.gov.tr/eng/abstarct/333/eng" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belleten.gov.tr">[Belleten]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belleten.gov.tr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on belleten.gov.tr.</span></span></span>

The basic facts are therefore documented:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Sinan’s tomb was opened under official scholarly authority.
* A skull believed to be his was removed.
* Researchers announced that it would be studied and eventually displayed.
* Its present location is unknown.

</div>

What cannot be established is where it went next. It may have entered an uncatalogued anthropological collection, been transferred between institutions, become separated from its identifying records or been quietly reburied. It is also possible that the remains removed in 1935 were never securely identified as Sinan’s in the first place.

The disappearance is not merely an odd museum story. It reflects a period when human remains were collected to serve racial theories that are now scientifically discredited. The mystery survives because the institutional chain of custody appears to have broken down and because no publicly verified rediscovery has settled the matter.

## Who owns the lost treasure of Troy?

In 1873, Heinrich Schliemann announced the discovery of a spectacular group of gold, silver and bronze objects at Hisarlık, the site identified with ancient Troy. He called the collection “Priam’s Treasure”, linking it to the king of Homer’s *Iliad*. The name was effective publicity but poor archaeology: the objects came from a level of Troy roughly a thousand years older than the conventional period associated with the Trojan War.

Schliemann’s own account of the discovery is unreliable in several respects. He dramatised the moment, credited his wife with helping remove the objects even though evidence suggests she was not present, and may have combined finds from more than one context. Because his excavation methods destroyed parts of the site and his documentation was inadequate, scholars cannot reconstruct the hoard’s exact archaeological setting with complete confidence.

He also removed the collection from Ottoman territory without lawful authorisation. Ottoman officials pursued legal action, and Schliemann eventually paid compensation and surrendered some objects while retaining most of the famous treasure. It later entered the Berlin museum collections. The Berlin State Museums still describe the discovery as the event that made him famous, while recognising the group by the conventional rather than historically accurate name “Treasure of Priam”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smb.museum/en/whats-new/detail/150-years-since-heinrich-schliemann-uncovered-the-treasure-of-priamos-in-troy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smb.museum">[Staatliche Museen zu Berlin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smb.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-title">150 years since heinrich schliemann uncovered the treasure of priamos in troy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">150 years since heinrich schliemann uncovered the treasure of priamos in troy</span></span></span>

The treasure then acquired a second missing chapter. During the Second World War, it was placed in protective storage in Berlin. In 1945 Soviet forces removed it to the Soviet Union, where its location was officially concealed for decades. Russia acknowledged possession in the 1990s. The Pushkin State Museum in Moscow now states that it holds 259 objects from the Trojan collections transferred from Berlin after the war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pushkinmuseum.art/museum/buildings/main/floor1/3_troy/index.php?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pushkinmuseum.art">[Pushkin Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pushkinmuseum.art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pushkinmuseum.art.</span></span></span>

This creates overlapping claims:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Turkey** can argue that the objects were unlawfully exported from Ottoman territory and form part of the archaeological heritage of Troy.
* **Germany** can argue that its museums legally acquired the collection from Schliemann and that the Soviet removal was wartime seizure.
* **Russia** has treated the objects as compensation for cultural destruction inflicted by Nazi Germany, although Germany regards them as displaced museum property.

</div>

The objects are no longer physically lost, but their lawful and moral ownership remains unresolved. The case links nineteenth-century excavation practices, Ottoman heritage law, wartime looting and modern restitution disputes. It also demonstrates how the romantic language of “discovery” can hide the removal of heritage from the country in which it was found.

## Why were Cappadocia’s underground cities created?

The underground complexes of Cappadocia include chambers, corridors, ventilation shafts, wells, storage rooms and heavy circular doors cut into soft volcanic rock. Derinkuyu is the most famous, but it belongs to a much larger landscape of subterranean architecture rather than being a unique hidden metropolis.

Popular accounts sometimes attribute these complexes to a lost civilisation or describe them as self-contained cities occupied continuously by tens of thousands of people. The archaeological evidence points instead to gradual construction and repeated reuse. Different sections may have been enlarged during different periods, making it difficult to assign a single foundation date.

The most convincing interpretation is that many complexes functioned as refuges. Cappadocia’s vulnerable communities faced recurring warfare, raids and political instability. Underground spaces offered concealment, defensible passages, storage and temporary shelter. Narrow corridors and stone doors could slow attackers, while ventilation systems allowed groups to remain below ground for extended periods. Research on Cappadocian underground structures accordingly classifies many of them as shelters shaped by the region’s favourable geology and long history of conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374438569_UNDERGROUND_SHELTERS_IN_CAPPADOCIA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">374438569 UNDERGROUND SHELTERS IN CAPPADOCIA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">374438569 UNDERGROUND SHELTERS IN CAPPADOCIA</span></span></span>

This does not mean every chamber had the same purpose. Some underground rooms were probably used routinely for storage, food processing, animals or religious worship. Existing caves could be expanded during emergencies, while older sections could be incorporated into larger defensive systems. A study of Derinkuyu emphasises its unusual architectural and urban complexity, but scale alone does not establish that its entire excavated area functioned as one permanently inhabited city.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877705816330594" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

The unresolved questions are mainly chronological. Who cut the earliest rooms? How much of the visible system belongs to late antiquity or the Byzantine period? Were some sections created much earlier and merely adapted by later Christian communities? Limited inscriptions, disturbed deposits and centuries of reuse make exact answers difficult.

The underground cities are therefore genuine archaeological puzzles, but not evidence of an unknown advanced culture. Their fascination lies in the long accumulation of human responses to danger, climate, storage needs and the unusual possibilities offered by Cappadocia’s rock.

## Is the Durupınar formation Noah’s Ark?

Near Doğubayazıt in eastern Turkey lies a long, boat-shaped formation known as the Durupınar site. Its outline and proximity to Mount Ararat have encouraged repeated claims that it is the fossilised remains of Noah’s Ark.

The shape is striking, but shape alone is weak evidence. Geological investigation has identified the formation as a natural structure produced by folded sedimentary layers, erosion and concentrations of iron-bearing minerals. Material promoted as metal fittings was found to be weathered volcanic or mineral matter, while alleged timber was naturally altered rock. Supposed anchor stones in the surrounding area are made from local stone rather than material transported by an ancient vessel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csun.edu/~vcgeo005/bogus.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csun.edu">[California State University, Northridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csun.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on csun.edu.</span></span></span>

The site has periodically returned to the news because private research groups report radar anomalies, unusual soil chemistry or lines that resemble decks and corridors. Ground-penetrating radar can detect changes beneath the surface, but it does not by itself identify wood, walls or a ship. Natural layers, fractures, moisture differences and mineral deposits can all produce geometric-looking signals.

A useful detail is that David Fasold, once one of the site’s best-known advocates, eventually joined geologist Lorence Collins in concluding that the feature was a common natural formation. Their work argued that the evidence promoted as a boat’s structure had geological explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csun.edu/~vcgeo005/bogus.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csun.edu">[California State University, Northridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csun.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on csun.edu.</span></span></span>

The wider flood tradition remains historically significant. Stories of overwhelming floods appear in ancient Mesopotamian literature as well as later Jewish, Christian and Islamic traditions, and communities around eastern Anatolia have long associated mountains with the Ark. But a meaningful religious or cultural landscape is not the same thing as an excavated vessel.

Durupınar is best classified as an explained geological formation surrounded by an unresolved cultural phenomenon: why each generation reinterprets natural features as physical confirmation of sacred history. Its persistence shows how easily an arresting landscape, ancient tradition and modern technology can combine to produce a mystery even when the central scientific question is largely settled.

## What these mysteries reveal about Turkey’s past

Turkey’s historical enigmas fall into three broad types.

The first consists of **real evidence gaps**. No inscription explains Göbekli Tepe’s imagery. The missing records and human remains in the Sinan case prevent a complete chain of custody. The circumstances of Abdülaziz’s death were compromised by political interference almost from the beginning.

The second consists of **contested ownership and interpretation**. The Trojan treasure is known and displayed, yet its excavation context, wartime journey and rightful home remain disputed. Cappadocia’s underground spaces are visible and measurable, but their long phases of construction resist a single origin story.

The third consists of **modern mysteries created by selective reading**. The Piri Reis map becomes inexplicable only when it is removed from the history of sixteenth-century cartography. Durupınar becomes an ancient ship only when geological evidence is treated as less persuasive than visual resemblance.

The strongest approach is therefore neither automatic scepticism nor romantic belief. It is to ask what was recorded at the time, whether the physical evidence has a secure context, how later politics shaped the surviving story, and which claims require assumptions beyond the available facts. Turkey’s unresolved past is most interesting not where evidence disappears beneath fantasy, but where archaeology, archives and public memory point towards different versions of what happened.
