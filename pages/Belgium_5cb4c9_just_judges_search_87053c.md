## Introduction

The missing *Just Judges* panel has not been authenticated since it was stolen from St Bavo’s Cathedral in Ghent during the night of 10–11 April 1934. The strongest evidence does not point to a random art thief selling a masterpiece abroad. It points instead to an organised ransom attempt, almost certainly connected to stockbroker Arsène Goedertier, who died before revealing the hiding place. Yet even that conclusion leaves the central questions unanswered: did Goedertier steal the panel himself, did he have an accomplice, and was the painting hidden, transferred or destroyed?

The mystery survives because several unusually strong clues exist without producing a recovery. There are ransom letters, the successful return of the panel’s painted reverse, papers found in Goedertier’s office and a reported deathbed statement. Decades of searches have nevertheless failed, and the East Flanders public prosecutor confirmed in 2024 that investigators still had no idea where the panel was.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.om-mp.be/nl/artikel/persbericht-gent-90-jaar-rechtvaardige-rechters-elke-tipgever-wordt-gehoord" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: om-mp.be">[om-mp.be]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">om-mp.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Persbericht - Gent - 90 jaar Rechtvaardige Rechters – “Elke tipgever wordt gehoord” &#124; Openbaar Ministerie...</span></span></span>

## How the 1934 theft was carried out

The theft was discovered on the morning of 11 April, when cathedral sexton Désiré Van Volsem found that the door to the chapel housing the Ghent Altarpiece had been forced. Two painted surfaces were missing: *The Just Judges* on the front and a grisaille, or monochrome painted figure, of John the Baptist on the reverse. They had once formed opposite sides of the same wing, but the wooden panel had been sawn apart in the nineteenth century so that both images could be displayed simultaneously. That earlier alteration made the two sections easier to remove separately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visit.gent.be/en/disappearance-just-judges-panel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visit.gent.be">[Visit Gent]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visit.gent.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Gent The disappearance of the Just Judges panel &#124; Visit Gent</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Gent The disappearance of the Just Judges panel &#124; Visit Gent</span></span></span>

The thief had removed the iron fittings holding the wing in place and taken the panels from their frame without seriously damaging the surrounding work. A French-language note left behind referred to Germany and the Treaty of Versailles, under which parts of the altarpiece formerly held in Berlin had been returned to [Belgium]({{ 'belgium/' | relative_url }}) after the First World War. The message may have been intended to give the theft a political appearance, but the later correspondence showed that the practical motive was ransom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visit.gent.be/en/disappearance-just-judges-panel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visit.gent.be">[Visit Gent]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visit.gent.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Gent The disappearance of the Just Judges panel &#124; Visit Gent</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Gent The disappearance of the Just Judges panel &#124; Visit Gent</span></span></span>

The initial police response produced little usable forensic evidence. No fingerprints or decisive physical traces were recovered, and the techniques now associated with major art-crime investigations were either unavailable or far less developed. The East Flanders prosecutor has since acknowledged that opportunities were missed in 1934, while also noting the limitations under which the original investigators worked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.om-mp.be/nl/artikel/persbericht-gent-90-jaar-rechtvaardige-rechters-elke-tipgever-wordt-gehoord" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: om-mp.be">[Om-mp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">om-mp.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Persbericht - Gent - 90 jaar Rechtvaardige Rechters – “Elke tipgever wordt gehoord” &#124; Openbaar Ministerie...</span></span></span>

That weak beginning mattered. The scene could not later be reconstructed with certainty, witnesses’ memories faded, and questions about access to the cathedral were never conclusively settled. The crime therefore became easier to theorise about than to prove.

## What the ransom letters reveal

On 30 April 1934, nineteen days after the theft, the Bishop of Ghent received a letter demanding one million Belgian francs for the panels’ return. The writer signed the correspondence with the initials “D.U.A.” and warned that the works might be destroyed if payment was refused. Twelve more letters reportedly followed, the last dated 1 October.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visit.gent.be/en/disappearance-just-judges-panel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visit.gent.be">[Visit Gent]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visit.gent.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Gent The disappearance of the Just Judges panel &#124; Visit Gent</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Gent The disappearance of the Just Judges panel &#124; Visit Gent</span></span></span>

The most important development came with the third letter. It contained a left-luggage receipt for Brussels North station. Investigators used it to recover the John the Baptist panel on 29 May. Returning that section served several purposes: it proved that the correspondent had access to the stolen work, increased the credibility of the threat and showed that the two painted surfaces had been handled separately after the theft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kikirpa.be/files/Pers/Persberichten-ouder-NL/2010-06-04-Persdossier-RechtvaardigeRechters-NL.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kikirpa.be">[KIK-IRPA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kikirpa.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">KIK-IRPAPersdossier-Rechtvaardige Rechters-NL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KIK-IRPAPersdossier-Rechtvaardige Rechters-NL</span></span></span>

The exchange then became an extended and unsuccessful negotiation. The authorities were unwilling to pay the full demand, while the writer continued to insist on substantial payment for *The Just Judges*. The return of John the Baptist is sometimes described as a gesture of goodwill, but it is better understood as proof of possession within a controlled extortion scheme. The ransomer surrendered the less valuable section while retaining the object that gave him bargaining power.

The letters also suggest planning rather than panic. The writer used indirect delivery methods, maintained contact for months and apparently understood how to avoid an immediate arrest. Yet the scheme never reached a successful exchange. That failure created the mystery as it exists today: a panel too famous to sell openly remained in the hands of someone whose intended route to profit had collapsed.

## Why Arsène Goedertier remains the central suspect

Arsène Goedertier was a stockbroker and businessman from Wetteren with respectable social and church connections. His name entered the case only after he collapsed at a political meeting in Dendermonde on 25 November 1934. According to the account given by his lawyer, Georges De Vos, Goedertier said before dying that he alone knew where the missing part of the Ghent Altarpiece was hidden and directed De Vos to papers in his office.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.om-mp.be/nl/artikel/persbericht-gent-90-jaar-rechtvaardige-rechters-elke-tipgever-wordt-gehoord" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: om-mp.be">[Om-mp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">om-mp.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Persbericht - Gent - 90 jaar Rechtvaardige Rechters – “Elke tipgever wordt gehoord” &#124; Openbaar Ministerie...</span></span></span>

The subsequent discovery was more incriminating than the deathbed remark by itself. In Goedertier’s desk were copies connected to the ransom correspondence and material indicating detailed knowledge of the negotiations. One unsent message is widely reported to have said that the panel rested in a place from which neither Goedertier nor anyone else could remove it without attracting public attention. That wording has driven much of the later search, especially the theory that the work was hidden in a conspicuous or regularly visited building.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Just_Judges" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Just Judges</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Just Judges</span></span></span>

This evidence makes Goedertier the strongest known link to the extortion attempt. It does not, however, prove every part of the crime. The Royal Institute for Cultural Heritage notes that he came to be regarded as the culprit without conclusive proof and that a reconstruction indicated the thief could not have acted alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kikirpa.be/files/Pers/Persberichten-ouder-NL/2010-06-04-Persdossier-RechtvaardigeRechters-NL.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kikirpa.be">[KIK-IRPA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kikirpa.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">KIK-IRPAPersdossier-Rechtvaardige Rechters-NL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KIK-IRPAPersdossier-Rechtvaardige Rechters-NL</span></span></span>

Three possibilities therefore remain open:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Goedertier organised and participated in the theft.** This is the simplest reading of the papers, the ransom campaign and the reported confession.
* **He handled the negotiation for others.** His possession of the correspondence might show that he was the intermediary or planner rather than the person who entered the cathedral.
* **He knew the hiding place but not the full operation.** His final words, as reported, concerned knowledge of the location more clearly than physical responsibility for removing the panel.

</div>

The distinction matters because an accomplice could have moved the painting after Goedertier’s death. It also affects how literally investigators should interpret the phrase about a publicly visible hiding place. A statement accurate in November 1934 might have ceased to be accurate days, years or decades later.

## Could the panel still be inside St Bavo’s Cathedral?

The cathedral theory has remained attractive because it fits several features of the case. The panel was large enough to be recognisable but slim enough to conceal within architectural spaces, furniture or service areas. The prosecutor’s office gives its dimensions as roughly 149 centimetres high by 55 centimetres wide and considers continued concealment at an original or later hiding place more plausible than some rival scenarios.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.om-mp.be/nl/artikel/persbericht-gent-90-jaar-rechtvaardige-rechters-elke-tipgever-wordt-gehoord" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: om-mp.be">[Om-mp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">om-mp.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Persbericht - Gent - 90 jaar Rechtvaardige Rechters – “Elke tipgever wordt gehoord” &#124; Openbaar Ministerie...</span></span></span>

A cathedral hiding place could also explain the line about removal attracting public attention. A wall, crypt, monument, stairway or frequently used room might have been accessible for concealment but risky to reopen. Someone familiar with St Bavo’s routines could perhaps have placed the panel where intrusive retrieval would be noticed.

Former Ghent police chief Karel Mortier became one of the best-known supporters of this interpretation. In the 1990s, he argued that the evidence pointed towards concealment inside the cathedral and advocated a systematic search using modern detection equipment. His confidence was striking, but the searches did not produce the missing panel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theartnewspaper.com/1995/09/01/seeking-out-van-eycks-the-just-judges-altarpiece" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theartnewspaper.com">[Art Newspaper]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theartnewspaper.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Art Newspaper Seeking out Van Eyck&#x27;s &quot;The Just Judges&quot; altarpiece</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Art NewspaperSeeking out Van Eyck&#x27;s &quot;The Just Judges&quot; altarpiece - The Art Newspaper - International art news and events...</span></span></span>

The theory also faces practical difficulties. St Bavo’s has been repaired, altered, surveyed and visited by generations of clergy, conservators, builders and investigators. Concealment in an obvious structural void becomes less likely with every major intervention, although absence from a search does not prove that every cavity was examined effectively. A wooden panel hidden in damp stonework could also have deteriorated beyond recognition.

The most responsible conclusion is that the cathedral remains a plausible location, not a solved cipher. Repeated searches have weakened specific hiding-place claims, but they have not eliminated every possible space within or near the complex.

## What later searches have actually found

The search has extended far beyond St Bavo’s. According to the East Flanders public prosecutor, investigative leads have taken authorities to sites including Ghent’s Kalandeberg and Sint-Jansvest, St Lawrence’s Church in Antwerp and even the Louvre in Paris. None produced an authenticated panel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.om-mp.be/nl/artikel/persbericht-gent-90-jaar-rechtvaardige-rechters-elke-tipgever-wordt-gehoord" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: om-mp.be">[Om-mp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">om-mp.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Persbericht - Gent - 90 jaar Rechtvaardige Rechters – “Elke tipgever wordt gehoord” &#124; Openbaar Ministerie...</span></span></span>

Some searches began with attempts to decode the ransom letters or Goedertier’s surviving notes. In 2008, a tip led investigators to excavate beneath a property in Ghent after a claim that the work had been placed in a zinc container in or near an old well. No panel was recovered. Another widely reported tip alleged that it was buried beneath floorboards beside human remains; that search also found nothing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_rechtvaardige_rechters_%28paneel%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">De rechtvaardige rechters (paneel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 11, 2004 — Op zaterdag 28 juni 2008 kreeg de politie van Gent een tip binnen van Franky De Smet die zich in de brieven van Goederti...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2004</span></span></span>

The investigation has nevertheless remained active in a limited sense. In April 2024, the prosecutor’s office stated that about thirty tips had arrived over the previous decade, averaging several each year, and that each informant was heard. The same statement made clear that none had established the painting’s whereabouts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.om-mp.be/nl/artikel/persbericht-gent-90-jaar-rechtvaardige-rechters-elke-tipgever-wordt-gehoord" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: om-mp.be">[Om-mp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">om-mp.be</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Persbericht - Gent - 90 jaar Rechtvaardige Rechters – “Elke tipgever wordt gehoord” &#124; Openbaar Ministerie...</span></span></span>

This pattern explains why the case generates frequent headlines without meaningful progress. A new theory may identify a crypt, house, grave, church or family archive, but a location hypothesis is only the start. To become evidence, it must connect credibly to Goedertier or the ransom operation, survive documentary checking and produce physical material consistent with a fifteenth-century painted oak panel.

## Private collection or secret family possession

The idea that *The Just Judges* entered a private collection is easy to imagine because stolen masterpieces have sometimes been retained by wealthy owners who cannot display them publicly. In this case, however, no verified photograph, conservation record, transport document or chain of ownership has emerged.

A secret owner would face serious obstacles. The panel is one of the most recognisable missing works in Europe. Selling it through a reputable dealer or auction house would invite immediate scrutiny. Conserving it would also be difficult: specialists capable of treating early Netherlandish panel painting might recognise the image, the oak support or the technique.

A family-possession theory can be adjusted to answer those objections. The work might have been inherited quietly, with later generations unsure how to return it without legal or reputational consequences. That possibility cannot be disproved, but it remains unsupported by direct evidence. Claims involving unnamed prominent families are especially difficult to test and can turn an evidential gap into a self-protecting story: the absence of proof is explained as the result of silence.

The return of John the Baptist also weighs against a conventional collector’s theft. The crime appears to have been designed around ransom, not acquisition. A later transfer to a private owner is possible, particularly after Goedertier’s death, but it would represent a second phase of the panel’s history rather than the clearest original motive.

## Was the panel destroyed?

Destruction is the least satisfying explanation, but it may be the most practical. Once the ransom effort failed, the panel became both useless as a saleable object and dangerous as evidence. A thief might have burned it, broken it up or abandoned it somewhere vulnerable to damp, fire or building work.

Accidental destruction is equally possible. A panel hidden in a roof, cellar, wall or container could have decayed, been discarded during renovation or been destroyed during the Second World War without being identified. The altarpiece’s wider history of wartime displacement encourages dramatic theories, but no reliable evidence shows that the missing panel travelled with the rest of the work or was seized by German forces.

There is also no known destruction record. No fragments have been authenticated, no credible witness described its disposal, and the ransom correspondence does not confirm that threats to damage it were carried out. Destruction therefore remains a plausible outcome inferred from the passage of time, not an established fact.

## Why the replacement is not the lost original

The visible *Just Judges* today is a reconstruction by Belgian restorer Jef Van der Veken. He proposed making the replacement in the 1930s and based it partly on the sixteenth-century copy of the Ghent Altarpiece painted by Michiel Coxcie. Work continued around the Second World War, and Van der Veken adjusted the panel to harmonise with the surviving original sections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kikirpa.be/files/Pers/Persberichten-ouder-NL/2010-06-04-Persdossier-RechtvaardigeRechters-NL.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kikirpa.be">[KIK-IRPA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kikirpa.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">KIK-IRPAPersdossier-Rechtvaardige Rechters-NL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KIK-IRPAPersdossier-Rechtvaardige Rechters-NL</span></span></span>

Its quality inspired a durable rumour that Van der Veken had secretly obtained the stolen original and painted over it. Scientific examination has rejected that idea. The Royal Institute for Cultural Heritage studied the reconstruction using X-radiography, infrared reflectography and material analysis. Differences in support, composition and paint structure rule out the original Van Eyck panel lying beneath Van der Veken’s surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kikirpa.be/files/Pers/Persberichten-ouder-NL/2010-06-04-Persdossier-RechtvaardigeRechters-NL.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kikirpa.be">[KIK-IRPA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kikirpa.be</span><span class="citation-popover-title">KIK-IRPAPersdossier-Rechtvaardige Rechters-NL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KIK-IRPAPersdossier-Rechtvaardige Rechters-NL</span></span></span>

This is an important dividing line between mystery and myth. The original panel’s fate remains unknown, but the object displayed as its replacement is not a hidden solution waiting to be cleaned.

## Which explanation best fits the surviving evidence?

The most evidence-based reconstruction is that Goedertier was closely involved in the ransom operation and knew the panel’s location in late 1934. He may have been the organiser rather than the sole physical thief, and the successful return of John the Baptist shows that the group controlling the correspondence could retrieve at least one part of the stolen wing.

After Goedertier’s death, the trail divides. The panel may have remained in its original hiding place; an accomplice may have moved it; a later possessor may have concealed it privately; or it may have been destroyed through intent, neglect or accident. None of these outcomes is proved.

The cathedral theory fits the cryptic surviving statement and the practical problem of moving such a famous object, but decades of unsuccessful searches count against confident claims that investigators merely need to inspect one more obvious cavity. The private-collection theory explains survival but lacks a traceable owner. Destruction explains the complete absence of sightings but lacks direct evidence.

That balance is why the case still matters in Belgium. It is not a mystery sustained only by folklore. The theft, correspondence, returned panel and Goedertier papers are documented. What is missing is the final link between those records and the physical fate of the painting. Until an oak panel is recovered and authenticated through its dimensions, wood, construction, paint layers and relationship to the Ghent Altarpiece, every proposed solution remains a theory rather than a recovery.
