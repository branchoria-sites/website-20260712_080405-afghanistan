## How many people died at Pidjiguiti?

On 3 August 1959, dock workers at the port of Bissau stopped work during a dispute over pay and conditions. Portuguese colonial security forces opened fire after the confrontation escalated. Workers were killed on the quay and, according to several accounts, in the water as they attempted to escape. The massacre became a turning point in the independence struggle: the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde, usually known as the PAIGC, concluded that peaceful urban protest was unlikely to defeat Portuguese colonial rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africasacountry.com/2023/12/between-the-anticolonial-struggle-and-national-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africasacountry.com">[africasacountry.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africasacountry.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">between the anticolonial struggle and national history</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Africa Is a CountryBetween struggle and national history8 Dec 2023 — The 1959 Pidjiguiti Massacre served as an important historical marke...</span></span></span>

The event itself is not in serious doubt. The mystery is the number and identity of those killed. Published estimates range from about 15 to more than 50, while some accounts also describe scores of wounded people. Such variation is too large to dismiss as simple rounding. It suggests differences in who was counted, whether victims who died later were included, and how much weight was given to colonial records, political testimony or later commemorative accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pidjiguiti_massacre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pidjiguiti massacre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pidjiguiti massacre</span></span></span>

There are several reasons why an exact figure remains difficult to establish. The killings occurred under an authoritarian colonial system with a strong interest in minimising responsibility. Some victims may have been removed by relatives or disappeared into the River Geba before a complete list was compiled. The PAIGC, meanwhile, had reason to emphasise the event’s scale and political meaning. Neither fact proves that a particular total is false; it means that casualty figures need to be traced to their original lists rather than repeated as interchangeable facts.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore cautious: colonial forces killed a substantial number of striking African workers, but the surviving public record does not support one universally accepted death toll. A reliable resolution would require a name-by-name reconstruction using hospital registers, burial records, employer files, police reports, family testimony and contemporary diplomatic correspondence. Until that work is complete, precise figures presented without qualification create a false certainty.

Pidjiguiti’s place in national memory is clearer than its statistics. The massacre symbolises the failure of peaceful petition under Portuguese rule and the transition towards armed resistance. It is remembered not simply as an unexplained killing, but as a moment when the colonial authorities’ response altered the course of Guinea-Bissau’s history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africasacountry.com/2023/12/between-the-anticolonial-struggle-and-national-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africasacountry.com">[Africa Is a Country]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africasacountry.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">between the anticolonial struggle and national history</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Africa Is a CountryBetween struggle and national history8 Dec 2023 — The 1959 Pidjiguiti Massacre served as an important historical marke...</span></span></span>

## Who ordered Amílcar Cabral’s assassination?

Amílcar [Cabral]({{ 'cabral/' | relative_url }}) was shot dead in Conakry, the capital of neighbouring Guinea, on 20 January 1973. He was the PAIGC’s principal strategist and the internationally recognised leader of the struggle to liberate Guinea-Bissau and Cape Verde. His killers came from within the movement: the central gunman was the disgruntled PAIGC naval commander Inocêncio Kani, who was captured after the conspirators attempted to leave with kidnapped party leaders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve06/d66" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

That much is established. The unresolved question is whether the conspirators acted largely from internal grievances or as part of a Portuguese-directed operation.

The internal explanation has considerable force. Cabral’s movement contained tensions between different groups, including disagreements over discipline, advancement and the relationship between Cape Verdean and mainland African members. Kani had personal and political grievances, and the plot required participants with access to Cabral and knowledge of PAIGC operations. A conspiracy rooted entirely outside the movement could not easily have succeeded without exploiting these divisions.

Portuguese involvement is nevertheless plausible. Portugal’s dictatorship had previously demonstrated both the desire and the capacity to attack the PAIGC beyond the borders of Portuguese Guinea. In November 1970, Portuguese forces and allied fighters carried out Operation Mar Verde, an amphibious raid on Conakry intended in part to damage the PAIGC and weaken the Guinean government that sheltered it. The operation makes the idea of continued Portuguese covert activity against Cabral historically credible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museudoaljube.pt/en/2020/11/21/operation-mar-verde/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museudoaljube.pt">[Museu do Aljube]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museudoaljube.pt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Museu do Aljube Operation “Mar Verde”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Museu do Aljube Operation “Mar Verde”</span></span></span>

The strongest contemporary evidence is ambiguous rather than decisive. A declassified United States intelligence assessment recorded that captured conspirators said they were working for the Portuguese. It also noted Portugal’s denial and warned that the prisoners’ accounts might have been extracted under pressure or shaped by Guinea’s president, Ahmed Sékou Touré. The assessment did not establish direct Portuguese control, but concluded that Lisbon’s complicity could not be excluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve06/d66" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Historical Documents</span></span></span>

Claims that Sékou Touré himself arranged Cabral’s death are weaker. Touré was suspicious of independent political organisations operating in Guinea and later presided over harsh reprisals against alleged conspirators. Yet a motive based on jealousy or fear is not proof that he organised the assassination. The surviving evidence supports concern about his conduct after the killing more clearly than it demonstrates that he ordered it.

The most plausible overall interpretation is a layered one: Cabral was killed by PAIGC dissidents whose resentment arose inside the movement, while Portuguese intelligence may have encouraged, contacted or manipulated them. What has not been demonstrated publicly is a documentary chain of command linking the gunmen to a specific Portuguese official. The difference matters. “Portugal benefited” and “Portugal ordered the shooting” are not equivalent claims.

The assassination also produced another unresolved human-rights question. Large numbers of alleged conspirators were detained, and accounts maintain that many were summarily killed during the subsequent purge. Those deaths received far less international attention than Cabral’s murder and remain poorly documented. The effort to solve one political assassination may therefore have generated further uninvestigated killings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Am%C3%ADlcar_Cabral" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Amílcar Cabral</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Amílcar Cabral</span></span></span>

## Was the 1985 coup plot genuine?

In October 1985, President João Bernardo Vieira’s government arrested senior political and military figures accused of planning a coup. Among them were Vice-President Paulo Correia and former attorney-general Viriato Pã. At least 50 people were reportedly detained, many of them from the Balanta community, which had a strong presence in the armed forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/fr/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/POL1000031986ENGLISH.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on amnesty.org.</span></span></span>

Six prominent defendants were sentenced to death in July 1986 and executed by firing squad within days. Amnesty International reported that the proceedings were unfair and that the defendants had been denied adequate protections. Other detainees died in custody, although published accounts differ over the total number of victims connected with the purge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/nws210091986en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on amnesty.org.</span></span></span>

The enduring question is whether the authorities uncovered a real attempt to overthrow Vieira or used the accusation to eliminate rivals.

Correia was not an implausible suspect. He was a powerful former guerrilla commander with support inside the military, and Guinea-Bissau had already experienced intense struggles within the ruling PAIGC. Vieira himself had seized power in the 1980 coup that overthrew Luís Cabral. Political competition was often expressed through military organisation rather than open electoral opposition.

Yet the case against the accused was never tested in a transparent, independent process. The speed of the executions, reports of torture and ill-treatment, the ethnic concentration of the arrests and the broader removal of Correia’s supporters all strengthen the alternative explanation: that a possible discussion of resistance or an ill-defined conspiracy was enlarged into a pretext for a political purge. Later peacebuilding studies have described the episode as part of a deeper pattern in which alleged plots were used to manage rival factions within the state and armed forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eplo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/EPLO_Background-Paper_Guinea-Bissau_EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eplo.org">[EPLO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eplo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on eplo.org.</span></span></span>

After Vieira was driven from office in 1999, a grave containing remains linked to victims of the 1986 repression was reportedly uncovered. The discovery confirmed that the executions and clandestine burials were not merely opposition rumours, but it could not by itself establish whether the original coup allegation had been genuine. Bodies can prove that people were killed; they cannot prove the political charge used to condemn them.

A proper reassessment would require the complete military trial file, detention registers, forensic identification of remains and testimony from surviving officials and prisoners. Until such material is examined openly, the most responsible description is that an alleged plot may have existed, but the trials cannot be relied upon as proof of its scope. The documented unfairness of the process makes the government’s version inseparable from the political interests of Vieira’s regime.

## What connected the killings of 2009?

Guinea-Bissau’s most dramatic modern political mystery unfolded over two days. On 1 March 2009, armed attackers killed the chief of the armed forces, General Batista Tagme Na Waie, in a bomb and rocket attack at military headquarters. Hours later, soldiers entered President João Bernardo Vieira’s residence and killed him. Military spokesmen portrayed Vieira’s death as retaliation by soldiers loyal to Na Waie, but the sequence immediately raised questions about advance planning and competing factions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://issafrica.org/iss-today/double-political-assassinations-in-guinea-bissau-what-future-for-the-country" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: issafrica.org">[issafrica.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">issafrica.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">double political assassinations in guinea bissau what future for the country</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">double political assassinations in guinea bissau what future for the country</span></span></span>

Vieira and Na Waie had a long and bitter relationship. Both were veterans of the liberation war, but later stood on opposing sides of the 1998–99 civil conflict. Each had reason to fear the other, and both had survived earlier violence. This makes personal and military revenge the simplest explanation for the second killing: soldiers who believed Vieira responsible for Na Waie’s death attacked the president.

The first assassination is harder to explain. Possible motives included rivalry within the security establishment, struggles over military appointments, unresolved grievances from the civil war and competition surrounding criminal trafficking networks. Guinea-Bissau had become an important transit point for cocaine moving from Latin America towards Europe, creating new sources of money and influence around an already fragmented state. However, the frequent description of every unexplained killing as “drug-related” can become a substitute for evidence. No publicly demonstrated chain links a particular trafficking organisation to both assassinations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://globalinitiative.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Lucia-Bird-Matt-Herbert-Crime-and-Sanctions-The-case-of-Guinea-Bissau-GI-TOC-January-2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalinitiative.net">[Global Initiative]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalinitiative.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Global Initiative GI-TOC_Crime and Sanctions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global Initiative GI-TOC_Crime and Sanctions</span></span></span>

The circumstances of Vieira’s death were also unusually brutal and public, but identifying the immediate attackers did not answer who authorised them or whether the presidential guard was deliberately neutralised. Nor did it settle whether Na Waie’s killing and Vieira’s killing were two stages of one operation or a first assassination followed by an improvised act of revenge.

Investigations struggled from the beginning. The United Nations reported problems protecting investigators and witnesses, as well as a lack of military cooperation. A separate military commission examined Na Waie’s death, creating the danger that the institution containing potential suspects also controlled crucial evidence. The Security Council repeatedly urged the authorities to accelerate the inquiries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/sites/default/files/en/sc/repertoire/2008-2009/Part%20I/Africa/08-09_Guinea-Bissau.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: main.un.org">[United Nations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">main.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">08 09 Guinea Bissau</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations11. The crisis widened in June 2009. Security forces killed presidential candidate Baciro Dabó and former minister Hélder Proença, claiming that they were involved in a coup plot. Relatives and political allies disputed the allegation, while the United Nations described the new deaths as part of a deeply troubling sequence of political violence. Official peacebuilding documents later observed that the circumstances of the March and June killings remained unclear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2009/jun/05/guinea-bissau-foiled-coup-baciro-dabo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Presidential candidate in alleged coup plot killed</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Presidential candidate in alleged coup plot killed</span></span></span>

This wider pattern weakens the idea that the March deaths can be understood purely as a private feud between Vieira and Na Waie. Even if revenge explains the timing of Vieira’s murder, the subsequent killings suggest a broader contest in which political and military actors used accusations of coup plotting to remove adversaries.

The inquiries made little visible progress, and the 2012 military coup further disrupted institutions capable of pursuing them. Later assessments continued to treat impunity for politically motivated assassinations as a central obstacle to reconciliation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africacenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/ASR02EN-Advancing-Stability-and-Reconciliation-in-Guinea-Bissau-Lessons-from-Africa%E2%80%99s-First-Narco-State.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africacenter.org">[africacenter.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africacenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on africacenter.org.</span></span></span>

## Why these cases remain unresolved

The mysteries share a common structure. Powerful institutions produced the first official explanation, but those same institutions were implicated in the events or benefited from the outcome.

At Pidjiguiti, the colonial authorities controlled the police reports and casualty records. In Cabral’s case, captured conspirators were questioned by an authoritarian Guinean government that conducted extensive reprisals. The 1986 defendants were tried by the regime they were accused of plotting against. In 2009, the military pursued its own inquiry while soldiers and senior officers were potential witnesses or suspects.

Guinea-Bissau’s archival difficulties deepen the problem. Researchers describe valuable collections divided among national institutions, private holdings and archives abroad, with preservation, access and cataloguing remaining uneven. Cabral’s papers, for example, are being preserved through an international digitisation project, illustrating both the richness of the surviving material and the work still needed to make it securely accessible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://legalhistoryinsights.com/out-and-about-a-look-at-the-archives-of-angola-guinea-bissau-and-cape-verde/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: legalhistoryinsights.com">[legalhistoryinsights.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">legalhistoryinsights.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">out and about a look at the archives of angola guinea bissau and cape verde</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A look at the archives of Angola, Guinea-Bissau and Cape...27 Jun 2024 — A young researcher&#x27;s experience in the African archives of Ango...</span></span></span>

Witness evidence is equally fragile. Political upheaval encourages exile, silence and selective memory. People who served in rival factions may describe the same episode differently without either account being wholly invented. Fear also matters: United Nations reporting on the 2009 inquiries specifically noted threats to witnesses and investigators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/sites/default/files/en/sc/repertoire/2008-2009/Part%20I/Africa/08-09_Guinea-Bissau.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: main.un.org">[United Nations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">main.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">08 09 Guinea Bissau</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations11. This helps explain why conspiracy theories flourish. A thin or inaccessible official record leaves space for claims that foreign intelligence services, drug traffickers or hidden ethnic networks controlled every major event. Some such actors may indeed have played roles, but repetition is not corroboration. The best-supported explanations usually involve overlapping forces: personal rivalry, institutional weakness, foreign interference, military factionalism and opportunities created by secrecy.

## What the evidence supports

The strongest conclusions are narrower than the most dramatic stories:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Pidjiguiti was a colonial massacre, but its exact death toll remains unsettled.** The disagreement concerns scale and identification, not whether security forces killed striking workers.
* **Amílcar Cabral was murdered by dissidents from within the PAIGC.** Portuguese encouragement or complicity remains plausible, but publicly available records do not prove a direct order from Lisbon.
* **The 1985–86 case involved real political tension, but the resulting trial was not a trustworthy test of guilt.** The proceedings and executions resemble a purge at least as much as an impartial response to a clearly established coup attempt.
* **Vieira was killed by soldiers after Na Waie’s assassination, but command responsibility for both deaths remains unresolved.** Revenge is persuasive as an immediate motive for Vieira’s murder; it does not explain the entire sequence of killings in 2009.

</div>

These mysteries matter because they shaped more than historical memory. Unresolved killings taught political and military actors that power could be settled violently without a final judicial reckoning. Each incomplete inquiry weakened confidence in the next one. The unanswered question running through Guinea-Bissau’s modern history is therefore not merely who pulled a trigger or planted a bomb, but why the country’s institutions repeatedly failed to establish an authoritative account afterwards.
