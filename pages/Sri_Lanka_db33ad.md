## Introduction

The strongest evidence supports a careful distinction. Sigiriya’s paintings and early inscriptions are real but open to interpretation. Adam’s Bridge is a genuine geological formation surrounded by sacred traditions, yet there is no persuasive archaeological proof that it is a constructed ancient causeway. Stories of King Ravana and a lost race of forest people belong chiefly to legend. By contrast, Sri Lanka’s missing persons and mass graves are documented human-rights and forensic problems whose unresolved details remain central to the country’s recent history.

## Why Sigiriya still resists a single explanation

Sigiriya, the great rock complex in central Sri Lanka, was developed as a royal centre during the reign of King Kassapa I in the late fifth century. The accepted historical outline is dramatic enough: Kassapa seized power after the death of his father, established himself on the rock and was eventually defeated by his brother. Yet the purpose and meaning of several of Sigiriya’s most famous features remain debated. UNESCO describes the site as the ruined capital built by Kassapa, but the surviving architecture also contains gardens, waterworks, terraces, defensive structures, religious traces and an elaborate ceremonial route. It therefore cannot be reduced neatly to either a fortress or a pleasure palace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/202/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreAncient City of SigiriyaThe ruins of the capital built by the parricidal King Kassapa I (477–95) lie on the s...</span></span></span>

The most recognisable puzzle concerns the surviving paintings of richly adorned women on the western face of the rock. Only a small group remains, although evidence suggests that the original painted scheme may have been far larger. They have been identified variously as celestial beings, members of a royal household, women taking part in a religious procession or symbolic figures connected with clouds, rain and fertility. Modern descriptions often present the “celestial maiden” interpretation as settled, but scholarship has continued to debate who the women represent and how they fit into the design of the complex.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379481651_Centuries-Old_Whispers_of_Sentiments_Unveiling_Poetic_Sexual_Fantasies_in_Graffiti_on_Ladies_in_the_Sigiriya_Frescoes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">These frescoes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unveiling Poetic Sexual Fantasies in Graffiti on Ladies in...March 6, 2024 — 2 Apr 2024 — Scholars have discussed the Sigiri...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 6, 2024</span></span></span>

The inscriptions on the polished wall below the paintings deepen rather than solve the puzzle. Visitors between roughly the eighth and tenth centuries scratched poems and comments into its surface, responding to women who had already been painted centuries earlier. These texts contain some of the earliest surviving poetry in the Sinhala language and preserve reactions ranging from admiration to frustration and desire. They prove that medieval visitors regarded the images as compelling female figures, but they do not reveal the intentions of the fifth-century artists who created them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaytimes.lk/100905/Plus/plus_19.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaytimes.lk">[sundaytimes.lk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaytimes.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sundaytimes.lk.</span></span></span>

What is well established is that Sigiriya was a carefully planned royal and symbolic landscape rather than a mysterious structure produced by a lost technology. Its pools, fountains and channels display sophisticated hydraulic engineering, but they fit within the capabilities of ancient Sri Lankan builders. The genuine mystery lies in interpretation: whether Kassapa intended the rock as an earthly palace, a representation of a divine mountain, a statement of kingship or a combination of these ideas. No surviving fifth-century explanation has been found, so architecture, paintings, later chronicles and visitor inscriptions must speak indirectly.

## Adam’s Bridge: sacred causeway or natural shoal?

Between north-western Sri Lanka and southern India lies a chain of sandbanks, reefs and shallow shoals commonly called Adam’s Bridge or Rama’s Bridge. It extends for about 48 kilometres between Mannar Island and Pamban Island, crossing waters that are in places only around a metre deep. Its unusual appearance has made it one of South Asia’s most persistent geographical mysteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islandstudiesjournal.org/article/84214.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandstudiesjournal.org">[Island Studies Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandstudiesjournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Island Studies Journal Do You Believe in Ram Setu?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Adam&#x27;s Bridge, Epistemic...by A Chatterjee · 2023 · Cited by 5 — Adam&#x27;s Bridge is an approximately 48 kilometre-long isthmus of shifting...</span></span></span>

Different religious traditions give the formation different meanings. Hindu tradition associates it with the bridge built for Rama’s passage to Lanka in the *Ramayana*. Islamic and later European traditions connected it with Adam’s journey from Sri Lanka towards the Asian mainland. These interpretations made the shoals part of a sacred landscape long before modern satellite imagery brought them to a global audience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://islandstudiesjournal.org/article/84214.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: islandstudiesjournal.org">[Island Studies Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">islandstudiesjournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Island Studies Journal Do You Believe in Ram Setu?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Adam&#x27;s Bridge, Epistemic...by A Chatterjee · 2023 · Cited by 5 — Adam&#x27;s Bridge is an approximately 48 kilometre-long isthmus of shifting...</span></span></span>

Satellite photographs are sometimes circulated online as proof that the feature was built by humans. They demonstrate only that the chain exists and has a bridge-like form. Geological explanations describe it as a dynamic system produced by sediment movement, former shorelines, reefs, changing sea levels and coastal currents. Its components are not a uniform line of cut stones or masonry, and no securely documented excavation has revealed a continuous human-built foundation beneath it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/295712892_Factors_Involved_In_The_Creation_of_Barrier_Island_Adam%27s_or_Rama_Setu_Bridge_and_Coral_Reefs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate Factors Involved In The Creation of Barrier Island (Adam&#x27;s</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1) is a 30 km (18 miles) long remanent causeway between Rameswaram Island, off the southeastern coast of...Read more...</span></span></span>

This does not mean that people never crossed, modified or navigated the shoals. Lower sea levels periodically connected Sri Lanka more closely to the Indian mainland, allowing the movement of humans and animals. In later historical periods, shallow passages and exposed banks may also have served travellers, fishers or pilgrims. Those possibilities are different from proving that an ancient army constructed the entire formation.

The most plausible conclusion is therefore mixed. Adam’s Bridge is overwhelmingly best understood as a natural coastal formation with a long human history around it. Its sacred identity is historically important, but religious meaning cannot by itself establish artificial construction. The mystery survives because geology, epic geography, national identity and modern politics have become tightly entangled.

## The unresolved geography of disappearance

Sri Lanka’s gravest mysteries concern people who vanished during political violence. Enforced disappearance means more than an unexplained absence: it usually involves detention or abduction by state agents, or by people acting with state support, followed by a refusal to acknowledge the person’s fate or location. Sri Lanka experienced repeated waves of [disappearances]({{ 'disappearances-cab96a/' | relative_url }}) during the 1971 uprising, the southern insurgency of the late 1980s and the civil war between the government and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam.

No universally accepted total exists. The United Nations Working Group on Enforced or Involuntary Disappearances registered more than 12,000 Sri Lankan cases from 1980 to 1997, while the Sri Lankan government previously estimated approximately 27,200 disappearances during 1988–90 alone. The country’s Office on Missing Persons has had to combine complaints gathered by different commissions, ministries, police bodies and military lists, creating problems of duplication, missing records and inconsistent detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://spcommreports.ohchr.org/TMResultsBase/DownLoadPublicCommunicationFile?gId=30101" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spcommreports.ohchr.org">[ohchr.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spcommreports.ohchr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Down Load Public Communication File</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enforced or Involuntary Disappearances registered over 12,000 cases from. 1980-1997. The Government of Sri Lanka, in a...Read more...</span></span></span>

These figures span different conflicts and communities. Many Sinhalese youths disappeared during counter-insurgency operations in the south. In the north and east, Tamil civilians vanished after arrests, checkpoint detentions, surrenders, abductions or military operations. Members of the armed forces and rebel organisations also remain missing. Responsibility cannot be assigned identically in every case: government forces, associated groups, insurgents and the Tamil Tigers have all been accused of abduction or unlawful killing. Nevertheless, United Nations investigations have found credible grounds for examining serious violations and abuses by both state forces and armed groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ohchr.org/en/stories/2024/05/legacy-enforced-disappearances-haunts-sri-lanka" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ohchr.org">[OHCHR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ohchr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">legacy enforced disappearances haunts sri lanka</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">legacy enforced disappearances haunts sri lanka</span></span></span>

For families, the central questions are intensely practical. Was the missing person detained? Were they transferred to a camp or prison? Were they killed, and if so where were they buried? Could surviving records, witnesses or DNA identify them? In thousands of cases, no authoritative answer has been provided. The absence of a body does not allow ordinary mourning, while the absence of a death certificate historically complicated inheritance, remarriage, property rights and access to assistance.

Sri Lanka established the Office on Missing Persons to collect information and trace the missing, but the scale of the task is vast. Its 2023 annual report referred to nearly 15,000 acknowledgement letters sent to people who had submitted complaints since 2021, illustrating that the issue remains active rather than purely historical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.omp.gov.lk/storage/23/1741334202_en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: omp.gov.lk">[omp.gov.lk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">omp.gov.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1741334202 en</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1741334202 en</span></span></span>

## What the mass graves can prove

Mass graves appear to offer physical answers to the disappearance crisis, but excavation alone rarely identifies who was buried, when they died or who killed them. Sri Lanka has repeatedly uncovered burial sites during construction or other accidental ground disturbance. Investigations have often suffered from delayed action, uncertain chains of custody, limited forensic resources and disputes over dating.

Chemmani, near Jaffna, is the most prominent example. In 1998, a soldier convicted in the rape and murder of schoolgirl Krishanthi Kumaraswamy and the killing of members of her family alleged that hundreds of bodies had been buried in the area. Excavations in 1999 recovered 15 sets of remains; at least two were linked through clothing to men who had disappeared following arrest in 1996. Some bodies showed signs consistent with violent death, including binding and blindfolding. Yet the inquiry did not develop into a comprehensive excavation of all the places mentioned in the allegation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://srilankachrd.org/office/uploads/278.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: srilankachrd.org">[srilankachrd.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">srilankachrd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on srilankachrd.org.</span></span></span>

The mystery returned with greater force in 2025, when construction work at a cremation ground in the Chemmani area exposed another mass burial. By August, investigators had recovered 141 skeletons, including children’s remains, along with a schoolbag, small clothes, footwear, bangles, a baby bottle and toys. Later official reporting stated that more than 200 sets of remains had been discovered while the investigation was still continuing. The identities, dates and causes of death were not yet established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/d570beb78890405c73bd0ee75c059e69" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The community and families of those who went missing are urging authorities to continue the investigation and conduct DNA analysis to ide...</span></span></span>

The location and earlier allegations naturally raise suspicion of a connection with civilians who disappeared after government forces retook Jaffna in the 1990s. However, suspicion is not the same as forensic proof. Several armed forces and groups operated in the wider region over many years. Reliable conclusions require careful mapping of burial layers, examination for trauma, radiocarbon dating where appropriate, comparison with dental and medical records, DNA sampling and reference samples from relatives.

Other sites show how easily public assumptions can outrun evidence. At Mannar, remains discovered during building work were initially widely associated with the civil war. Radiocarbon tests on selected samples instead placed them between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries. That result did not explain every aspect of the cemetery, but it substantially altered the leading interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tamilguardian.com/content/five-skeletal-remains-including-child-exhumed-chemmani-mass-grave-seven-more-identified" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tamilguardian.com">[Tamil Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tamilguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tamilguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that mass graves tell us nothing. They can establish that people were buried together, sometimes secretly and sometimes after violence. The lesson is that context must be reconstructed rather than assumed. Sri Lanka has also lacked a fully developed national system for mass-grave investigation and comparison of DNA from remains with relatives of missing people. United Nations experts have repeatedly urged stronger forensic capacity, preservation of evidence and independent oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2025/10/un-committee-enforced-disappearances-publishes-findings-benin-montenegro-and" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ohchr.org">[OHCHR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ohchr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ohchr.org.</span></span></span>

## Where is Prageeth Eknaligoda?

The disappearance of journalist and cartoonist Prageeth Eknaligoda is one of Sri Lanka’s clearest individual cases of an unresolved political disappearance. He left his workplace near Colombo on 24 January 2010, two days before a presidential election, and never arrived home. He was known for work critical of the government of President Mahinda Rajapaksa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.omct.org/en/resources/urgent-interventions/disappearance-of-mr-prageeth-ranjan-bandara-eknaligoda-a-journalist-working-for-the-lankaenews-website" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: omct.org">[OMCT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">omct.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on omct.org.</span></span></span>

For years, basic facts remained obscured by contradictory claims and stalled inquiries. A renewed police investigation beginning in 2015 produced evidence that Eknaligoda had been abducted and taken to an army camp at Giritale. Military intelligence personnel and former soldiers were questioned, and criminal proceedings eventually followed. Amnesty International has reported that investigators concluded he was last seen alive in military custody, although his body has not been recovered and no final account of his fate has been judicially established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2023/01/where-is-prageeth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Where is Prageeth?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Where is Prageeth?</span></span></span>

The case illustrates why an enforced disappearance can remain a mystery even when the broad outline becomes increasingly credible. Investigators may identify vehicles, telephone contacts, detention locations and suspected participants without establishing what happened after the final confirmed sighting. Witnesses may fear retaliation, records may be missing and institutions may resist disclosure. Proceedings can then stretch across changes of government, weakening public confidence and exhausting relatives.

Eknaligoda’s wife, Sandya, transformed the private search for her husband into a public campaign. The case consequently became a symbol of threats against journalists as well as a test of whether Sri Lankan courts could examine alleged involvement by military intelligence. Reporters Without Borders was still calling for a transparent and impartial judicial process in late 2024, nearly 15 years after the disappearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rsf.org/en/sri-lanka-rsf-calls-new-government-ensure-case-disappearance-cartoonist-prageeth-eknaligoda-fully" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rsf.org">[Reporters Without Borders]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rsf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rsf.org.</span></span></span>

The strongest available evidence does not support theories that Eknaligoda voluntarily disappeared or secretly escaped abroad. It points towards abduction and unlawful detention. What remains unknown is who authorised the operation, exactly what happened at or after Giritale, where his remains may be and whether everyone responsible will be identified.

## Chronicles, Ravana and the limits of archaeology

Some Sri Lankan mysteries arise from attempts to read sacred or literary traditions as straightforward historical records. The *Mahavamsa*, composed by Buddhist monks and continued over later centuries, is indispensable to the study of Sri Lankan history. It supplies a long political and religious narrative and has often been supported by inscriptions and archaeology. UNESCO’s Memory of the World nomination describes it as a continuous chronicle whose many historical details have been tested against archaeological and epigraphic findings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/53_131%252B.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1.0 Title of item or collection being proposed 2.0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1.0 Title of item or collection being proposed 2.0</span></span></span>

That does not make every episode literal. Ancient chronicles were written to explain kingship, morality, religion and legitimacy, not to satisfy modern standards of neutral documentation. Accounts of miraculous events, dynastic origins and moral punishment must be read alongside inscriptions, settlement archaeology, environmental evidence and sources from outside the island.

The arrival of Prince Vijaya, traditionally treated as a foundational moment in the origin of the Sinhalese people, is a clear example. Archaeology establishes human settlement in Sri Lanka long before the period assigned to Vijaya and indicates complex prehistoric and early historic communities. The debate is therefore not whether the island was empty before an immigrant prince arrived; it plainly was not. The harder question is whether the chronicle preserves a transformed memory of migration and political change or presents primarily a legitimising origin story. Even archaeologists who value the chronicle do not possess direct material evidence identifying a historical Vijaya.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archaeology.lk/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Prehistory_and_protohistory_of_Sri_Lanka.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeology.lk">[Sri Lanka Archaeology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeology.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prehistory and protohistory of Sri Lanka</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prehistory and protohistory of Sri Lanka</span></span></span>

Claims about King Ravana require still greater caution. Numerous places in Sri Lanka are now linked in tourism and popular culture to the ruler of Lanka in the *Ramayana*. Caves, waterfalls, airfields and palaces are sometimes said to preserve evidence of his technologically advanced kingdom. Yet the Sri Lankan Department of Archaeology has stated that it has no historical evidence for Ravana as an actual ruler or for a definable “Ravana era”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ft.lk/front-page/Buddhika-Pathirana-calls-for-research-on-King-Ravana/44-751729" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ft.lk">[Daily FT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ft.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Daily FTBuddhika Pathirana calls for research on King Ravana</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily FTBuddhika Pathirana calls for research on King Ravana</span></span></span>

Ravana remains culturally important regardless of historicity. Modern retellings have recast him as a learned monarch, local hero, aviation pioneer or symbol of pre-colonial greatness. These interpretations reveal much about present-day identity and memory, but they should not be confused with excavated evidence. A place can possess genuine ancient ruins and later acquire a legendary association without the ruins proving the legend.

## The forest people who left no bones

The Nittaewo are said in Sri Lankan folklore to have been small, hairy, upright forest-dwellers with claw-like nails. Accounts collected in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries claimed that they had lived in the south-east of the island and were eventually trapped in a cave and killed by local forest communities.

The story became attractive to cryptozoologists, who proposed identities ranging from an unknown primate to a surviving archaic human population. Expeditions found caves, animal remains and features attributed by informants to the Nittaewo, but no skeleton, tool industry, habitation layer or DNA has established the existence of a separate species or people. A Sri Lankan archaeological discussion of the tradition acknowledges that no physical evidence has been recovered from excavations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/8158183/Nittaewo_The_Cursed_Child_of_the_Veddah" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

The original accounts are also far removed from direct observation. One influential version, recorded by colonial official Hugh Nevill, passed through several storytellers before reaching him. Later researchers heard similar traditions, which confirms that the story circulated widely but not that its creatures were biological fact.

Several ordinary explanations remain possible. The story may preserve a distorted memory of conflict between human communities. Descriptions may have absorbed features of sloth bears, monkeys or people viewed as outsiders. It may also be a cautionary tale about territorial rivalry and extermination. Without physical evidence, attempts to identify the Nittaewo with *Homo erectus* or another ancient human species are speculation.

This is a useful boundary case. The Nittaewo are a genuine part of Sri Lanka’s recorded folklore, but not a demonstrated lost population. Their mystery concerns the origin and meaning of the tradition rather than the location of an undiscovered tribe.

## What separates a mystery from a modern myth

Sri Lanka’s unresolved history is best understood by ranking claims according to the quality of their evidence.

**Documented but incompletely explained:** Sigiriya’s paintings, inscriptions and design survive physically, although their precise meanings remain disputed. Adam’s Bridge exists, even though its cultural interpretation is contested.

**Probable events with missing final evidence:** In the Eknaligoda case, investigative findings strongly indicate abduction and military detention, but his fate and remains remain unknown.

**Forensic questions awaiting identification:** Chemmani and other mass graves contain real human remains. Their dates, identities and relationships to particular disappearance cases require controlled scientific investigation.

**Literary traditions with partial historical value:** The *Mahavamsa* preserves important names, places and sequences but combines history with religious and dynastic storytelling.

**Legends unsupported by material evidence:** Archaeology has not established Ravana’s kingdom as a historical state or the Nittaewo as a distinct people or species.

The distinction matters because false mystery can obscure real mystery. Claims about advanced lost civilisations may attract more attention than missing archives, unidentified remains or witnesses who have never been heard in court. Sri Lanka does not lack spectacular legends, but its most consequential unresolved questions concern accountability: who was detained, who gave orders, where victims were buried and why investigations repeatedly stopped.

## Why these mysteries still matter

Sri Lanka’s historical puzzles reveal how evidence becomes entangled with identity and power. Sigiriya is interpreted through ideas of kingship, religion and artistic heritage. Adam’s Bridge connects geology with competing sacred geographies. Chronicles shape national origin stories. Ravana has been transformed from an epic antagonist into a modern cultural hero.

Disappearances and mass graves carry a different weight. They are not entertaining riddles but unfinished histories lived by surviving families. Until remains are identified, archives opened and chains of command examined, uncertainty itself can serve political interests. It allows incompatible accounts to persist: that the missing escaped, died in combat, were secretly detained or never existed as reported.

The responsible approach is neither to dismiss every tradition nor to treat every claim as equally credible. Archaeology can confirm occupation without confirming a legendary king. A witness statement can justify investigation without proving every allegation. A mass grave can establish hidden burial before it identifies a perpetrator. A sacred landscape can be historically meaningful even when its physical formation is natural.

Sri Lanka’s enduring enigmas are therefore most revealing at the point where stories meet recoverable evidence. Some may never yield a single answer. Others could be resolved through records, forensic science and sustained legal inquiry. The difference depends not on how mysterious a case sounds, but on whether the surviving evidence is protected, tested and allowed to challenge the stories that have grown around it.
