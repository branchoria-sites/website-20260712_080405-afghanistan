## Introduction

[Bosnia and Herzegovina]({{ 'bosnia-and-herzegovina/' | relative_url }})'s medieval **stećci** are among Europe's most distinctive funerary monuments, yet their carvings remain one of the country's most enduring historical puzzles. More than 70,000 of these monumental stone tombstones survive across Bosnia and neighbouring Croatia, Montenegro and Serbia, with the overwhelming majority found in Bosnia and Herzegovina. Created mainly between the twelfth and sixteenth centuries, many are decorated with raised hands, horsemen, hunting scenes, circles, stars, crosses and mysterious dancing figures. Archaeologists agree these images were meaningful to the communities that commissioned them, but no single symbolic system explains all of them. Instead, the carvings appear to blend local traditions, Christian beliefs, noble identity, everyday life and older artistic influences in ways that still resist simple interpretation. Their ambiguity is precisely what has made the stećci one of Bosnia's greatest archaeological mysteries.

## Who used the stećci burial grounds?

For more than a century, scholars argued over who created the stećci. Early writers often linked them exclusively to the medieval Bosnian Church and the so-called Bogomils, a Christian movement long portrayed as heretical. That interpretation became deeply embedded in nationalist histories but has steadily lost support as archaeological evidence has accumulated. Modern research shows that the monuments were used by members of all three major medieval Christian communities in the region: Catholics, Orthodox Christians and followers of the Bosnian Church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1504/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Stećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreStećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2016</span></span></span>

This broader picture comes from several kinds of evidence:

* Burial grounds associated with Catholic, Orthodox and Bosnian Church communities all contain stećci.
* Inscriptions mention identifiable noble families, local rulers and individuals without indicating a single exclusive religious identity.
* The geographic distribution follows the political and economic landscape of the medieval western Balkans rather than the supposed boundaries of one religious group.
* Decorative styles evolved over several centuries instead of appearing suddenly with one movement or sect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1504/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Stećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreStećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2016</span></span></span>

UNESCO now describes the stećci as an **inter-confessional** tradition rather than the property of one church or ethnic community. This does not eliminate every question about their origins, but it removes one of the oldest and most influential explanations for their symbolism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1504/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Stećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreStećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2016</span></span></span>

## Why are the carvings still so difficult to interpret?

Unlike Egyptian hieroglyphs or Viking runestones, the carvings on the stećci come with no surviving handbook explaining their meaning. Only a minority of monuments bear inscriptions, and even those usually identify the deceased or offer reflections on mortality rather than explaining the accompanying images.

Another difficulty is that the same symbol appears in many different contexts. A raised hand, for example, may occur alone, beside a cross, next to hunting scenes or combined with elaborate geometric patterns. Without contemporary written explanations, archaeologists must compare motifs across hundreds of cemeteries, medieval art and regional traditions rather than relying on direct evidence.

The monuments also span roughly four centuries. A symbol carved in the late twelfth century may not have carried exactly the same meaning by the fifteenth century, making any single interpretation risky.

## Competing meanings of riders, hands and dancing figures

Some of the most recognisable stećci carvings continue to generate debate because several interpretations fit the available evidence.

### The raised hand

The raised right hand is perhaps the best-known symbol on Bosnian stećci.

Possible interpretations include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A gesture of greeting or farewell**, acknowledging visitors to the grave.
* **An oath or legal gesture**, reflecting medieval customs of public testimony.
* **A symbol of divine protection or blessing**, adapted from Christian artistic traditions.
* **A sign of social status**, marking free landowners or members of the nobility.

</div>

No explanation accounts for every example. The same gesture appears on monuments belonging to different regions and social groups, suggesting that it probably carried several related meanings rather than one universal message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1504/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Stećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreStećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2016</span></span></span>

### Horsemen and hunting scenes

Mounted warriors and hunting parties are equally striking.

These scenes have been interpreted as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* representations of noble identity and military status;
* idealised memories of aristocratic life;
* symbols of courage or honour after death;
* echoes of older heroic traditions inherited from earlier Balkan cultures.

</div>

Because similar imagery appears in medieval secular art elsewhere in Europe, most historians no longer see these figures as evidence of a secret religion. Instead, they appear to celebrate status, memory and personal identity alongside religious belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1504/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Stećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreStećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2016</span></span></span>

### Dancing circles

Some stones show groups of people apparently holding hands in circular formations.

This imagery has inspired especially varied explanations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* communal dances connected with seasonal festivals;
* wedding celebrations;
* symbolic representations of unity between the living and the dead;
* purely decorative scenes depicting everyday social life.

</div>

The absence of written descriptions means none of these theories can be confirmed. Similar circle dances existed in parts of the Balkans for centuries, but it is impossible to prove that the carvings depict the same traditions.

## Astronomical theories, religious claims and archaeological limits

Because the symbols are so unusual, they have attracted many speculative explanations.

One recurring idea claims that circles, crescents and stars encode astronomical observations or celestial events. A small number of researchers have even suggested that some carvings preserve memories of spectacular phenomena such as the supernova recorded in AD 1054. While such proposals are imaginative, they remain highly controversial and have not achieved broad acceptance among medieval archaeologists. Similar geometric motifs are widespread in medieval decorative art and do not necessarily record specific astronomical events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">European Historical Evidence of the Supernova of AD 1054 Balkan Medieval TombstonesApril 20, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 20, 2021</span></span></span>

Religious interpretations have been equally contested. Earlier generations frequently argued that nearly every unusual motif reflected Bogomil beliefs. Modern scholarship considers this too simplistic. The archaeological evidence instead suggests that the imagery developed within a shared regional culture influenced by western, eastern and local artistic traditions rather than by a single theological programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://revue.nulk.cz/en/clanky/stecci-the-bogomil-myth-and-nationalist-narratives-in-bosnia-and-herzegovina/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revue.nulk.cz">[Journal of Ethnology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revue.nulk.cz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of EthnologyStećci, the Bogomil Myth, and Nationalist Narratives in Bosnia and Herzegovina - Journal of Ethnology...</span></span></span>

Archaeology also imposes clear limits on interpretation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Most burial grounds were reused over long periods.
* Many monuments have weathered badly, obscuring details.
* Numerous stećci were moved, damaged or reused in later construction.
* Thousands remain undocumented or survive only as fragments.

</div>

These factors make it difficult to reconstruct the original arrangement of monuments or determine whether neighbouring carvings were intended to be read together.

## What the inscriptions reveal—and what they do not

Around a few hundred stećci preserve inscriptions, providing valuable glimpses into medieval Bosnia. They record names, noble titles, family relationships and reflections on death. Some ask passers-by not to disturb the grave, while others remind readers that everyone eventually shares the same fate.

These texts are important because they firmly place the monuments within medieval Christian society rather than an isolated or secretive culture. At the same time, they rarely explain the surrounding reliefs. The inscriptions therefore answer questions about the people buried beneath the stones but leave the symbolism largely unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1504/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Stećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreStećci Medieval Tombstone Graveyards - UNESCO World Heritage CentreJuly 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2016</span></span></span>

## Questions left unresolved by Stecci
The mystery of the stećci is not that historians know nothing about them. Their dates, distribution, craftsmanship and cultural importance are increasingly well understood. The unanswered question is whether their remarkable imagery formed a shared symbolic language or whether each motif carried different meanings depending on family, locality, status and changing traditions.

That uncertainty has wider significance. During the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, competing national movements often claimed the monuments as evidence of exclusive ethnic or religious ownership. UNESCO's recognition of the stećci as a shared cultural heritage of Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Montenegro and Serbia reflects that modern understanding. The monuments remain enigmatic not because they hide a lost code waiting to be cracked, but because they preserve the artistic language of a medieval society whose beliefs, customs and symbols can now only be reconstructed in fragments.
