## Introduction

The Franklin Expedition remains one of [Canada]({{ 'canada/' | relative_url }})'s most compelling historical mysteries not because the broad outline is unknown, but because each new discovery changes the story. The disappearance of HMS *Erebus* and HMS *Terror* after they became trapped in Arctic ice in the late 1840s is no longer a complete mystery. The wrecks have been found, archaeological investigations continue, Inuit oral history has been vindicated in remarkable ways, and modern DNA analysis is identifying individual crew members nearly 180 years after their deaths. Yet the expedition's final months still contain important unanswered questions, particularly about who survived longest, whether either ship was reoccupied after the official abandonment, and whether written records remain sealed inside the wrecks. Together, these discoveries are transforming the Franklin story from a tale of disappearance into an evolving forensic investigation.

## The final confirmed timeline

The last firmly documented events of the expedition come from the famous Victory Point Note, the only surviving official written record left by the expedition itself. The note establishes that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* HMS *Erebus* and HMS *Terror* wintered at Beechey Island in 1845–46.
* Sir John Franklin died on 11 June 1847.
* The ships remained trapped in ice northwest of King William Island.
* On 22 April 1848, 105 surviving officers and men abandoned the vessels and planned to march south towards the Back River.

</div>

Everything after that point must be reconstructed from physical evidence, Inuit testimony and later archaeological discoveries rather than contemporary written records.

This distinction matters. The expedition is no longer mysterious because nothing is known; it is mysterious because the surviving evidence comes from different sources that illuminate only parts of the story. Archaeologists can identify where people died. Inuit witnesses recorded encounters with starving sailors and abandoned ships. The wrecks preserve objects exactly where they were left. None, however, provides a complete narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://parks.canada.ca/lhn-nhs/nu/epaveswrecks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parks.canada.ca">[Parks Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parks.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Parks Canada Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic SiteJune 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2025</span></span></span>

## How Inuit knowledge led searchers back

Perhaps the greatest change in understanding the Franklin Expedition has been the recognition that Inuit knowledge was not simply supplementary evidence but one of the central reasons the modern discoveries became possible.

### Accounts that proved remarkably accurate

Beginning in the 1850s, Inuit described encounters with exhausted European sailors, abandoned camps and eventually deserted ships. British searchers often collected these accounts but struggled to interpret them because they expected precise European-style maps rather than geographically grounded oral history.

Over time, researchers such as David Woodman demonstrated that many apparently conflicting stories described different moments in the expedition's collapse rather than contradicting one another. This reassessment influenced modern search strategies.

Parks Canada now openly acknowledges that Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit—traditional Inuit knowledge—defined the most promising search areas, while underwater archaeology supplied the technical means to investigate them. Officials have stated that without this knowledge the discoveries would have been vastly more difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://parks.canada.ca/lhn-nhs/nu/epaveswrecks/culture/inuit/qaujimajatuqangit" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parks.canada.ca">[Parks Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parks.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Parks Canada Inuit traditional knowledge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inuit traditional knowledge - Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic Site...</span></span></span>

### The discoveries of *Erebus* and *Terror*

The discovery of HMS *Erebus* in 2014 and HMS *Terror* in 2016 dramatically confirmed that Inuit testimony had preserved reliable geographical information across generations.

The ships were located in different positions from where many earlier Western theories had predicted, yet broadly consistent with Inuit reports describing abandoned vessels south of their last recorded position. The validation of these oral histories has become one of the expedition's most significant historical lessons, demonstrating that Indigenous knowledge preserved critical evidence for more than 160 years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/parks-canada/news/2016/09/parks-canada-media-statement-validation-discovery-terror.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Parks Canada Media Statement – Validation of discovery of HMS Terror</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Parks Canada Media Statement – Validation of discovery of HMS Terror - Canada.caFebruary 13, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 13, 2017</span></span></span>

## What the wrecks are revealing

Finding the ships did not solve the mystery overnight. Instead, it opened an entirely new phase of investigation.

### A remarkably preserved time capsule

*Terror* rests in exceptionally good condition beneath Arctic waters. Cabins, storage spaces and many fittings remain intact. *Erebus*, although more exposed to currents and ice movement, has yielded hundreds of artefacts through carefully controlled excavation.

Recovered objects include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* navigation instruments;
* medicine bottles;
* clothing and leather items;
* tableware;
* scientific equipment;
* firearms and ammunition;
* personal possessions from officers and crew.

</div>

Rather than spectacular treasure, these finds provide evidence of everyday life aboard the ships and the practical decisions made during the expedition's final phase. Thousands of photographs and detailed three-dimensional surveys are allowing researchers to document the vessels before natural deterioration accelerates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/parks-canada/news/2024/01/parks-canadas-underwater-archaeologists-complete-seasonal-research-at-wrecks-of-hms-erebus-and-hms-terror-national-historic-site.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Parks Canada’s underwater archaeologists complete seasonal research at Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic Site...</span></span></span>

### The question of surviving documents

One of the most intriguing possibilities is that sealed compartments within the wrecks may still contain written records.

Cold Arctic conditions have preserved many organic materials better than expected, although water damage remains a major concern. Archaeologists therefore avoid unrealistic claims that complete logbooks will necessarily survive. Nevertheless, Parks Canada continues to regard the possibility of recoverable documents as one reason the wrecks remain internationally important archaeological sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://parks.canada.ca/lhn-nhs/nu/epaveswrecks/nature" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parks.canada.ca">[Parks Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parks.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Parks Canada Nature and science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nature and science - Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic SiteOctober 28, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2024</span></span></span>

### Did anyone return to the ships?

One debate has become stronger since the discovery of the wrecks.

The Victory Point Note records that both ships were abandoned in April 1848. Yet the condition and locations of the wrecks have led some researchers to suggest that at least one vessel may later have been reoccupied by part of the surviving crew.

Evidence cited includes the final resting places of the ships, the state of certain equipment and indications that propulsion systems may have been used after the recorded abandonment. None of this constitutes proof, and alternative explanations remain plausible, including natural drifting while trapped in moving ice. The question remains one of the most actively debated aspects of the expedition because it would substantially change the accepted sequence of events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/parks-canada/news/2024/01/parks-canadas-underwater-archaeologists-complete-seasonal-research-at-wrecks-of-hms-erebus-and-hms-terror-national-historic-site.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Parks Canada’s underwater archaeologists complete seasonal research at Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic Site...</span></span></span>

## What bones and DNA reveal

Human remains recovered around King William Island have transformed understanding of the expedition even more than the wrecks themselves.

### From anonymous skeletons to named individuals

For many years researchers could establish only that particular bones belonged to members of the expedition.

Modern genetic techniques have changed that. By comparing DNA extracted from teeth and bones with living descendants, researchers have begun identifying individual sailors with confidence.

Recent work has identified several crew members, including Captain James Fitzjames and, more recently, William Orren, David Young, John Bridgens and Harry Peglar. These identifications do more than restore names. Because each set of remains comes from a documented location, they reveal where specific crew members reached during the desperate retreat and who may have travelled together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/sep/25/sir-john-franklin-1845-expedition-sailor-identified" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This discovery is part of a long-term investigation into Franklin’s crew, who succumbed to Arctic conditions. ### Reconstructing the final journey

Archaeological sites show that the retreat south was not a single organised march ending in one catastrophe.

Instead, evidence points towards a prolonged collapse involving smaller groups moving at different times. Some individuals died close together, while others travelled considerable distances before succumbing. DNA evidence therefore supports a picture of fragmentation rather than one final collective end.

This gradual reconstruction is one reason the Franklin mystery continues to evolve. Every newly identified individual adds another fixed point to a map that was once almost entirely blank.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/archaeology/more-doomed-franklin-expedition-sailors-identified-revealing-clues-about-how-they-tried-to-find-safety" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The victims were part of the ill-fated British mission that departed in 1845 to discover the Northwest Passage. ### Evidence for disease, starvation and cannibalism

Scientific examination of bones has also clarified the expedition's physical decline.

Earlier theories focused heavily on lead poisoning from tinned food or the ships' water systems. Modern research suggests lead exposure probably occurred but was unlikely to have been the single cause of disaster.

Instead, the strongest evidence supports multiple interacting hardships:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* prolonged ice entrapment;
* malnutrition and starvation;
* scurvy caused by vitamin C deficiency;
* hypothermia;
* exhaustion during the overland retreat.

</div>

Cut marks and fractured bones found on some remains also provide compelling evidence that cannibalism occurred during the final stages of survival. ## Why the mystery still matters

The Franklin Expedition is unusual because it is simultaneously becoming better understood and more nuanced.

Major mysteries have already been resolved. The ships are no longer lost. Inuit testimony has been powerfully confirmed. Individual crew members are being identified through DNA, and archaeology has established much of the expedition's final geographical footprint.

Yet important uncertainties remain. Researchers still do not know precisely when the last survivors died, whether anyone successfully sailed either ship after April 1848, whether further written records survive within the wrecks, or exactly how decisions were made during the expedition's final months.

Rather than shrinking into a solved historical puzzle, the Franklin Expedition has become one of Canada's richest examples of how archaeology, Indigenous knowledge, forensic science and genetics can work together. Each new season of research replaces speculation with evidence while reminding us that some of history's most enduring mysteries are solved not by dramatic breakthroughs but by the careful accumulation of many small discoveries.
