## Why was Saruq al-Hadid built in the desert?

[Saruq al-Hadid]({{ 'saruq-al-hadid/' | relative_url }}), on the northern edge of the Empty Quarter in Dubai, is the UAE’s clearest archaeological mystery. People repeatedly used the site over a very long period, but its most remarkable phase came during the early Iron Age, when it became associated with large-scale metalworking and the deposition of weapons, tools, ornaments, pottery and other objects. Excavations have found copper-smelting waste, ingots, unfinished material, scrap and finished artefacts, demonstrating that metal production and recycling genuinely occurred there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ancientarabia.huma-num.fr/dictionary/definition/saruq-alhadid" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ancientarabia.huma-num.fr">[huma-num.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ancientarabia.huma-num.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ancient Arabia Sarūq al-Ḥadīd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ancient ArabiaSarūq al-Ḥadīd - Ancient ArabiaThe site is characterised by archaeological remains dating from the Neolithic to the Islamic...</span></span></span>

The difficulty is its location. Metal production requires ore, fuel and water, yet Saruq al-Hadid lies in a dune landscape far from the mountains where copper ore was obtained and without an obvious permanent water supply. The Polish Centre of Mediterranean Archaeology describes it as a metallurgical centre operating for more than half a millennium, while scientific work portrays at least part of the site as temporary or seasonally used rather than a normal settled town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X18304711" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

One practical explanation is that ancient conditions and supply networks differed from those visible today. Fuel, food, fish, metal and water could have been transported into the area, perhaps along routes connecting the coast, inland oases and the Hajar Mountains. The site may also have served mobile communities whose activities left fewer buildings than a permanent settlement would. Evidence from imported objects and pottery indicates that Saruq al-Hadid belonged to broad exchange networks rather than functioning as an isolated desert workshop.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X18304711" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

Yet economics alone may not explain why so many valuable or usable objects accumulated there. Some appear to have been deliberately deposited rather than simply discarded. Snake images are unusually common, appearing as metal figurines and as decoration on pottery. Analysis of the ceramics has led researchers to suggest that people from different production communities may have brought vessels to the site, possibly for shared ceremonies. This supports the interpretation of Saruq al-Hadid as both a working centre and a place of ritual gathering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/45163456" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

The popular phrase “snake cult” goes further than the evidence permits. Repeated snake imagery probably carried symbolic or religious meaning, but no surviving text identifies a deity, ceremony or priesthood. Snakes might have represented protection, fertility, water, healing, danger or a tradition whose meaning is now unrecoverable. Even the classification of the whole site as a ritual centre remains an interpretation, not a settled fact.

The most plausible answer is therefore a combined one: Saruq al-Hadid was a periodically visited centre where specialised production, exchange and communal ritual overlapped. What remains unexplained is why this particular patch of desert acquired such importance and what participants believed they were doing when they left objects there.

## Has the lost pearl city of Tu’am been found?

Historical sources refer to Tu’am as an important district or settlement on the Gulf coast, noted particularly for pearls. Its precise location was eventually forgotten, producing a long-running geographical puzzle. Some scholars associated the name with a broad territory extending through parts of what are now the UAE and Oman, while others searched for a specific urban centre.

Excavations on Al Sinniyah Island in Umm Al Quwain have made one proposed identification much more persuasive. Archaeologists uncovered a substantial settlement with origins in about the fourth century and a peak during the fifth and sixth centuries. The remains include densely arranged residential buildings beside evidence of pearling activity and a Christian monastery. It is the size, date and economic character of this settlement that have encouraged archaeologists to suggest that it may be Tu’am.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeologymag.com/2024/06/the-lost-city-of-tuam-in-uae/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeologymag.com">[archaeologymag.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeologymag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeologymag.com.</span></span></span>

The discovery fits several parts of the historical picture. The town prospered at a time when Gulf communities supplied pearls to distant markets. Its position within a sheltered lagoon system would have supported boats, fishing and access to offshore pearl banks. The settlement was also larger than other known coastal communities of the same period in the UAE, strengthening the argument that it was a regional centre rather than an ordinary village.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeologymag.com/2024/06/the-lost-city-of-tuam-in-uae/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeologymag.com">[archaeologymag.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeologymag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeologymag.com.</span></span></span>

Nevertheless, archaeology has not produced an inscription reading “Tu’am”. Identifying an ancient place usually requires several forms of evidence to converge: the right date, location, size, economy and relationship to written descriptions. Al Sinniyah performs well against those tests, but the name remains an inference. Future discoveries elsewhere could complicate the identification, while a seal, inscription or administrative document from the island could make it far more secure.

The settlement’s decline also remains open to interpretation. Sixth-century trade disruption, political instability and epidemic disease have all been proposed as contributing factors. The first pandemic of plague affected large parts of the Mediterranean and Near East from the 540s, but its direct impact on Al Sinniyah has not been archaeologically demonstrated. Environmental change, altered trade routes or the gradual movement of population may have been equally important. Claims that one dramatic disaster destroyed Tu’am therefore outrun the present evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newsweek.com/lost-ancient-city-famed-pearls-may-found-1913886" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newsweek.com">[newsweek.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newsweek.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lost Ancient City Famed for Pearls May Have Been Found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lost Ancient City Famed for Pearls May Have Been Found</span></span></span>

Al Sinniyah is best described as the leading candidate for the lost city, not its conclusively proven identity. The larger significance is already clear: it reveals an organised, commercially active and religiously diverse Gulf community immediately before and during the emergence of Islam.

## Where exactly was ancient Magan?

More than four thousand years ago, scribes in Mesopotamia wrote about a distant land commonly rendered in English as Magan. Their texts associated it with seaborne trade, copper and valuable stone. Unlike Mesopotamia, however, southeastern Arabia has not produced a comparable archive naming its rulers, cities or political boundaries. Archaeologists must therefore connect foreign written descriptions with physical remains scattered across modern states.

The broad identification is no longer especially mysterious. Archaeological and geological evidence places Magan principally within what are now Oman and the UAE. Bronze Age sites in both countries show copper production, distinctive monumental tombs and long-distance connections with Mesopotamia and the Indus civilisation. UNESCO’s assessment of the cultural sites of Al Ain explicitly links [ancient Magan]({{ 'ancient-magan/' | relative_url }}) with the UAE and Oman and notes the importance of copper and imported objects in demonstrating those connections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/4295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Decision 35 COM 8B.24 Cultural Properties</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Magan (the UAE and Oman) was the source of much Mesopotamian copper. Criterion (iv): Each of the Cultural Sites of Al Ain yielded an outs...</span></span></span>

The unresolved question is what “Magan” represented. It may have been a single kingdom at certain times, a Mesopotamian label for several communities, a trading region or a shifting political landscape centred on copper-producing mountains and coastal ports. Modern national borders cannot simply be projected backwards onto the third millennium BC. The same trade system could have included mines and settlements in Oman, oases around Al Ain, and ports along the coast of the present UAE.

Sites such as Hili and Tell Abraq show why the UAE belongs at the centre of the discussion. Hili preserves large communal tombs, settlement remains and evidence of increasingly sophisticated water management. Tell Abraq has produced objects and materials linking southeastern Arabia to Mesopotamia and the Indus world, including foreign-style weights, beads and ceramics. Such discoveries demonstrate participation in the economic world described by Mesopotamian texts, even when they cannot reveal what local inhabitants called themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/4295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Decision 35 COM 8B.24 Cultural Properties</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Magan (the UAE and Oman) was the source of much Mesopotamian copper. Criterion (iv): Each of the Cultural Sites of Al Ain yielded an outs...</span></span></span>

Magan should therefore not be imagined as a “lost civilisation” awaiting discovery beneath one desert mound. The civilisation is already visible in mines, tombs, settlements and imported goods. What has been lost is its internal voice. Without local written archives, its political structure, language and precise territorial extent remain uncertain.

## How did the great port of Julfar disappear?

Julfar was one of the most important medieval trading and pearling centres on the southern Gulf. Historical accounts describe a port connected to routes reaching Arabia, East Africa, India, Southeast Asia and China. Archaeological discoveries north of modern Ras al-Khaimah include extensive settlement remains, imported ceramics and evidence for locally produced pottery known as Julfar ware.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10114859/1/aae.12162.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.ucl.ac.uk">[ucl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCL DiscoveryA reconsideration of the development of Julfarby RA Carter · 2020 · Cited by 11 — The archaeological site of Julfar consiste...</span></span></span>

The mystery is not whether Julfar existed, but where it was at different times. The name appears to have referred variously to a district, an oasis area and successive port settlements. Early activity has been associated with Kush near Shimal, while later medieval occupation expanded at Al Mataf and Al Nudud. From around the sixteenth century, maritime activity increasingly centred on the site of modern Ras al-Khaimah.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10114859/1/aae.12162.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.ucl.ac.uk">[ucl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCL DiscoveryA reconsideration of the development of Julfarby RA Carter · 2020 · Cited by 11 — The archaeological site of Julfar consiste...</span></span></span>

This movement was partly environmental. Lagoons, creeks and channels along the coast changed as sediment accumulated. A harbour that had once offered sheltered access could become shallow or difficult to navigate. UNESCO’s tentative listing for the trading town notes that silting behind Al Mataf and Al Nudud helped shift the principal port towards Ras al-Khaimah.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6465/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Political and military pressures also mattered. Julfar operated within the commercial sphere of the Kingdom of Hormuz and later faced Portuguese, Persian and Omani competition and intervention. Researchers have debated the exact chronology of decline because different parts of the wider settlement system did not necessarily fail at the same moment. A shortage of later imported ceramics at one site may indicate abandonment there, while written references to Julfar may concern a successor settlement carrying the same name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10114859/1/aae.12162.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.ucl.ac.uk">[UCL Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UCL DiscoveryA reconsideration of the development of Julfarby RA Carter · 2020 · Cited by 11 — The archaeological site of Julfar consiste...</span></span></span>

Julfar therefore “vanished” through relocation rather than a single catastrophic destruction. Its people and commercial functions moved as waterways, political power and trading opportunities changed. The case warns against assuming that an old place-name must correspond to one fixed point on a modern map.

## Deserted villages: mystery, memory and modern myth

The UAE’s rapid twentieth-century transformation has created a newer category of supposed mystery: abandoned villages overtaken by sand. Photographs of empty houses and partially buried rooms are often accompanied online by stories of ghosts, sudden evacuations or supernatural forces. These accounts are visually effective but usually poorly sourced.

Al-Ghuraifa in Sharjah, sometimes promoted as a mysterious “ghost village”, illustrates the problem. It was a planned settlement built in the 1970s for families moving from semi-nomadic life into permanent housing. It appears to have been abandoned gradually in the 1990s as residents sought better services and opportunities elsewhere. Its sand-filled buildings preserve evidence of a period of exceptionally rapid social change, but there is no solid basis for claims of a sudden unexplained disappearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/67d2db1b302c6756af167f567ab9f0c9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

Such places remain historically valuable. They document changes in housing, employment, transport and state development during the decades after federation. Their mystery lies less in why residents fled than in how quickly a once-new settlement became obsolete. Folklore may become part of a site’s later identity, but it should not be confused with the documented history of abandonment.

## What the evidence resolves — and what it cannot

The UAE’s strongest historical mysteries are rarely blank spaces. Excavation has already answered many of the basic questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Saruq al-Hadid really was a major centre of metalworking and object deposition, but its desert location and ritual meaning remain debated.
* Al Sinniyah preserves a large late antique pearling town and is the strongest candidate for Tu’am, although no inscription has confirmed the name.
* Magan can be located broadly in the UAE and Oman, but its boundaries, political organisation and local identity remain unknown.
* Julfar was a real commercial centre whose location shifted as coastlines and communities changed, rather than a city that simply vanished overnight.
* Modern “ghost villages” usually reflect migration and development, not supernatural evacuation.

</div>

These cases endure because archaeological evidence is rich in objects but poor in explanations. Slag proves that metal was worked; snake images prove that the symbol mattered; imported ceramics prove that goods travelled. None of them records what an Iron Age visitor prayed for, what the inhabitants of Al Sinniyah called their town, or whether a Bronze Age trader thought of Magan as a kingdom, homeland or foreign label.

That gap between material fact and human meaning is the central historical mystery of the United Arab Emirates. The landscape is not silent, but it speaks in fragments: a buried harbour, a communal tomb, a field of metal waste, a pearl settlement and a symbol repeated without an accompanying text. Responsible interpretation begins by recognising how much those fragments reveal—and where the evidence still requires uncertainty.
