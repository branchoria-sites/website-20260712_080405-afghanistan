## Introduction

The Monument to the Unknown Hero on Mount Avala, just south of Belgrade, is one of [Serbia]({{ 'what-still-haunts-serbias-historical/' | relative_url }})'s most important national memorials. Yet it embodies two enduring historical mysteries at once. The first concerns the identity of the young Serbian soldier buried beneath it. Despite repeated attempts to reconstruct his story, no reliable evidence has ever revealed his name. The second concerns the monument itself: why King Alexander I ordered the demolition of the medieval fortress of Žrnov in 1934 so that an entirely new state monument could rise in its place. Both mysteries have shaped public memory for decades. One centres on an unknown individual who came to represent thousands of unnamed war dead; the other reflects how governments reshape landscapes to express new political ideals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Unknown_Hero" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span></span></span>

## What is known about the buried soldier

Unlike many unknown soldier memorials, Avala's monument stands above the remains of a man who had already been buried on the battlefield rather than selected later from military cemeteries. During the First World War, Serbian defenders fought around Mount Avala as Austro-Hungarian forces advanced towards Belgrade. Local people buried an unidentified Serbian soldier close to the medieval fortress of Žrnov beneath a simple wooden cross.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%BDrnov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

In 1921, a state commission opened the grave to determine whether the burial genuinely belonged to a Serbian combatant before creating a formal memorial. The examination established several important facts:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The skeleton belonged to a young male, probably around 19 or 20 years old, though some later accounts suggested he might have been even younger.
* The left side of the chest had been shattered by artillery fragments, indicating death from shellfire.
* Fragments of the shell that apparently killed him were found beneath the skull.
* No identification disc or personal documents survived.
* Military equipment and burial circumstances strongly indicated that he had been a Serbian soldier killed during the defence of Belgrade in 1915.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%BDrnov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

The remains were reburied with military honours inside a modest stone monument erected in 1922. When the larger national memorial was completed in 1938, the body was transferred into the crypt beneath today's monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%BDrnov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Why proposed identities remain unproven

The mystery persists because almost every clue points towards a broad category rather than an individual.

The missing identity disc is particularly important. Contemporary investigators suggested that this could mean the soldier belonged to the so-called Third Call regiments, formations composed largely of older reservists who were not always equipped like front-line units. Others argued instead that he was a very recent recruit mobilised during the desperate defence of Belgrade before standard identification procedures had been completed. Neither explanation can be proved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%BDrnov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

His apparent youth has encouraged additional speculation. Descriptions of the skeleton as slight or "boyish" led some writers to imagine that he had been an under-age volunteer. Serbia did indeed have famous youthful volunteers during the First World War, but nothing recovered from the grave connects the Avala soldier to any documented individual. These suggestions therefore remain possibilities rather than evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%BDrnov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Modern forensic science has transformed the identification of many historical unknown soldiers elsewhere in the world through DNA analysis and archival comparison. At Avala, however, there has been no comparable programme capable of matching the remains to descendants of a specific missing serviceman. Even if such testing were technically possible, the lack of a shortlist of likely identities would make confirmation exceptionally difficult.

For this reason, historians generally regard the Avala soldier as permanently anonymous. His symbolic role is considered more important than discovering a single name. Like other unknown soldier memorials established after the First World War, he represents thousands of families whose relatives never returned or could never be identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Unknown_Hero" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span></span></span>

## How Žrnov was erased to build a national memorial

The second mystery concerns not the man beneath the monument but the landscape above him.

For centuries, the summit of Avala had been occupied by Žrnov, a fortified site with Roman origins that later became an important medieval Serbian fortress. Although damaged over time, substantial remains still stood during the early twentieth century. Rather than incorporating the ruins into a memorial, King Alexander I ordered their complete destruction in 1934. Military engineers demolished the fortress with explosives over two days to clear the summit for an entirely new monument designed by the sculptor Ivan Meštrović.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%BDrnov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The decision immediately proved controversial.

Many writers, historians and members of the public argued that the fortress itself was a valuable historical monument deserving preservation. Among the best-known critics was the playwright Branislav Nušić, who opposed destroying a medieval landmark to create a modern memorial. Their objections failed to alter the king's decision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%BDrnov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

King Alexander laid the foundation stone on 28 June 1934, only a few months before his assassination in Marseille. Construction continued after his death, and the completed monument was inaugurated in 1938.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Unknown_Hero" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span></span></span>

## Why the demolition still puzzles historians

Unlike the unknown soldier's identity, the destruction of Žrnov is not mysterious because the event lacks documentation. The explosions, construction and political decisions are well recorded. The unresolved question is motivation.

Several explanations have been proposed.

**Creating a Yugoslav national symbol.** The most widely accepted interpretation is that King Alexander wanted a monumental memorial expressing the unity of the Kingdom of Yugoslavia after the Balkan Wars and the First World War. A commanding summit overlooking Belgrade provided an ideal symbolic location. The existing fortress did not communicate that new political message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Unknown_Hero" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span></span></span>

**Replacing regional history with state memory.** Some historians see the project as an attempt to shift attention away from a specifically medieval Serbian fortress towards a memorial representing all peoples of the Yugoslav kingdom. The monument's eight caryatid figures symbolise women from different regions of Yugoslavia rather than a single national tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spomenikdatabase.org/avala" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spomenikdatabase.org">[spomenikdatabase]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spomenikdatabase.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spomenik Databse &#124; Soviet Veterans Memorial at Mt. Avala</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spomenik Databse &#124; Soviet Veterans Memorial at Mt. Avala...</span></span></span>

**An unexplained personal insistence.** The most debated point is why demolition was considered necessary at all. Contemporary critics argued that the fortress could have been preserved alongside the monument. Surviving records do not fully explain why King Alexander insisted on complete removal rather than architectural coexistence. Historians therefore acknowledge that the precise reasoning behind this decision remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%BDrnov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Because the fortress was entirely demolished, opportunities for later archaeological investigation became extremely limited. The decision permanently changed the summit of Avala and transformed an ancient defensive site into a modern ceremonial landscape.

## Why both mysteries continue to matter

The Unknown Hero Monument combines two different forms of historical uncertainty.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Unknown_Hero" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span></span></span>

The identity of the buried soldier is an ordinary wartime mystery created by incomplete records, battlefield destruction and anonymous burial. There is no credible evidence for hidden identities or political concealment; the most likely explanation is simply that his name was genuinely lost amid the chaos of 1915.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%BDrnov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The disappearance of Žrnov reflects a different kind of mystery. Here, the facts are known, but the deeper reasoning behind an irreversible political decision remains open to interpretation. The fortress was deliberately sacrificed so that a new national symbol could occupy Serbia's most prominent hilltop memorial site, yet historians still debate whether that sacrifice was necessary or justified.

Together, these two stories illustrate how national memory is shaped both by what history forgets and by what governments choose to replace. Beneath Avala rests a soldier whose name has vanished; above him stands a monument built where another part of Serbia's past was intentionally erased.
