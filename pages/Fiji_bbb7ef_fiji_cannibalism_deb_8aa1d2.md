## Introduction

Did Fiji’s bones really prove cannibalism? The short answer is: some archaeological evidence strongly supports cannibalism at particular sites, but modified human remains alone do not automatically prove that human flesh was eaten. Cut marks, burning, deliberate breakage and bone fragmentation can result from several different practices, including warfare, mortuary rituals and the preparation of bodies after death. The real historical puzzle lies in separating these possibilities.

This question matters because colonial-era writers made Fiji famous as the "Cannibal Isles", often presenting cannibalism as the defining feature of Fijian society. Modern archaeology paints a much narrower picture. Instead of accepting or rejecting every historical account, researchers compare human bones with animal remains, burial practices and known mortuary customs to determine which cases genuinely indicate consumption and which are better explained by other rituals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09676-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Nature Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer Nature LinkThe Archaeology of Cannibalism: a Review of the Taphonomic Traits Associated with Survival and Ritualistic Cannibalis...</span></span></span>

## How colonial accounts shaped the story

European missionaries, traders and colonial officials left numerous descriptions of cannibalism in nineteenth-century Fiji. These accounts profoundly influenced how Fiji was viewed overseas. Stories of ritual killings, cannibal feasts and specialised eating implements appeared repeatedly in travel literature and missionary reports, helping create an image of Fiji as exceptionally violent and exotic.

Modern historians treat these sources carefully rather than dismissing or accepting them outright. Several factors complicate their interpretation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Missionaries often wished to emphasise the moral importance of conversion to Christianity.
* Colonial governments benefited from portraying indigenous societies as needing outside control.
* Some travellers repeated dramatic stories that they had not personally witnessed.
* Oral testimony collected decades later could mix remembered events with legend.

</div>

At the same time, it would also be misleading to assume that every colonial account was fabricated. Multiple independent observers recorded similar practices in different regions and periods. The challenge is determining which reports describe real events, which exaggerate them, and how representative they were across Fiji as a whole. Recent archaeological reviews argue that historical documents should always be tested against physical evidence rather than treated as proof on their own.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09676-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Nature Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer Nature LinkThe Archaeology of Cannibalism: a Review of the Taphonomic Traits Associated with Survival and Ritualistic Cannibalis...</span></span></span>

## What modified bones can actually show

Archaeologists study human bones using the same methods applied to butchered animal remains. Individual marks rarely answer the question by themselves. Instead, researchers look for patterns.

Among the most important indicators are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Cut marks** made by stone or metal tools while removing flesh.
* **Burning** that occurred before burial rather than through later accidental fires.
* **Fresh-bone fractures**, showing bones were deliberately broken while still containing marrow.
* **Percussion marks**, where bones were struck with tools.
* **Peeling**, caused when fresh bone is bent during butchery.
* **The archaeological context**, especially whether human bones occur mixed with food refuse instead of formal burials.

</div>

None of these observations alone proves cannibalism. Bodies prepared for secondary burial, ritual defleshing or other mortuary practices can also show cut marks. Conversely, cannibalism involving only soft tissue may leave little or no trace on skeletons. Archaeologists therefore rely on combinations of evidence rather than any single feature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09676-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Nature Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer Nature LinkThe Archaeology of Cannibalism: a Review of the Taphonomic Traits Associated with Survival and Ritualistic Cannibalis...</span></span></span>

## Why Navatu became one of Fiji's strongest archaeological cases

The best-known archaeological evidence comes from the Navatu site, where researchers compared formal burials with a separate midden—a refuse deposit—containing both human and animal bones.

The comparison produced a striking pattern. Human bones from the midden displayed many of the same modifications seen on butchered animal remains. Researchers documented extensive fresh breakage, burning, cut marks, peeling and percussion damage. By contrast, individuals buried formally at the same site showed very little of this treatment beyond ordinary post-depositional damage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10502244/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Fijian cannibalism: osteological evidence from Navatu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fijian cannibalism: osteological evidence from Navatu - PubMed...</span></span></span>

Equally important was where the bones were found. The heavily modified human remains had been discarded among everyday food waste rather than placed in graves. Researchers also ruled out many natural explanations such as weathering, animal scavenging, trampling or wave action, concluding that most modifications resulted from deliberate human activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10502244/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Fijian cannibalism: osteological evidence from Navatu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fijian cannibalism: osteological evidence from Navatu - PubMed...</span></span></span>

Because the treatment of human bones closely paralleled that of butchered mammals, the Navatu assemblage remains one of the strongest archaeological arguments that at least some individuals in prehistoric Fiji were processed and consumed after death.

## Cannibalism or mortuary ritual?

The question becomes more difficult at other sites.

Research at Vunda illustrates why archaeologists avoid broad conclusions. Although modified human remains were present, the pattern differed from Navatu. Human bones showed relatively few cut marks and less evidence of burning and percussion than accompanying animal remains. Some researchers concluded that the evidence did not fit straightforward butchery and could instead reflect mortuary practices involving limited manipulation of bodies after death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/253635380_Fijian_cannibalism_and_mortuary_ritual_Bioarchaeological_evidence_from_Vunda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This distinction is crucial because many societies alter bodies without consuming them. Possible explanations include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Cleaning bones before reburial.
* Ritual disarticulation of corpses.
* Ceremonial handling of the dead.
* Warfare-related treatment of enemies.
* Symbolic removal of small amounts of tissue.

</div>

Modern archaeological research therefore asks not simply, "Are there cut marks?" but whether the entire pattern resembles food processing more closely than known burial customs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09676-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Nature Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer Nature LinkThe Archaeology of Cannibalism: a Review of the Taphonomic Traits Associated with Survival and Ritualistic Cannibalis...</span></span></span>

## Why the debate remains complicated

The mystery persists because archaeological evidence can rarely reconstruct exactly what happened to an individual body.

Several limitations remain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Most excavated assemblages are incomplete.
* Tropical environments accelerate bone decay.
* Earlier excavations often lacked modern recording standards.
* Historical descriptions do not always identify the specific communities or events to which they refer.
* Different forms of cannibalism—whether associated with warfare, ritual or funerary practice—may leave similar skeletal signatures.

</div>

Recent reviews of archaeological evidence emphasise that bone modifications alone cannot reliably distinguish every motivation for cannibalism. Context—including historical accounts, burial practices, artefact associations and site organisation—remains essential.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s10816-024-09676-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Archaeology of Cannibalism: a Review of the Taphonomic Traits Associated with Survival and Ritualistic Cannibalism &#124; Journal of Ar...</span></span></span>

## What the evidence now suggests

The balance of evidence has shifted away from two extremes. Archaeologists no longer accept every dramatic colonial story as literal fact, but neither do they reject all reports as colonial invention.

Instead, the current picture is more nuanced:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Some Fijian archaeological sites, particularly Navatu, provide convincing physical evidence consistent with cannibalism.
* Other modified human remains are better explained by mortuary treatment or remain ambiguous.
* Colonial literature greatly amplified cannibalism's role in Fiji's international image, making it appear more universal and central than archaeological evidence supports.
* The strongest conclusions come from combining osteological analysis with careful historical and archaeological context rather than relying on either alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10502244/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Fijian cannibalism: osteological evidence from Navatu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fijian cannibalism: osteological evidence from Navatu - PubMed...</span></span></span>

</div>

Rather than proving that all colonial accounts were true or false, Fiji's modified human remains reveal a more complex history. They show that cannibalism almost certainly occurred in some circumstances, while also demonstrating that the treatment of human bodies cannot automatically be equated with eating them. That distinction is the key to understanding one of Fiji's most enduring historical debates.
