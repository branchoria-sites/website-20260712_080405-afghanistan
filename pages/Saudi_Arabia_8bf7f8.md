## Introduction

Some of these mysteries are now partly solved. Desert “kites”, once mistaken for walls or ritual symbols, are firmly identified as animal traps. Excavation has shown that the vast rectangular monuments known as [mustatils]({{ 'mustatils/' | relative_url }}) had ceremonial uses. Yet important questions remain: who organised their construction, what beliefs they expressed, why prosperous settlements were deserted and how ancient communities survived periods of severe aridity. The evidence supports a history of sophisticated societies adapting to environmental change—not fantasies of vanished super-civilisations.

## Why were thousands of mustatils built?

Among Saudi Arabia’s strangest archaeological features are long, low-walled rectangles scattered across the north-west. Researchers call them mustatils, meaning “rectangles”. More than 1,600 have been identified through satellite surveys, aerial photography and fieldwork, although only a small proportion have been excavated. Many are tens or hundreds of metres long, and some were constructed on slopes or ridges where they would have been highly visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09596836241275010" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsLandscape positioning of Neolithic mustatil stone...Sep 11, 2024 — More than 1600 mustatils have been identified by the Aer...</span></span></span>

Their age makes them especially remarkable. Excavated examples date mainly to the late sixth millennium BC, making them older than Stonehenge and the Egyptian pyramids. Their walls were generally too low to contain livestock or defend a settlement, while their narrow internal spaces were poorly suited to ordinary habitation. This weakened early suggestions that they were pens, houses or territorial boundaries. Research published in *Antiquity* instead identified them as part of one of the world’s earliest known monumental ritual landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/mustatils-cult-and-monumentality-in-neolithic-northwestern-arabia/80E2AD8B538C268E7CA5C3C29CB4EC52" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe mustatils: cult and monumentality in Neolithic north-...by H Thomas · 2021 · Cited by 51 — Re...</span></span></span>

Excavation east of AlUla provided the clearest evidence. Archaeologists found carefully arranged animal horns and skull fragments, particularly from cattle, goats and gazelles, deposited around a standing stone inside the monument. A later human burial had also been placed there. The excavators interpreted the animal remains as offerings, possibly brought by pastoral communities gathering from the surrounding region. They cautiously used the idea of “pilgrimage” to describe repeated journeys to a shared ceremonial site, not to suggest an organised religion identical to later traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0281904" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Excavations at a mustatil east of AlUla &#124; PLOS Oneby M Kennedy · 2023 · Cited by 29 — Excavations at this site (IDIHA-0008222) reveal...</span></span></span>

The broad function is therefore less mysterious than it once was: mustatils were ceremonial monuments associated with animal offerings and communal activity. What remains unknown is the meaning of the ceremonies. Cattle may have represented wealth, fertility, rain or group identity, but no writing survives to confirm any interpretation. Nor is it clear whether all mustatils served precisely the same purpose over their long period of construction.

Their scale raises another puzzle. Building hundreds of substantial monuments across a sparsely populated landscape required planning, labour and agreement over access to land. The structures suggest that mobile herders could create large cooperative networks without cities, palaces or permanent bureaucracies. Their importance lies partly in overturning the old assumption that monumental architecture emerged only after settled urban life.

## What do the desert kites reveal about prehistoric hunting?

From the air, another class of structure resembles a child’s drawing of a kite: two long stone lines converge upon an enclosure or a cluster of pits. Thousands exist across the Middle East and Central Asia, including dense concentrations on the lava fields around AlUla and Khaybar. A remote-sensing survey found 32 in AlUla County and another 175 in nearby parts of the same volcanic landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/kites-of-alula-county-and-the-%E1%B8%A5arrat-%CA%BFuwayri%E1%B8%8D-saudi-arabia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research-repository.uwa.edu.au">[the UWA Profiles and Research Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research-repository.uwa.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">kites of alula county and the ḥarrat ʿuwayriḍ saudi arabia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the UWA Profiles and Research RepositoryKites of AlUla County and the Ḥarrat ʿUwayriḍ, Saudi Arabiaby R Repper · 2022 · Cited by 18 — A c...</span></span></span>

For much of the twentieth century, their purpose was disputed. Suggestions ranged from defensive works and livestock enclosures to ritual monuments. Archaeological surveys, landscape analysis and the discovery of terminal pits have now made the central explanation convincing: most were large hunting traps. Groups drove gazelles, ibex or other wild animals between the guiding walls towards pits or confined killing areas. The walls did not necessarily have to be high; hunters could use movement, noise and the animals’ reluctance to cross even modest barriers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hal.science/hal-03678385/file/Barge%20et%20al-2022-open%20kites.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal.science">[hal.science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal.science</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Barge et al 2022 open kites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saudi Arabia, of a sample of open...</span></span></span>

This solution creates new questions. Some installations were enormous, requiring coordinated construction and detailed knowledge of animal routes. Researchers still debate how often they were used, whether they trapped whole herds or smaller groups, and how their design changed over thousands of years. So-called open or early forms may represent stages in the development of the classic enclosed kite, but firm dates are difficult because the structures contain little material suitable for scientific dating.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hal.science/hal-03678385/file/Barge%20et%20al-2022-open%20kites.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hal.science">[HAL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hal.science</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Barge et al 2022 open kites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saudi Arabia, of a sample of open...</span></span></span>

There is also uncertainty about their environmental effect. Mass hunting may have reduced local animal populations, particularly when climate conditions were worsening. Yet it would be an exaggeration to claim that kites alone caused regional extinctions. The available evidence shows technological sophistication and collective hunting, but it cannot establish how many animals were killed over the lifetime of each structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X21002078" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New insights into the &#x27;desert kite&#x27; phenomenon in Arabiaby HS Groucutt · 2021 · Cited by 22 — Over 6,000 &#x27;desert kites&#x27;—mass...</span></span></span>

Mustatils and kites can look confusingly similar in satellite images, encouraging internet claims that all ancient stone patterns had one mysterious purpose. Excavation shows otherwise. Kites were primarily practical hunting machines; mustatils were ceremonial spaces. The distinction demonstrates why shape alone is not enough to explain an archaeological site.

## Why did a Babylonian king rule from Tayma?

One of the most unusual episodes in Saudi Arabia’s recorded ancient history occurred during the sixth century BC. [Nabonidus]({{ 'nabonidus/' | relative_url }}), the last king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, left Babylon and spent about ten years at Tayma, a major oasis in north-western Arabia. His son governed in Babylonia while the king campaigned through northern Arabia and established a royal presence far from his empire’s traditional centre. Ancient royal documents confirm the absence, but they do not provide a neutral, complete explanation for it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_Sp-II-964" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">W Sp II 964</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">W Sp II 964</span></span></span>

The main written source is the Nabonidus Chronicle, a damaged cuneiform tablet now in the British Museum. It records that the king was at Tayma and notes the resulting disruption of Babylon’s important New Year festival. However, the surviving tablet is a later copy, parts are missing and the broader documentary tradition was shaped by people who had reasons to criticise Nabonidus after the Persian conquest of Babylon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_Sp-II-964" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">W Sp II 964</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">W Sp II 964</span></span></span>

Several explanations compete:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Trade and imperial expansion:** Tayma stood on caravan routes linking southern Arabia, the Levant and Mesopotamia. Controlling the oasis could provide revenue and influence over regional commerce.
* **Military strategy:** Nabonidus may have wanted to secure northern Arabian oases and prevent rivals from dominating routes near Babylonian territory.
* **Religious motives:** The king was strongly devoted to the moon god Sîn. Religious connections in northern Arabia may have made Tayma personally significant to him.
* **Political conflict at home:** His religious policies may have antagonised Babylon’s powerful priestly institutions, making prolonged absence useful or necessary.
* **A new western power base:** Building activity at Tayma suggests more than a temporary visit, although there is insufficient evidence that Nabonidus intended permanently to replace Babylon as his capital.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabonidus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

No single theory explains why the king himself remained for so long. Commercial or military objectives could have been managed by officials. Religious devotion alone seems inadequate for a decade-long relocation of the monarch. Political tension in Babylon is plausible, but much of the hostile evidence was produced or preserved under Nabonidus’s enemies.

The most reasonable conclusion is that several motives overlapped. Tayma offered strategic and economic advantages, while Nabonidus’s personal religious interests and difficulties within Babylon encouraged an unusually direct royal commitment. What cannot be recovered is the balance between those motives. The mystery survives because the decisive evidence—the king’s own full account of his decision—has not been found.

## Why were thriving settlements abandoned?

### The walled town at Khaybar

At the Khaybar oasis, archaeologists have identified a fortified Bronze Age town called al-Natah. Built between about 2400 and 2000 BC, it occupied roughly 2.6 hectares and contained compact residential buildings, streets, a probable administrative area and an adjacent cemetery. Estimates suggest a population of about 500 people. The settlement lasted until at least 1500 BC and perhaps as late as 1300 BC, possibly with interruptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0309963" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on plos.org.</span></span></span>

Al-Natah matters because it reveals a form of gradual urbanisation distinct from the much larger cities of Egypt and Mesopotamia. Its residents lived in organised, defended communities supported by oasis agriculture, but they did not create a vast city-state. This “slow urbanism” suggests that ancient Arabian communities followed their own path between mobile pastoralism and fully urban life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0309963" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on plos.org.</span></span></span>

Why the town was abandoned remains unknown. Environmental stress, declining water availability, damaged trade connections, conflict and changes in regional settlement patterns are all possible. The excavated area is still limited, and there is no surviving text naming the inhabitants or describing the town’s end. Claims that a single drought or invasion destroyed al-Natah therefore go beyond the evidence.

### The sudden end of Al-Faw

Farther south, near the edge of the Empty Quarter, Al-Faw became a major commercial and cultural centre on routes connecting southern Arabia with the peninsula’s interior. Archaeologists have documented houses, roads, markets, temples, cemeteries, wells, rock inscriptions and evidence of extensive contact with other Arabian and Mediterranean societies. UNESCO describes the site as containing nearly 12,000 archaeological remains and notes that it was abruptly abandoned around the fifth century AD.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1712/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Its abandonment is harder to explain than its prosperity. Trade routes may have shifted, political authority may have weakened, water supplies may have deteriorated or inhabitants may have moved towards more secure centres. Warfare is another possibility, but there is no universally accepted destruction layer or written account establishing a final catastrophe.

Al-Natah and Al-Faw should not be forced into one explanation simply because both were deserted. They flourished in different periods, regions and economic systems. What links them is the difficulty of reconstructing social collapse from ruins alone. Abandonment may be gradual from the inhabitants’ perspective even when it appears abrupt in the archaeological record.

## Were the Wabar craters really a lost city?

In 1932, the explorer St John Philby crossed the Empty Quarter searching for a legendary ruined settlement often associated with the name Ubar. Instead, he encountered areas of black glass, iron fragments and circular depressions at Wabar. The strange landscape encouraged speculation that a city had been destroyed by fire from the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/secrets-wabar-craters" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">secrets wabar craters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">secrets wabar craters</span></span></span>

Scientific investigation established that Wabar is an impact site. A meteorite struck the sand with enough force to create craters, melt silica into glass and produce minerals formed under extreme pressure. The discovery there of coesite, a high-pressure form of silica, provided particularly strong evidence of an extraterrestrial impact. Geophysical surveys have continued to examine features buried beneath the moving dunes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/coesite-wabar-crater-near-al-hadida-arabia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on usgs.gov.</span></span></span>

Dating remained difficult because dunes repeatedly cover and expose the site. A luminescence study estimated the impact at about 290 years before the samples were analysed, with an uncertainty of 38 years, making a relatively recent event plausible. Researchers have compared this result with possible historical reports, but no surviving account can be securely matched to the impact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/luminescence-dating-wabar-meteorite-craters-saudi-arabia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on usgs.gov.</span></span></span>

The physical mystery is therefore mostly solved: Wabar was created by a meteorite, not by the destruction of a technologically advanced lost city. The legendary connection is much weaker. Philby was looking for Ubar when he found the craters, but finding an impact site during a search does not make the site the object of the legend.

The broader tradition of a vanished Arabian settlement may preserve memories of real caravan centres that declined or were abandoned. It may also combine religious storytelling, geographical uncertainty and later romantic literature. Wabar’s melted glass and iron debris made it an ideal setting onto which the story could be projected. The responsible distinction is clear: the craters are documented geology; the identification with a lost city is modern mythmaking.

## Who carved the giant animals in the northern desert?

Recent research in northern Saudi Arabia has added another mystery to the country’s prehistoric record. At sites south of the Nefud Desert, archaeologists documented more than 130 engravings on dozens of rock panels. They include life-sized or larger images of camels, gazelles, ibex, wild donkeys and an aurochs. Dating of associated deposits and tools places the main activity between roughly 12,800 and 11,400 years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/business/environment/ancient-arabian-desert-rock-art-showing-camels-marked-water-sources-2025-09-30/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ancient Arabian desert rock art showing camels marked water sources</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ancient Arabian desert rock art showing camels marked water sources</span></span></span>

The engravings are technically impressive. Some were carved high on cliff faces or from narrow ledges where artists could not easily step back to assess the finished image. A stone tool found in context helped link the artwork to the dated occupation layers, reducing the uncertainty that usually surrounds rock-art chronology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/ab914512f46e80d722537f3d5506a616" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This discovery pushes back the timeline of human habitation in the area by about 2,000 years and raises questions about how ancient commu...</span></span></span>

Researchers have proposed that the panels helped mark seasonal water sources and routes through a landscape becoming habitable after an extremely arid period. Their locations near temporary lakes or water-collecting hollows support that idea. The pictures may have functioned as geographical markers while also expressing shared identity, memory or beliefs about animals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2025/oct/analysis-12000-year-old-rock-art-marked-ancient-water-sources-arabias-desert" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucl.ac.uk">[ucl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">analysis 12000 year old rock art marked ancient water sources arabias desert</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">analysis 12000 year old rock art marked ancient water sources arabias desert</span></span></span>

Yet “road signs to water” should not be treated as a complete translation of the art. No one can know whether every animal carried the same meaning or whether practical information was more important than ceremony and storytelling. The presence of an aurochs—an animal associated with wetter habitats—may reflect environmental memory, travel connections or knowledge passed between distant groups.

The carvings deepen rather than eliminate the central puzzle of early Arabia: how did small communities navigate, exchange knowledge and maintain social connections across a landscape subject to dramatic climatic change? They show that the desert was not an empty barrier. It was a known and interpreted environment, mapped not on paper but through routes, monuments, images and collective memory.

## What the strongest evidence supports

Saudi Arabia’s historical enigmas are gradually becoming more precise. Archaeology has moved several famous examples away from pure speculation:

* Mustatils were ceremonial monuments, although the beliefs behind their offerings remain unknown.
* [Desert kites]({{ 'desert-kites/' | relative_url }}) were mainly hunting installations, not alien runways, city plans or undeciphered writing.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Nabonidus genuinely spent years at Tayma, but no surviving source fully explains his motives.
* Al-Natah and Al-Faw were substantial settlements whose abandonment cannot yet be reduced to a single cause.
* Wabar is a meteorite-impact site, while its association with a lost city belongs to legend rather than demonstrated history.
* Early rock art documents skilled desert communities, but its full social and symbolic meaning is unrecoverable.

</div>

The enduring mystery is not whether ancient Saudi Arabia possessed a hidden civilisation erased from history. The evidence instead reveals many different societies—hunters, herders, oasis farmers, merchants and imperial rulers—whose lives are preserved unevenly in stone, bone, inscriptions and damaged archives. Each new excavation narrows the field of plausible explanations, but the gaps are part of the history itself.
