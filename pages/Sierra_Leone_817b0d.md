## Who made the stone figures found beneath the soil?

Small carved stone figures known most widely as [nomoli]({{ 'nomoli/' | relative_url }}) are among Sierra Leone’s oldest and most enigmatic works of art. Farmers, miners and builders have often found them underground rather than in excavated archaeological sites. Their burial removed them from their original surroundings, making it difficult to determine precisely when they were carved, who commissioned them or how they were first used.

The figures vary considerably. Some portray seated or crouching people with large heads and forceful expressions; others show rulers, attendants, animals or scenes involving several figures. Different communities have used different names for related sculptures, including nomoli in parts of southern Sierra Leone and pomdo farther north and east. Because many discoveries occurred by chance, there is rarely a secure archaeological layer that can be scientifically dated.

Museum and art-historical research nevertheless provides a plausible outline. The figures appear to belong to artistic traditions associated with peoples whom early Portuguese visitors collectively called the Sapi. Their facial proportions, poses and ornament resemble ivory objects carved by West African artists for Portuguese customers around 1490–1530. These parallels suggest that at least part of the stone-carving tradition flourished around the period of early Atlantic contact, perhaps beginning earlier. They do not support internet claims that the figures date from the Ice Age.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.openedition.org/aaa/2753?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.openedition.org">[openedition.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.openedition.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OpenEdition JournalsIvory and Stone: Direct Connections between Sculptural...The carvers of ivory for Portuguese clients were translatin...</span></span></span>

The original purpose is less certain. Frederick Lamp argued that many may have served as memorial images connected with rulers, chiefs or honoured ancestors. Their upright, formal presentation is consistent with authority and remembrance rather than simple decoration. Later communities who uncovered them did not necessarily know the intentions of their makers. Some placed the stones in rice fields or shrines, treating them as spiritually powerful objects capable of protecting crops or encouraging fertility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collegeart.org/pdf/artbulletin/Art%20Bulletin%20Vol%2065%20No%202%20Lamp.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collegeart.org">[collegeart.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collegeart.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Memorial Art of Fifteenth-Century Sierra Leone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 25, 2011 — by FJ Lamp · 1983 · Cited by 31 — The Mende call them nomoli (pl., nomolisia), properly the term for the African bowstring...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2011</span></span></span>

That change of use is central to the mystery. The figures may have passed through several lives:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* first as portraits, memorials or sacred objects;
* then as buried remains of displaced or transformed societies;
* later as objects rediscovered and incorporated into new religious practices;
* finally as museum pieces and internationally traded works of African art.

</div>

No single theory explains every example. The stones were produced across a wide area and probably over an extended period, so different figures may have had different functions.

Modern mythmaking has made the subject harder to understand. Online stories describe metallic spheres inside statues, mysterious blue “sky stones”, reptilian beings and dates as early as 17,000 BC. These claims are usually repeated without documented excavations, laboratory reports or reliable chains of custody. By contrast, the stylistic connection with fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Sapi ivory carving is based on identifiable objects and comparative scholarship. The figures remain genuinely mysterious, but the mystery concerns cultural attribution, chronology and lost meaning—not extraterrestrial intervention.

Their significance has also become entangled with questions of ownership. Many examples entered European and North American collections under colonial conditions or through poorly documented art markets. A nomoli returned from the Netherlands was formally received at the Sierra Leone National Museum in January 2025, turning an archaeological puzzle into a contemporary debate about restitution and cultural memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tourism.gov.sl/nomoli-returns-home-sierra-leone-reclaims-a-sacred-piece-of-its-heritage/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tourism.gov.sl">[Ministry of Tourism and Cultural Affairs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tourism.gov.sl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ministry of Tourism and Cultural AffairsNOMOLI RETURNS HOME: SIERRA LEONE RECLAIMS A...Nomoli artifacts are ancient stone figures origin...</span></span></span>

## How did Sierra Leone remember the wrong face of Bai Bureh?

[Bai Bureh]({{ 'bai-bureh/' | relative_url }}), the Temne leader who directed resistance to British rule during the Hut Tax War of 1898, became one of Sierra Leone’s defining national heroes. Yet for much of the twentieth century, the face associated with him was not based on a verified photograph.

In 1963, the Sierra Leone Museum commissioned a sculpture intended to give the public a recognisable image of the resistance leader. The artist had no clear frontal photograph to work from and relied partly on a contemporary drawing and later interpretation. The result became authoritative through repetition. It appeared in public art, educational material and eventually on the 1,000-leone banknote, even though the sculpture’s facial features and clothing were not historically authentic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sierraleoneheritage.org/item/SLNM.1963.19.56/sculpture-of-bai-bureh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sierraleoneheritage.org">[sierraleoneheritage.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sierraleoneheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sierraleoneheritage.org.</span></span></span>

The mystery changed dramatically in August 2012, when researchers Gary Schulze and William Hart identified an 1898 photograph offered through an online sale. The image, taken after Bai Bureh’s capture, showed him seated and looking directly at the camera. It is believed to be the only surviving photograph of him and was placed on public display in Sierra Leone the following year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sierra-leone.org/Bai_Bureh.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sierra-leone.org">[Sierra Leone Web]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sierra-leone.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sierra-leone.org.</span></span></span>

The discovery did more than correct a portrait. It demonstrated how easily a provisional representation can become accepted historical truth. For half a century, Sierra Leoneans had seen an invented face reproduced by institutions that gave it official weight. Once the photograph appeared, the contrast exposed the chain by which national memory had been constructed.

Questions were later raised in the Sierra Leonean press about ownership, presentation and claims surrounding copies of the photograph. These disputes did not establish that the underlying image was false, but they showed how a major historical discovery can become surrounded by competing claims of custody and credit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thesierraleonetelegraph.com/doubts-widen-over-ownership-and-authenticity-of-the-alleged-original-picture-of-bai-bureh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thesierraleonetelegraph.com">[The Sierra Leone Telegraph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thesierraleonetelegraph.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thesierraleonetelegraph.com.</span></span></span>

The Bai Bureh case is therefore a largely solved identification mystery with an unresolved archival lesson. Other photographs may remain mislabelled in private albums, military collections or colonial records. The sole known image survived not because an official archive had carefully preserved Sierra Leone’s perspective, but because a forgotten colonial-era photograph resurfaced more than a century later.

## What happened to Johnny Paul Koroma?

Johnny Paul Koroma’s disappearance is Sierra Leone’s clearest unresolved political mystery. Koroma was the army officer who led the Armed Forces Revolutionary Council after the 1997 coup. His junta removed President Ahmad Tejan Kabbah and entered an alliance with the Revolutionary United Front during one of the civil war’s most violent phases.

The Special Court for Sierra Leone indicted Koroma in March 2003 for war crimes and crimes against humanity. He disappeared before he could be arrested. Reports soon circulated that he had crossed into Liberia and been killed, possibly by former associates. In June 2003, court investigators acknowledged receiving reports of his death but described them as unconfirmed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://reliefweb.int/report/liberia/sierra-leone-court-investigates-reports-koroma-may-be-dead" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reliefweb.int">[ReliefWeb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reliefweb.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sierra leone court investigates reports koroma may be dead</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sierra leone court investigates reports koroma may be dead</span></span></span>

The absence of a conclusively identified body prevented legal closure. Remains reportedly found in Liberia were tested, but accounts cited by Sierra Leonean media stated that the DNA did not match Koroma. Witness evidence connected to the Charles Taylor trial also complicated claims that Taylor’s circle had arranged his killing. In 2010, judges ordered prosecutors to disclose material that could undermine testimony alleging Koroma had been murdered on Taylor’s instructions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ijmonitor.org/2010/10/judges-order-prosecutors-to-disclose-exculpatory-material-and-payments-suggesting-that-afrc-leader-johnny-paul-koroma-was-not-killed-by-charles-taylor/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ijmonitor.org">[International Justice Monitor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ijmonitor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ijmonitor.org.</span></span></span>

Rumours repeatedly placed Koroma elsewhere. Some claimed he was alive in Liberia or another West African country. Others alleged that he had returned secretly to Sierra Leone and died there years later. None produced public forensic proof.

The Residual Special Court continued to treat him as its outstanding fugitive long after the first reports of his death. Its 2019 annual report said the prosecutor had asked Sierra Leone’s government to confirm or deny a fresh rumour that Koroma had died in the country. Reports in 2020 and 2021 still described the court as retaining authority to prosecute him if he were found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rscsl.org/Documents/AnRpt2019.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rscsl.org">[RSCSL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rscsl.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rscsl.org.</span></span></span>

Several explanations remain possible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **He was killed in Liberia in 2003.** This became the dominant early account, but the reported absence of matching remains leaves it unproved.
* **He survived under protection or an assumed identity.** His military and political networks could theoretically have helped him hide, although no verified sighting has established this.
* **He returned secretly and died later.** Later rumours support this version, but they have not been accompanied by a body, death certificate or independently tested evidence.
* **Different actors promoted conflicting stories deliberately.** Declaring him dead could have protected those sheltering him, discouraged further searches or prevented uncomfortable testimony about wartime alliances.

</div>

The last possibility explains the confusion without proving Koroma survived. He possessed knowledge potentially damaging to former soldiers, politicians and regional powerbrokers. His disappearance prevented both a trial and a public account of the AFRC’s networks. That makes the mystery historically consequential: the unanswered question is not only when Koroma died, but what testimony disappeared with him.

## Was Samuel Hinga Norman’s death suspicious?

Samuel Hinga Norman was another figure whose death generated enduring suspicion. Norman led the Civil Defence Forces, including the Kamajor militias that fought against the Revolutionary United Front and the AFRC. Many Sierra Leoneans regarded him as a defender of elected government, while prosecutors accused forces under his authority of murder, cruel treatment, child recruitment and other serious crimes.

He was detained by the Special Court and transferred to Dakar, Senegal, for medical treatment. After surgery in February 2007, he collapsed and died in hospital before judgement in his trial. The timing immediately encouraged allegations that he had been deliberately silenced. Norman had himself expressed fears of poisoning, and his supporters distrusted a court that they believed was prosecuting a national defender.

Unlike Koroma’s disappearance, however, Norman’s death was subjected to a formal forensic examination. A four-member team conducted the autopsy, with experts representing the Special Court and Sierra Leone’s government. A doctor observed on behalf of Norman’s family. The reported conclusion was that he died from a myocardial infarction—a heart attack—and that there were no signs of violence or poison. A later court review likewise stated that the poisoning allegations were unfounded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rscsl.org/Documents/Press/2007/pressrelease-032807.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rscsl.org">[RSCSL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rscsl.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Autopsy Shows Sam Hinga Norman Died of Natural Causes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Autopsy Shows Sam Hinga Norman Died of Natural Causes</span></span></span>

On the available public evidence, natural death is therefore considerably better supported than assassination. Yet the political mystery did not disappear, because scientific findings and public trust are different matters.

Norman died before completing his defence and before the court could deliver a verdict in his case. Sierra Leone’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission had already criticised restrictions that prevented its planned public engagement with Special Court detainees. After his death, the commission argued that his version of events had gone to the grave with him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sierraleonetrc.org/index.php/resources/the-hinga-norman-saga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sierraleonetrc.org">[sierraleonetrc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sierraleonetrc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sierraleonetrc.org.</span></span></span>

The unresolved issue is consequently less medical than historical. A credible autopsy answered the immediate question of physical cause, but it could not recover Norman’s full testimony about government policy, militia command or wartime decisions. Claims of poisoning remain weak; the loss of evidence created by his death is real.

## Where are the civil war’s unnamed dead?

Sierra Leone’s 1991–2002 civil war left a larger and more painful set of unresolved questions. Families lost relatives to attacks, abductions, forced recruitment, displacement and secret killing. Some never learned whether the missing had died, joined an armed faction, crossed a border or adopted another identity.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission gathered testimony and attempted to produce an impartial historical record. It also sent investigators into most districts to locate mass graves. The commission explicitly warned, however, that this was not an exhaustive national survey. Its purpose was to show the geographical scale of human loss and encourage protection and memorialisation of sites, not to identify every victim through systematic forensic excavation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sierraleonetrc.org/index.php/resources/interactive-map-mass-graves-and-other-sites" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sierraleonetrc.org">[sierraleonetrc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sierraleonetrc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sierraleonetrc.org.</span></span></span>

This limitation matters. A mapped grave may establish that a killing occurred without revealing:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the complete number of bodies;
* the names of those buried;
* the exact date of death;
* which armed group was responsible;
* whether remains were moved after burial;
* whether nearby families are searching for the same people.

</div>

Witness memories were also formed under extreme conditions. Survivors fled, perpetrators concealed actions, records were destroyed and front lines shifted. Testimony could clarify broad patterns while remaining uncertain about individual identities or precise sequences.

These cases should not be treated as an entertaining puzzle. For families, the absence of verified information can prevent mourning, inheritance arrangements and recognition of victimhood. Unmarked burial sites may also be damaged by construction, erosion, farming or mineral extraction before proper investigation becomes possible.

The surviving TRC database, testimonies and mass-grave map provide essential foundations, but they cannot substitute for a fully funded missing-persons process using archival research, family interviews, forensic archaeology and DNA comparison. The mystery is measurable: investigators know the historical record is incomplete because the commission itself stated that its grave survey was partial.

## Why these mysteries still matter

Sierra Leone’s unresolved history reflects repeated breaks in the custody of evidence. Older societies left sculptures without written explanations that survived into the present. Colonial archives preserved the image of a captured resistance leader while Sierra Leone publicly remembered an imagined face. Civil war produced fugitives, anonymous graves and testimony that vanished when key figures died.

The cases also show that mysteries can move towards resolution without disappearing completely. Art historians can narrow the likely age of the nomoli without identifying every carver. A rediscovered photograph can correct Bai Bureh’s face while raising new questions about archival ownership. An autopsy can rebut poisoning allegations while leaving an incomplete political record. A truth commission can map atrocities without naming everyone buried beneath the ground.

The most defensible conclusions are therefore restrained. Sierra Leone’s stone figures are products of regional human artistry, not evidence of ancient aliens. Bai Bureh’s familiar twentieth-century image was a reconstruction later corrected by a credible historical photograph. Samuel Hinga Norman most probably died of natural causes. Johnny Paul Koroma’s fate remains genuinely unresolved in the absence of verified remains or an authoritative account. Thousands of wartime stories remain incomplete because documentation and forensic investigation never matched the scale of the violence.

These mysteries endure because they concern more than curiosity. They shape who is remembered, who receives justice, which institutions are trusted and how Sierra Leone understands the parts of its past that cannot yet be reconstructed with certainty.
