## Introduction

These puzzles matter because they show how Jamaican history has been made as well as remembered. Archaeology can recover a seventeenth-century household from underwater mud, yet may never locate every grave or wreck. Archives can confirm that a rebel existed while revealing how later writers altered his story. A famous ghost tale may prove historically weak but culturally powerful. The mystery often lies not simply in what happened, but in how evidence, power and public memory became entangled.

## How much of Port Royal is still beneath the sea?

[Port Royal]({{ 'port-royal/' | relative_url }}) is Jamaica’s most substantial archaeological mystery because there is no doubt that the catastrophe occurred. On 7 June 1692, an earthquake devastated the port at the entrance to Kingston Harbour. A large part of the town sank or slid into waterlogged ground, while buildings, streets, possessions and human remains were buried beneath sand and harbour sediment. Before the disaster, Port Royal had been one of the most important English commercial centres in the Caribbean. It was a fortified deep-water port connected to Atlantic trade, privateering, imperial warfare and the trafficking of enslaved Africans. Its reputation as a pirate stronghold has frequently overshadowed its importance as a busy colonial town containing homes, workshops, warehouses, taverns, churches and defensive works. The popular image of an intact “sunken city” should nevertheless be treated cautiously. Port Royal did not descend neatly to the seabed like a Caribbean Pompeii. Different parts of the town collapsed, tilted, liquefied or were covered by shifting deposits. Survivors and professional salvagers began retrieving valuables soon after the earthquake, and later divers, treasure hunters and archaeological teams disturbed or investigated additional areas. The Jamaica National Heritage Trust records underwater exploration from at least 1859, when naval diver Jeremiah Murphy identified remains associated with Fort James.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jnht.com/site_port_royal_underwater_archaeology.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jnht.com">[Jamaica National Heritage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jnht.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">site port royal underwater archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jamaica National Heritage TrustPort Royal Underwater ArchaeologyAfter the 1692 earthquake, people tried to salvage anything considered to...</span></span></span>

The genuine mystery is therefore archaeological rather than supernatural: exactly what remains, where are the limits of the lost streets, and how much information survives in undisturbed deposits? Excavations have revealed buildings and everyday objects, but only portions of the drowned settlement have been investigated scientifically. Large areas remain inaccessible, covered or vulnerable to environmental damage.

Port Royal also contains smaller unresolved questions. Sir Henry Morgan, the privateer who became lieutenant-governor of Jamaica, was buried there after his death in 1688. The cemetery containing his grave was among the ground lost in 1692, and his remains have never been securely recovered. Claims about buried pirate treasure are far weaker. Morgan and other privateers certainly brought captured wealth through Jamaica, but the historical and archaeological record does not support the idea of a known, enormous hoard waiting beneath Port Royal.

The site’s international importance was formally recognised when the Archaeological Ensemble of Seventeenth-Century Port Royal entered UNESCO’s World Heritage List. That recognition rests not on pirate legend but on the extraordinary survival of a colonial town divided between land and sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1595/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Today, its terrestrial and underwater remains offer rare insights into urban...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Was the White Witch of Rose Hall a real murderer?

Rose Hall Great House near Montego Bay is associated with Jamaica’s most famous ghost story. In its familiar form, the tale says that Annie Palmer was raised in Haiti, learnt supernatural practices, married the owner of Rose Hall and murdered several husbands and enslaved lovers before being killed herself. Her ghost is then said to have remained in the house.

Rose Hall is a genuine plantation-era building, and a woman named Annie Palmer did live there. The murderous biography attached to her, however, is not supported by reliable contemporary evidence. Archival researcher Geoffrey Yates found that the woman historically connected with Rose Hall was Annie Mary Patterson, who married John Rose Palmer. He argued that neither contemporary records nor plantation documents identify her as a serial killer or as the “White Witch”. That title appears to have been popularised by Herbert G. de Lisser’s 1929 novel *The White Witch of Rosehall*, written many decades after emancipation and about a century after the period in which the story is set.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jamaicanfamilysearch.com/Samples2/mpalmer.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jamaicanfamilysearch.com">[Jamaican Family Search]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jamaicanfamilysearch.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jamaican Family SearchThe Rose Hall Jamaica legendI must also state that neither was Mrs. Palmer ever known to her slaves or contemporari...</span></span></span>

Elements of the legend may have migrated from other places and people. Yates linked part of the tradition to an account concerning a Mrs Palmer at the neighbouring Palmyra estate, where a woman was reportedly found strangled. Over time, a shared surname, local oral traditions, literary invention and the atmosphere of ruined plantation houses may have merged into a single biography.

This does not mean that Rose Hall’s history was benign. Like other Jamaican sugar estates, it belonged to a plantation economy maintained through enslavement and coercion. The danger in concentrating on a fictional white female monster is that the documented violence of the plantation system can be displaced by a more marketable tale of personal wickedness, sexuality and supernatural revenge.

The legend also reflects changing attitudes towards women, racial identity and power. Literary analysis has shown that later versions repeatedly emphasised Annie Palmer’s supposed sexual appetite, magical knowledge and refusal to follow respectable social roles. The “witch” became a useful figure through whom different writers could dramatise fears surrounding colonial authority and uncontrolled female power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/23019871" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

Today the story remains central to Rose Hall’s identity as a tourist attraction; the property itself promotes haunted tours and retells the murders as part of the visitor experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rosehall.com/rose-hall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rosehall.com">[Rose Hall Jamaica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rosehall.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rosehall.com.</span></span></span> That commercial afterlife helps explain why the legend has proved more durable than the archival correction.

The most plausible assessment is straightforward:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* **Well established:** Annie Palmer was a real plantation mistress connected with Rose Hall.
* **Unsupported:** there is no persuasive contemporary evidence that she murdered three husbands or numerous enslaved men.
* **Probable:** the modern legend combined fiction, confused identities and fragments of older plantation stories.
* **Culturally important:** the tale remains one of Jamaica’s best-known examples of history being reshaped into folklore, literature and tourism.

</div>

The mystery is therefore not whether a supernatural serial killer once ruled Rose Hall. It is how a largely literary creation became accepted as historical memory.

## Who was Three-Fingered Jack before the legend took over?

Three-Fingered Jack, often called Jack Mansong in later literature, was not invented from nothing. Evidence in the *Jamaican Royal Gazette* indicates that a fugitive from slavery known by that name operated in eastern Jamaica before being killed in 1781. He appears to have led or belonged to a community of fugitives in the mountainous country of St Thomas-in-the-East, rather than simply wandering alone as a highway robber.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obeahhistories.org/three-fingered-jack/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obeahhistories.org">[Obeah Histories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obeahhistories.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Obeah Histories Three-Fingered Jack</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Obeah Histories Three-Fingered Jack</span></span></span>

Beyond that core, the record becomes difficult. Colonial newspapers and officials wrote about fugitives largely as threats to property and public order. They rarely preserved the fugitives’ own accounts of why they escaped, how their communities were organised or how they understood their struggle. Even the name Jack Mansong may owe more to subsequent novels and stage versions than to surviving contemporary documentation.

After his death, British writers transformed him repeatedly. Benjamin Moseley’s *A Treatise on Sugar*, published in 1799, presented Jack’s supposed use of spiritual power as a means of frightening followers and enemies. William Earle’s novel *Obi; or, the History of Three-Fingered Jack*, published soon afterwards, turned the Jamaican fugitive into a sentimental and theatrical character for British readers. Plays, chapbooks and later performances carried different versions across Britain and North America.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obeahhistories.org/three-fingered-jack/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obeahhistories.org">[obeahhistories.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obeahhistories.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Obeah Histories Three-Fingered Jack</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Obeah Histories Three-Fingered Jack</span></span></span>

These retellings created several competing Jacks. In one, he was a bloodthirsty bandit. In another, he was a rebellious hero resisting enslavement. In another, he was a supposedly superstitious man whose spiritual protections failed when confronted by superior force. Each version served the political and racial assumptions of its storyteller.

Even the account of his death was simplified. The best-known version credits a single Maroon fighter with defeating Jack in hand-to-hand combat. Evidence from colonial records instead suggests that he was killed by a party involving Maroon fighters and colonial leadership. The episode occurred within the difficult political world created by the eighteenth-century treaties between the British authorities and established Maroon communities. Under those agreements, treaty Maroons were expected to help capture people who escaped from plantations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obeahhistories.org/three-fingered-jack/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obeahhistories.org">[Obeah Histories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obeahhistories.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Obeah Histories Three-Fingered Jack</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Obeah Histories Three-Fingered Jack</span></span></span>

That context prevents an easy hero-and-villain reading. Jack’s pursuers were not simply representatives of an undivided colonial society. Maroon communities were defending autonomy won after prolonged warfare, but that autonomy came with obligations that could place them in conflict with later fugitives. Jack’s story therefore exposes a painful divide within Jamaica’s wider history of resistance.

What remains unknown is substantial: his birth name, origins, motives, the size and character of his community, and how much of the behaviour attributed to him actually occurred. Yet the documentary core is firmer than in the Rose Hall story. A real fugitive leader existed; colonial forces feared him; he was killed; and print culture rapidly reconstructed him for audiences far removed from Jamaica.

## What lies beneath the first Spanish capital?

At Seville Heritage Park in St Ann, archaeology has been recovering a landscape occupied and reshaped by Indigenous Jamaicans, Spanish colonists, enslaved Africans and British plantation owners. The Spanish settlement of Sevilla la Nueva was founded near the existing Indigenous village of Maima in the early sixteenth century. It became Spain’s first capital in Jamaica before the seat of government shifted to Villa de la Vega, later Spanish Town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5431/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[unesco.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Unlike Port Royal, the settlement was not lost in a single spectacular catastrophe. It was abandoned, built over, cultivated and gradually obscured. Its exact position was identified in the twentieth century, and formal excavation began in the 1950s. Archaeological work has since uncovered evidence of buildings, religious activity, food production and forced labour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/44/2022/04/JCA-Woodward.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: floridamuseum.ufl.edu">[Florida Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">floridamuseum.ufl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JCA Woodward</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JCA Woodward</span></span></span>

The surviving remains raise difficult questions about whose settlement the site represents. Spanish documents describe colonial construction, administration and agriculture, but archaeology indicates that the colonists established themselves within or beside an existing Indigenous community. The town was therefore not built on an empty coast. It occupied a landscape already inhabited and understood by the island’s Indigenous population.

Researchers have used human, animal and material remains to examine the early colonial economy, including evidence relating to coerced Indigenous labour. Such discoveries complicate older narratives in which the Spanish town appeared mainly as the beginning of “recorded” Jamaican history. Written records began to multiply under European rule, but Jamaican history did not begin with European settlement.

Several points remain debated or incomplete. The exact sequence in which parts of the town were constructed and relocated is not always clear, and official summaries give slightly different dates for its abandonment. Archaeologists must also distinguish material associated with Maima, the Spanish colony and later plantation occupation, all within a site repeatedly reused over centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeology.org/issues/january-february-2018/off-the-grid/trenches-jamaica-seville-heritage-park/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeology.org">[archaeology.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Magazine Off the Grid</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Magazine Off the Grid</span></span></span>

Seville is thus a mystery of historical visibility. Its ruins are not evidence of an inexplicably vanished civilisation. They reveal how one community can disappear from written accounts while another leaves documents that dominate the narrative. The site’s value lies in bringing Indigenous and African lives back into a history once told mainly through governors, landowners and colonial buildings.

## Is the famous portrait really Paul Bogle?

Paul Bogle’s life and death are not mysterious in their broad outlines. Born around 1822, he became a Baptist deacon and political organiser in St Thomas. In October 1865, he led protesters from Stony Gut to Morant Bay amid grievances over poverty, justice, political exclusion and access to land. After the confrontation at the courthouse, colonial forces imposed martial law and carried out a violent campaign of executions, floggings and destruction. Bogle was captured, tried and hanged on 24 October 1865.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jis.gov.jm/information/heroes/paul-bogle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jis.gov.jm">[Jamaica Information Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jis.gov.jm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">paul bogle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">paul bogle</span></span></span>

The uncertainty concerns his face.

A nineteenth-century photograph widely presented as Bogle has become Jamaica’s de facto official image of the national hero. It appears in institutional collections and has influenced public representations, including currency. Yet the National Gallery of Jamaica acknowledges unresolved questions about the photograph’s attribution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nljdigital.nlj.gov.jm/items/show/6195" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nljdigital.nlj.gov.jm">[National Library of Jamaica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nljdigital.nlj.gov.jm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nlj.gov.jm.</span></span></span>

The photograph has also circulated elsewhere as a portrait of Thomas L. Jennings, an African-American inventor and abolitionist. That competing attribution has led to repeated claims that Jamaica has honoured Bogle using the image of another man. The problem is that online repetition is not proof in either direction. Images copied without a clear chain of ownership frequently acquire incorrect identities, and later captions may be mistaken even when they appear authoritative.

Defenders of the Bogle identification point to the reported history of the photograph in Jamaica and argue that the Jennings attribution lacks firm provenance. Critics note that no securely dated, independently verified photograph of Bogle has emerged and that the accepted image entered public use many decades after his death. Jamaican scholars and cultural institutions have therefore treated the matter as unresolved rather than decisively settled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nationalgalleryofjamaica.wordpress.com/tag/paul-bogle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgalleryofjamaica.wordpress.com">[wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgalleryofjamaica.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Gallery of Jamaica Paul Bogle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Gallery of Jamaica Paul Bogle</span></span></span>

The dispute matters because portraits shape public memory. Bogle’s face appears not merely in history books but in national ceremonies, monuments and money. When a photograph is repeated often enough, familiarity can become mistaken for authentication.

Edna Manley’s 1965 monument to Bogle offers a different approach. It does not pretend to be a photographic likeness. It represents his courage, leadership and resistance through posture and symbolic form. The contrast between the sculpture and the contested photograph reveals two ways of remembering a historical figure: reconstructing an uncertain physical appearance or expressing the meaning later generations attach to the person.

Until an original, traceable photograph or stronger documentary evidence appears, the most responsible wording is that the familiar portrait is **traditionally identified as Paul Bogle**, not that its identity has been proved beyond doubt.

## What these mysteries reveal about Jamaica’s past

Jamaica’s strongest historical mysteries are rarely empty puzzles waiting for an ingenious final solution. They are disputes created by uneven survival.

At Port Royal, mud and seawater preserved parts of a town while concealing others. At Rose Hall, a novel and tourist tradition became more influential than the archives. Three-Fingered Jack entered the record through the words of the society pursuing him, then became a character rewritten for foreign stages. At Seville, archaeology has had to recover Indigenous and African lives from beneath colonial buildings and documentary silences. In Paul Bogle’s case, the nation possesses a powerful public image without an uncontested chain of identification.

These cases also demand different levels of confidence. Port Royal’s destruction is established fact, although much of its archaeology remains unexplored. Three-Fingered Jack was real, but many details of his biography are literary invention. The White Witch’s murders lack credible contemporary support. Seville’s buried landscape is gradually becoming clearer through excavation. Bogle’s accepted portrait remains plausible to some researchers and doubtful to others.

The common lesson is that uncertainty should not be confused with permission to invent. Jamaica’s past is already dramatic without unsupported treasure maps, supernatural confessions or grand conspiracies. Its genuine enigmas arise from earthquakes, enslavement, rebellion, colonial archives, archaeological loss and the continuing struggle over who is remembered, by what evidence and in whose words.
