## The queen removed from Dahomey’s royal story

One of Benin’s most intriguing historical disputes concerns Hangbe, a woman said to have governed Dahomey during an early eighteenth-century succession crisis. She appears in several oral traditions but not in the conventional sequence of kings commemorated at Abomey. The central question is therefore not simply whether Hangbe existed, but whether she actually ruled and was later deliberately excluded from the dynasty’s authorised history.

According to the best-known version, Hangbe was the twin sister of King Akaba. When Akaba died, probably around 1716, his heir was considered too young to succeed. Hangbe then governed temporarily, perhaps as regent and perhaps as sovereign in her own right, before their younger brother Agaja secured the throne. Accounts disagree over whether her authority lasted a few months or several years, what role she played in military campaigns and what happened during the resulting struggle over succession.

The lack of a contemporary written record makes certainty impossible. Much of Dahomey’s early history was transmitted through court ceremonies, family traditions, royal art and accounts collected later by visitors or historians. Those traditions were not neutral archives: they helped legitimise whichever branch of the dynasty held power. Research into Dahomey’s foundation stories has shown that official narratives could minimise the political importance of royal mothers, wives and other powerful women, making Hangbe’s omission plausible as an act of dynastic memory rather than proof that she never governed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/path-of-the-leopard-motherhood-and-majesty-in-early-danhome/77D6D898B8B1DF87E21AB4AF6B740116" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Path of the Leopard: Motherhood and Majesty in Early...by SP Blier · 1995 · Cited by 29 — Thi...</span></span></span>

Hangbe’s descendants, or people recognised as belonging to her lineage, have maintained a presence near the royal palaces at Abomey. Their traditions preserve both her claim to authority and the belief that her memory was suppressed after Agaja’s victory. Such continuity strengthens the case that she was a significant historical figure, but lineage tradition alone cannot settle whether her position met the later definition of a reigning monarch.

She has also been credited with creating Dahomey’s famous women’s military corps. That claim is even harder to prove. The first firm written references to organised women fighters date from the 1720s, after Hangbe’s supposed rule. Other accounts trace the corps to women elephant hunters or to palace guards developed under male kings. The most defensible conclusion is that Dahomey’s women warriors emerged gradually from several institutions rather than being invented at one identifiable moment by one person.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/the-true-story-of-the-women-warriors-of-dahomey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the true story of the women warriors of dahomey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicThe true story of the women warriors of Dahomey14 Sept 2022 — But the prevailing origin story of the Dahomey women war...</span></span></span>

Hangbe’s story remains powerful because it exposes a basic problem in historical reconstruction: absence from an official list can mean non-existence, but it can also mean defeat. Her reign is not established beyond doubt. Nor is it sensible to dismiss her as legend merely because the victorious dynasty did not commemorate her alongside its kings.

## Why were chambers dug beneath Abomey?

In February 1998, road construction near Bohicon uncovered an extensive group of underground structures at Agongointo-Zoungoudo, roughly nine kilometres from Abomey. The discovery brought renewed attention to similar subterranean chambers scattered across the wider Abomey plateau. Cut into iron-rich earth, they include shafts, rooms and geometrically shaped spaces several metres below ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/988/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreVillage souterrain d&#x27;Agongointo-ZoungoudoLors de la construction d&#x27;une rocade, la société danoise DANISA a dé...</span></span></span>

They are commonly described as an “underground village”, but that phrase may create the wrong impression. The chambers were not necessarily a single permanently inhabited town, nor is there evidence that an entire population lived continuously below ground. The real mystery is functional: were they military refuges, storage facilities, mines, water systems, ritual spaces or some combination of these?

An early interpretation treated them primarily as defensive shelters. Oral accounts described Dahomean fighters disappearing when enemies approached and later emerging in unexpected positions. Concealed chambers would have offered protection during raids and may have allowed soldiers, provisions or vulnerable residents to remain hidden. Their location near the political heart of Dahomey and their probable seventeenth-century date make a connection with the kingdom’s consolidation reasonable.

Yet the structures vary too much to support a single explanation. Some appear suitable for concealment or temporary occupation. Others may have been created while extracting mineral-rich earth, then adapted for storage. Certain chambers could have helped preserve water or food, while narrow entrances may have provided security without implying a complex network of military tunnels. Later archaeological interpretations have therefore moved towards multiple uses rather than one grand design.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agongointo-Zoungoudo_Underground_Town" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Agongointo-Zoungoudo Underground Town</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Agongointo-Zoungoudo Underground Town</span></span></span>

There are also limits to what the discovery can reveal. Organic remains deteriorate quickly in tropical conditions, and chambers reused over generations do not preserve a simple snapshot of their original purpose. Modern labels such as “bunker”, “house” or “mine” may impose categories that did not exist for their builders.

Agongointo is mysterious in a productive sense. The structures are real, extensive and clearly deliberate; there is no need to embellish them as a vanished subterranean civilisation. Their importance lies in showing that Dahomey’s landscape contained hidden infrastructure whose functions changed according to war, resources and local need. More systematic dating, excavation and comparison between sites could clarify how many were built at the same time and whether particular chamber types served distinct purposes.

## The royal treasures that vanished into European collections

When French forces captured Abomey in 1892, King Béhanzin ordered parts of the royal city destroyed rather than surrender them intact. Soldiers entering the capital removed thrones, statues, doors, ceremonial objects and royal emblems. Some were formally assigned to French museums; others passed through military families, private collections, dealers and institutions elsewhere in Europe.

France returned 26 royal objects to Benin in November 2021. Their restitution was historically important, but it also revealed how incomplete the surviving inventory remained. The returned group represented only a small portion of the material removed during conquest and colonial rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcnews.com/International/france-returning-26-looted-treasures-back-benin/story?id=80999015" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcnews.com">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News France returning 26 looted treasures back to Benin</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsFrance returning 26 looted treasures back to BeninNovember 8, 2021 — 8 Nov 2021 — The pieces were looted following the war fought...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 8, 2021</span></span></span>

A revealing example is the three-legged royal stool associated with Béhanzin. It had once belonged to the same museum grouping as the 26 restituted works but was not among them. Research later established that it had been transferred from France to Finland in 1939 and had remained in the Finnish national collection. Finland formally returned it to Benin in May 2025, more than 130 years after the capture of Abomey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/afrique/article/2025/05/13/la-finlande-restitue-officiellement-le-27e-tresor-royal-d-abomey-au-benin_6605828_3212.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lemonde.fr.</span></span></span>

This was not the discovery of a fabulous treasure in a secret chamber. It was something more typical of colonial collecting: an object had become separated from its history through transfers, incomplete cataloguing and changing museum systems. Its location was recoverable only by reconstructing institutional records across several countries.

Other cases remain disputed because their documentation begins with the person who acquired the object during the invasion. In 2024, for example, Benin successfully sought the suspension of a Paris auction involving a sceptre attributed to Béhanzin. The seller’s history reportedly treated it as a legitimately acquired family possession, while Beninese representatives argued that an object taken in the setting of military conquest should not be described as a voluntary gift without persuasive evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/afrique/article/2024/12/25/le-benin-reussit-a-suspendre-la-vente-parisienne-d-un-sceptre-du-roi-behanzin_6466558_3212.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[Le Monde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le sceptre aurait été acquis par Emmeran de Curzon durant la conquête coloniale française, et hérité par ses descendants. These disputes turn on provenance: the documented history of an object’s ownership and movement. A museum number or inheritance record may prove where an artefact went after entering Europe, but not whether its original removal was consensual. Colonial officers often described seized, purchased and presented objects using language that concealed the unequal conditions under which they changed hands.

The surviving [royal treasures]({{ 'royal-treasures/' | relative_url }}) are also evidence, not merely decoration. Sculptures and palace reliefs recorded dynastic claims, military victories, ritual relationships and royal identities. Their dispersal broke apart an interconnected historical record. Returning individual works can restore physical possession, but it cannot automatically reconstruct the palace settings, ceremonies and knowledge systems from which they were removed.

## Is Ouidah’s Slave Route historically exact?

Ouidah was one of the principal Atlantic slaving ports on the coast of present-day Benin. Captives brought from inland regions were confined, sold and transported towards the shore, where smaller vessels carried them to ships anchored offshore. The city is now a major site of remembrance, centred on a route leading from the former commercial quarter towards the Door of No Return memorial on the beach.

The broad history is beyond dispute: enormous numbers of enslaved people were exported through the region, and Ouidah was deeply connected to Atlantic markets. What historians question is whether today’s marked route reproduces a single fixed path regularly followed by captives over several centuries.

The present commemorative landscape was substantially created in the 1990s, when Benin, international organisations and cultural figures developed monuments associated with the slave trade and African diaspora. The Door of No Return is therefore a modern memorial, not a surviving gateway through which every captive literally passed. Research on the politics of slavery remembrance in Benin has cautioned that parts of the route combine historical locations, oral traditions, ritual symbolism and modern heritage planning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://medium.com/from-traditional-to-contemporary-aesthetic/the-slaving-port-of-ouidah-and-monumental-discourse-around-the-atlantic-perimeter-a41968341a57" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medium.com">[Medium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medium.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Slaving Port of Ouidah and Monumental Discourse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Slaving Port of Ouidah and Monumental Discourse</span></span></span>

That does not make the route false. Ports change, shorelines shift, paths vary and traumatic journeys are rarely recorded from the viewpoint of their victims. Captives could have been held in different compounds, taken along different tracks or embarked from changing points on the coast. A commemorative route can represent this shared experience without being a precise archaeological reconstruction.

Problems arise when symbolic features are presented as if their stories were documented for every period. Particular trees, stopping points and rituals may preserve genuine local memory, but some descriptions have been standardised for visitors long after the slave trade ended. The careful distinction is between a historically grounded landscape of remembrance and a literal itinerary supported at every stage by contemporary records.

Ouidah’s route matters precisely because archival precision is limited. European shipping documents recorded cargoes, vessels, merchants and numbers more readily than the identities and experiences of those forced aboard. The memorial landscape attempts to give physical form to people whom the commercial archive reduced to entries in ledgers. Its uncertainty should encourage better interpretation, not dismissal.

The final voyage of the schooner *Clotilda* illustrates both the strength and limits of connection. In 1860, decades after the United States had outlawed the international slave trade, the ship carried more than 100 captive Africans from Ouidah to Alabama. The vessel was burned and sunk to hide the crime, while survivors later founded Africatown. The wreck’s identification and the descendants’ preserved memories have created an unusually traceable link between the Bight of Benin and a specific diaspora community. Even here, however, reconstructing the exact experiences and origins of every captive remains difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2025/03/03/dredging-up-the-ghostly-secrets-of-slave-ships" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker Dredging Up the Ghostly Secrets of Slave Ships</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker Dredging Up the Ghostly Secrets of Slave Ships</span></span></span>

## The “Benin Bronzes” are not from Benin

One persistent source of confusion deserves a clear correction. The famous Benin Bronzes looted by British forces in 1897 came from the Kingdom of Benin, whose capital was Benin City in present-day Nigeria. They did not come from the modern Republic of Benin or from the Kingdom of Dahomey.

The shared name is the result of separate historical developments. The Republic of Dahomey adopted the name Benin in 1975, drawing on the regional name associated with the Bight of Benin. The older Kingdom of Benin lay farther east and had its own royal dynasty, artistic traditions and political history.

Museum restitution stories can therefore appear misleading when headlines say that “Benin objects” are being returned. The 26 treasures returned by France in 2021 went to the Republic of Benin because they were removed from Abomey by French forces in 1892. The Benin Bronzes seized by Britain in 1897 are being returned to Nigeria and the royal institutions of Benin City. German museum agreements and other restitution documents explicitly identify Nigeria as the receiving country for those works.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smb.museum/en/whats-new/detail/return-of-benin-bronzes-from-the-ethnologisches-museum-artefacts-now-in-nigerian-hands/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smb.museum">[smb.museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smb.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smb.museum.</span></span></span>

Confusing the two does more than muddle geography. It merges two distinct conquests, two sets of looted collections and two modern debates over ownership. Benin’s unresolved heritage story concerns the dispersed royal culture of Dahomey and other communities within the present republic, not the Nigerian bronzes that happen to bear the same name.

## What the strongest evidence supports

Benin’s major historical enigmas are best understood as questions of incomplete transmission rather than wholly unknowable events.

**Hangbe probably belonged to the highest level of Dahomey’s royal politics**, and she may have governed during the crisis following Akaba’s death. Her precise status, dates and military role remain disputed because later dynastic tradition had reasons to minimise her authority.

**The chambers beneath the Abomey plateau were probably multifunctional.** Defence and concealment are credible uses, but archaeological variation makes it unlikely that every underground structure was built as part of one military system.

**Many royal objects removed in 1892 can still be traced**, but provenance research is unfinished. Objects may remain misidentified in museum stores or private collections, while ambiguous acquisition records continue to obscure whether they were seized, bought or supposedly presented.

**Ouidah’s Slave Route is an authentic place of remembrance rather than a perfectly documented itinerary.** Its monuments express a real history of forced departure, although the present route combines historical geography with oral memory and late twentieth-century commemoration.

These cases endure because each involves power over the historical record. Victorious rulers determined royal genealogies. Colonial armies dispersed objects and documents. Merchants recorded transactions while neglecting the lives behind them. Modern governments and heritage institutions then turned fragments into public narratives. Benin’s mysteries are therefore not empty spaces waiting for dramatic theories. They are contests over whose memories survived, whose evidence was preserved and how responsibly the surviving pieces can now be put together.
