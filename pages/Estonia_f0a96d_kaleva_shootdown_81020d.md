## Introduction

On 14 June 1940, a Finnish civilian airliner named *Kaleva* took off from Tallinn for Helsinki. Roughly ten minutes later, two Soviet naval bombers opened fire and shot it down over the Gulf of Finland, killing all nine people aboard. The attackers are known, the timing is known, and the political context is well documented. What remains uncertain is the crucial question: **why was the aircraft deliberately destroyed?** More than eight decades later, no surviving Soviet order has been found that explains the decision. The mystery matters because the attack took place only three days before the Soviet occupation of [Estonia]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-estonias/' | relative_url }}). It was the last scheduled flight to leave Tallinn before Soviet control closed the country's connection with the outside world. Understanding why *Kaleva* was attacked therefore sheds light not only on a wartime tragedy but also on the methods used as the Baltic states lost their independence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8376e3aa3237ec2e551749afc74ccbbe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antheil Jr. The incident has long intrigued historians, as it occurred just days before the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states. ## The final flight from Tallinn

The aircraft was a Finnish Aero Junkers Ju 52 operating its regular scheduled service from Tallinn to Helsinki. On board were two Finnish crew members and seven passengers from several countries, including American diplomat Henry W. Antheil Jr., two French diplomatic couriers, two German businessmen, a Swedish passenger and an Estonian-Finnish traveller. The flight itself was routine; what made it exceptional was the rapidly deteriorating political situation surrounding Estonia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8376e3aa3237ec2e551749afc74ccbbe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antheil Jr. The incident has long intrigued historians, as it occurred just days before the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states. Recen...</span></span></span>

By 14 June 1940, Soviet forces had already surrounded Estonia by sea and air while preparing to occupy the country. Although the formal occupation began on 17 June, military pressure was already intense. *Kaleva* became the final civilian aircraft to depart Tallinn before Soviet control effectively sealed the country's air links.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8376e3aa3237ec2e551749afc74ccbbe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antheil Jr. The incident has long intrigued historians, as it occurred just days before the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states. Recen...</span></span></span>

Witnesses and later investigations agree that the airliner was intercepted shortly after take-off by two Soviet Ilyushin DB-3 bombers belonging to Soviet Naval Aviation. The bombers approached closely enough to identify the aircraft before opening fire with machine guns, causing it to crash into the sea near Keri island. There is no serious historical dispute that the aircraft was intentionally attacked rather than accidentally destroyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaleva_%28airplane%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kaleva (airplane</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kaleva (airplane</span></span></span>

## Why would the Soviet Union attack a civilian aircraft?

The enduring mystery is not who carried out the attack but what objective Soviet commanders hoped to achieve. Several explanations have been proposed, with varying degrees of supporting evidence.

The strongest possibility is that *Kaleva* was viewed as part of a wider effort to isolate Estonia before occupation. Soviet naval and air forces were enforcing an undeclared blockade around the country. Preventing aircraft from leaving Tallinn would have fitted that broader strategy by limiting the escape of diplomats, intelligence and information. The timing of the attack, immediately before the occupation, strongly supports this interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8376e3aa3237ec2e551749afc74ccbbe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antheil Jr. The incident has long intrigued historians, as it occurred just days before the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states. Recen...</span></span></span>

A second and widely discussed explanation focuses on intelligence. The aircraft carried approximately 227 kilograms of diplomatic mail. Henry W. Antheil Jr. had been evacuating sensitive American diplomatic material from the United States legations in Tallinn and Riga, while the French couriers were also transporting official diplomatic bags. If Soviet intelligence knew that confidential documents were leaving Estonia, intercepting the aircraft would have offered an opportunity to seize valuable information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8376e3aa3237ec2e551749afc74ccbbe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antheil Jr. The incident has long intrigued historians, as it occurred just days before the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states. Recen...</span></span></span>

A third possibility is that local commanders interpreted broad blockade instructions aggressively without receiving explicit orders to destroy this particular aircraft. Soviet operational records that have surfaced identify the patrol aircraft involved but do not include a surviving command explaining why the passenger aircraft was fired upon. That absence leaves room for the possibility of initiative by the aircrews or intermediate commanders, although there is no direct evidence proving such a scenario.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaleva_%28airplane%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kaleva (airplane</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kaleva (airplane</span></span></span>

## Blockade orders and diplomatic mail

The diplomatic cargo has attracted particular attention because several eyewitness accounts describe events after the crash that appear difficult to explain unless Soviet authorities considered the mail important.

Local Estonian fishermen reportedly reached floating wreckage before a Soviet submarine surfaced nearby. According to later witness accounts, Soviet personnel collected diplomatic pouches and other debris recovered from the sea. These accounts have been repeated in historical research and have become central to the intelligence theory. If accurate, they suggest that recovering documents formed part of the operation after the aircraft had been destroyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8376e3aa3237ec2e551749afc74ccbbe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[apnews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antheil Jr. The incident has long intrigued historians, as it occurred just days before the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states. Recen...</span></span></span>

However, an important distinction must be maintained. Recovering diplomatic bags after the crash does **not** prove that the aircraft was shot down specifically to obtain them. Historians have not found an operational order stating that Soviet intelligence targeted *Kaleva* because of particular documents or named passengers. The recovery of mail demonstrates Soviet interest in the cargo but does not reveal the original decision-making process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8376e3aa3237ec2e551749afc74ccbbe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antheil Jr. The incident has long intrigued historians, as it occurred just days before the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states. ## What remains uncertain?

Several key questions remain unanswered because relevant Soviet archival material has either not survived, has never been released or has not yet been identified.

Among the unresolved issues are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Who authorised the interception and destruction of the aircraft?
* Did Soviet intelligence know in advance about the diplomatic passengers and cargo?
* Were the bomber crews specifically instructed to destroy the aircraft rather than force it to land?
* Was the attack directed from Moscow, by Baltic Fleet commanders or by lower-level officers implementing blockade instructions?

</div>

Historians generally agree that the Soviet Union bears responsibility for the attack itself. The debate concerns the chain of command and the precise motive rather than the identity of the perpetrators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaleva_%28airplane%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kaleva (airplane</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kaleva (airplane</span></span></span>

## What the newly found wreck may reveal

For decades, another mystery complicated the investigation: the location of the aircraft itself. Numerous searches failed to locate the wreck, leading to speculation that it might have been dispersed beyond recovery or even salvaged during the Soviet period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airlinerdown.com/post/how-the-kaleva-was-found-84-years-after-russian-bombers-shot-it-down" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airlinerdown.com">[Airlinerdown]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airlinerdown.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How the Kaleva was found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How the Kaleva was found - 84 years after Russian bombers shot it down...</span></span></span>

In 2024, an Estonian diving team announced that it had located substantial remains of a Junkers Ju 52 at a depth of around 70 metres near Keri island. Visible landing gear, engines and wing structures closely matched *Kaleva*, convincing investigators that the aircraft had finally been found. The discovery resolved the geographical mystery surrounding the crash site after eighty-four years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8376e3aa3237ec2e551749afc74ccbbe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antheil Jr. The incident has long intrigued historians, as it occurred just days before the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states. Recen...</span></span></span>

The wreck could still answer practical questions. Detailed mapping may improve understanding of how the aircraft broke apart, while any surviving cargo or personal effects could clarify what remained on board after impact. Yet expectations should remain realistic. Unless written Soviet operational records emerge alongside archaeological evidence, the wreck itself is unlikely to reveal who ordered the attack or exactly why it occurred. ## Why the mystery still matters

The destruction of *Kaleva* occupies a unique place in Estonian history because it sits at the intersection of aviation, diplomacy, intelligence and the Soviet occupation of the Baltic states. Unlike many historical mysteries, the central facts are not disputed: a civilian aircraft was deliberately shot down during peacetime, and everyone aboard was killed. What remains hidden is the reasoning behind that decision.

The most convincing interpretation is that the attack formed part of the Soviet effort to isolate Estonia while preventing sensitive diplomatic material from leaving the country. Yet the absence of a definitive written order means this remains an inference rather than a proven conclusion. Until new archival evidence appears, *Kaleva* will continue to stand as one of Estonia's most significant unresolved wartime mysteries: a case in which the perpetrators are known, but their exact purpose remains concealed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8376e3aa3237ec2e551749afc74ccbbe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antheil Jr. The incident has long intrigued historians, as it occurred just days before the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states.
