## Introduction

The question of whether the so-called **Harper Seven** were truly proven guilty remains one of [Liberia]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-liberias/' | relative_url }})'s most debated criminal justice controversies. Seven people were publicly hanged in Harper, Maryland County, on 16 February 1979 after being convicted of murdering the popular fisherman and singer Moses Tweh in what prosecutors described as a ritual killing. Their convictions appeared to demonstrate that even politically connected figures could be punished. Yet almost immediately, doubts emerged over how the investigation had been conducted, whether confessions and witness testimony had been obtained under torture or coercion, and whether political pressure to reassure a frightened public affected the fairness of the trial. These unresolved questions have kept the case alive in Liberia's historical memory long after the executions themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

## The Murder of Moses Tweh

By the mid-1970s, Maryland County had become gripped by fear over a series of disappearances and mutilation murders widely believed to be ritual killings. Reports of missing people had accumulated over several years, while local authorities were increasingly criticised for failing to investigate effectively. Public confidence collapsed when Moses Tweh, a well-known local fisherman and musician, disappeared in July 1977. His mutilated body was later discovered, provoking national outrage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

The government of President William Tolbert responded with an unusually forceful investigation. A number of influential figures—including senior county officials and politicians—were arrested. The willingness to prosecute members of Liberia's long-dominant political establishment made the case exceptional and attracted attention far beyond Maryland County.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

Although the existence of Moses Tweh's murder is not disputed, historians distinguish between two separate questions:

* **Was Tweh murdered?** The evidence overwhelmingly indicates yes.
* **Were all those eventually executed responsible beyond reasonable doubt?** This is where the controversy begins.

## The Trial, Witnesses and Executions

The prosecution initially charged more than ten defendants. During the proceedings, two accused men were released and became prosecution witnesses, while ten others were convicted. Before the executions took place, one convicted defendant received a presidential pardon and two others died, leaving seven prisoners to face execution. These seven became known collectively as the **Harper Seven**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

The executions were carried out publicly by hanging at dawn on 16 February 1979. President Tolbert allowed the sentences to proceed despite the political prominence of several defendants and despite criticism from some supporters of his own ruling True Whig Party. Contemporary observers saw the decision as an attempt to demonstrate that the government was prepared to confront ritual killings regardless of social status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

At trial, the prosecution relied heavily on testimony from former co-defendants who had become state witnesses, together with confessions and other evidence said to connect the accused to Tweh's killing. Liberia's Supreme Court upheld the convictions before the executions proceeded, meaning the sentences were not carried out solely on the basis of a local court verdict. Later Liberian case law continued to cite aspects of the Harper proceedings when discussing criminal evidence and witness testimony, showing that the case retained legal significance beyond its immediate political impact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

## Why the Verdicts Remain Contested

The central historical mystery is not whether ritual killings occurred in Maryland County—they clearly did—but whether the Harper Seven received a process that would satisfy modern standards of criminal justice.

Several factors continue to trouble historians and legal commentators.

**Allegations of torture.** Numerous later accounts report that suspects claimed they had been beaten or tortured during interrogation. If true, this raises obvious concerns about the reliability of confessions and statements obtained before trial. While such allegations have been widely repeated, surviving public records do not always establish exactly which defendants were mistreated or how individual claims were investigated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

**Pressure on witnesses.** The prosecution's decision to release some accused individuals and use them as state witnesses has also attracted scrutiny. Such arrangements are common in criminal prosecutions, but critics argue that they may create incentives for testimony favourable to the prosecution, especially in a highly charged political atmosphere. The available public record does not conclusively resolve how much independent corroboration existed for every important allegation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

**Political urgency.** By 1977 the government faced mounting criticism for failing to stop ritual killings. Public fear was intense, and Maryland County had become associated with official incompetence and rumours of elite involvement. Bringing influential suspects to trial helped demonstrate decisive government action, but it also created pressure for visible results. Historians therefore continue to debate whether the investigation was shaped partly by the need to restore public confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

**Incomplete historical records.** Much of what is known today comes from contemporary newspaper reports, official government releases and later historical reconstructions rather than complete surviving court files. Liberia's later civil wars further complicated preservation of legal and administrative records, making independent reassessment difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://liberiapastandpresent.org/MarylandRitualMurders11.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: liberiapastandpresent.org">[Liberia Past and Present]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">liberiapastandpresent.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Liberia Past and Present</span></span></span>

## What Can Be Said with Confidence?

The Harper Seven case illustrates the difference between a legally confirmed conviction and a historically uncontested one.

Several points are well established:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Moses Tweh was murdered during a period of widespread ritual killings in Maryland County.
* Senior local political figures were arrested and prosecuted.
* Liberia's courts convicted several defendants, and the Supreme Court affirmed the convictions.
* Seven convicted prisoners were publicly executed in February 1979.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

</div>

Other questions remain unresolved:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Whether every confession was voluntary.
* Whether torture influenced the investigation.
* Whether witness testimony was entirely reliable.
* Whether political pressure affected the conduct of the prosecution or trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>

</div>

No credible evidence has emerged proving that the Harper Seven were collectively innocent, but neither has the historical record eliminated every reasonable concern about the fairness of the proceedings. The uncertainty lies less in whether a terrible crime occurred than in whether the criminal justice process reached the truth in every respect.

## Why the Case Still Matters

The Harper Seven remain central to Liberia's history because the trial sits at the intersection of crime, politics and public fear. It demonstrated an unprecedented willingness to prosecute politically connected individuals while simultaneously exposing concerns about coercive policing and the limits of judicial independence during a national crisis.

For historians, the case serves as a caution against treating either extreme as settled fact. It is not simply a story of obvious guilt, nor is there convincing evidence that the convictions were entirely fabricated. Instead, it remains a rare example of a case in which genuine criminal violence, credible allegations of investigative abuse and intense political pressure all coexisted. That combination explains why, decades after the executions, the Harper Seven continue to occupy a distinctive place among Liberia's unresolved historical controversies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_ritual_killings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maryland ritual killings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maryland ritual killings</span></span></span>
