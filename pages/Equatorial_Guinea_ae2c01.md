## Introduction

The deaths surrounding the political crisis of [March 1969]({{ 'march-1969/' | relative_url }}) remain clouded by incompatible accounts. ## The disappearance of Acacio Mañé

Acacio Mañé was an influential anti-colonial organiser in mainland Spanish Guinea during the final decade of Spanish rule. He helped mobilise support for independence while the government of Francisco Franco was trying to contain African nationalism and present the territory as an overseas extension of Spain rather than a colony. His disappearance in 1959 has become the country’s clearest unresolved political killing from the colonial period.

The broad outline is well established. Spanish authorities had detained and questioned Mañé over his political activity. In November 1959, after another encounter with the authorities in Bata, he vanished. His body was never found, no credible public investigation established what happened, and his family lost both a relative and much of the security attached to his position in colonial society. Later accounts alleged that Spanish personnel abducted him, killed him and disposed of his body at sea, but the precise sequence of events and identities of those responsible have never been proved in court.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cidafucm.es/acacio-mane-ela-un-heroe-de-la-independencia-de-guinea-ecuatorial/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cidafucm.es">[CIDAF-UCM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cidafucm.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CIDAF-UCMAcacio Mañe Elá, un héroe de la independencia de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CIDAF-UCMAcacio Mañe Elá, un héroe de la independencia de</span></span></span>

Recent research has narrowed the mystery without closing it. The documentary *Negro limbo*, based on years of investigation, traced Mañé’s last known movements to detention and transfer by colonial forces. Its reconstruction indicates that he was taken aboard a vessel bound towards Bioko but never arrived there. The same investigation highlighted the continuing difficulty of accessing Spanish state records concerning late colonial rule and decolonisation. In 2024, Spanish reporting described Mañé’s official recognition as a victim of Francoist political and colonial repression, an important acknowledgement of state responsibility in broad terms but not a judicial finding that identifies individual perpetrators or recovers his remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elpais.com/cultura/2024-11-15/negro-limbo-cuando-el-estado-franquista-asesino-al-lider-independentista-de-guinea-ecuatorial.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elpais.com">[El País]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elpais.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on elpais.com.</span></span></span>

The most plausible explanation is therefore not a voluntary disappearance or an ordinary crime. The evidence points strongly towards an enforced disappearance carried out by, or with the knowledge of, elements of the colonial state. What remains unresolved is the chain of command: who ordered it, who physically carried it out, where Mañé was killed and where his body was left.

That distinction matters. Calling the case “solved” because colonial responsibility appears likely would erase the unanswered questions that concern the family and the historical record. Calling it completely mysterious would be equally misleading because it would ignore the political context, witness accounts and official recognition now available. It is best understood as a probable state killing whose operational details remain concealed.

## The deaths behind the crisis of March 1969

Only five months after independence, Equatorial Guinea entered a political crisis that helped transform President Francisco Macías Nguema’s government into a dictatorship. On the night of 4–5 March 1969, Foreign Minister Atanasio Ndongo Miyone and associates were accused of attempting to overthrow Macías in Bata. There was a real confrontation at the presidential palace, arrests followed, and Ndongo suffered catastrophic injuries after falling from an upper window. Beyond those points, the story becomes contested.

One version holds that Ndongo led a genuine but badly organised coup. According to this account, his supporters occupied parts of the palace and detained officials, but failed to secure sufficient military backing. Macías rallied loyal forces, retook control and confronted Ndongo, who either jumped while trying to escape or was thrown from the window. Contemporary foreign reporting accepted that some kind of coup attempt occurred, although even early accounts depended heavily on information controlled by Macías’s government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/archive/6633988/equatorial-guinea-fangs-a-lot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Equatorial Guinea: Fangs a LotNdongo tried to mount a golpe (coup) against Macias, The coup, without a leg to stand-on, collapsed. Ib...</span></span></span>

Competing accounts differ over almost every decisive moment: whether Macías was already aware of the plot, who warned him, whether Ndongo jumped or was pushed, and whether the entire event was encouraged or manipulated to justify a purge. Ndongo survived the fall with severe injuries but was allegedly abused before being taken into custody. He died later that month. No transparent post-mortem or independent judicial inquiry established the immediate cause of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1969_Equatorial_Guinea_coup_attempt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1969 Equatorial Guinea coup attempt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">June 5, 2026 — The leaders of the coup attempt were reportedly Foreign Minister Atanasio Ndongo Miyone. The crisis also enveloped other political figures. Bonifacio Ondó Edú, the former head of the autonomous colonial government and Macías’s principal rival in the 1968 election, had returned from exile and was imprisoned. The government claimed that he committed suicide on 5 March 1969. Historical accounts instead describe starvation, torture or execution in custody. The differences cannot be reconciled from official records because the government responsible for explaining his death was also conducting the purge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonifacio_Ond%C3%B3_Ed%C3%BA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bonifacio Ondó Edú</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bonifacio Ondó Edú</span></span></span>

The safest conclusion is that an anti-Macías operation probably existed, but that the regime’s account cannot be treated as a complete or neutral description. Macías used the episode to remove rivals, intensify anti-Spanish rhetoric and consolidate power. Whether the conspiracy was extensive, partially infiltrated, deliberately provoked or later exaggerated remains uncertain.

This is not merely a dispute over palace intrigue. The March crisis became the foundation story used to justify years of arrests and executions. Establishing exactly what happened would clarify whether the dictatorship emerged in response to an authentic threat, through the opportunistic exploitation of a failed coup, or through a mixture of both.

## How many people died under Macías?

Francisco Macías Nguema ruled from independence in October 1968 until his overthrow in August 1979. His government destroyed political opposition, suspended ordinary legal protections and presided over killings, imprisonment, forced labour and mass flight. The existence and scale of repression are beyond serious doubt. The unresolved question is how many people died, who they were and which state officials were responsible in individual cases.

Reliable counting was almost impossible. Civil administration and the courts ceased functioning normally. Many victims disappeared without formal records, while relatives fled to Cameroon, Gabon, Nigeria or Spain. The government restricted outside scrutiny, and international organisations often had to rely on refugee testimony. By 1979, an International Commission of Jurists observer described an economy and public administration close to collapse and noted that the United Nations had acted after years of inadequate government cooperation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/1979/01/Equatorial-Guinea-fair-trial-trial-observation-report-1979-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of Jurists</span></span></span>

Estimates repeated in later histories vary widely, and dramatic claims should be handled carefully. Reports that one-third of the population died sometimes blur together those killed, those who died through neglect and those driven into exile. Contemporary accounts did agree that a very large share of the population had fled and that particular communities, including educated people and many Bubi residents of Bioko, suffered severe persecution. The exact totals remain uncertain because no comprehensive register of victims was created before records and institutions were destroyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1979/08/09/equatorial-guinea-a-bloody-decade/29fda837-5db4-4692-9d8f-5f021d32f5a1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Washington Post Equatorial Guinea: A Bloody Decade</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington Post Equatorial Guinea: A Bloody Decade</span></span></span>

Macías’s public trial in September 1979 offered a rare opportunity to assemble evidence. The International Commission of Jurists’ observer found abundant, convincing evidence that multiple murders had been committed on Macías’s direct orders and that the theft of public funds was also established. Yet the observer criticised parts of the legal case, including charges that lacked a sound basis in the law then applicable. Macías and several co-defendants were executed within hours of sentencing, leaving no meaningful time for appeal or clemency proceedings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/1979/01/Equatorial-Guinea-fair-trial-trial-observation-report-1979-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of Jurists</span></span></span>

The trial therefore answered one question while leaving another open. It demonstrated that Macías bore direct responsibility for murder and systematic repression. It did not create a complete historical accounting. Only eleven defendants were tried, despite the much larger number of officials, guards, party militants and local authorities who participated in the regime. The new head of state, Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo, had himself held senior military responsibilities under Macías, a fact the international observer noted when discussing refugees’ doubts about the new government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj.org/wp-content/uploads/1979/01/Equatorial-Guinea-fair-trial-trial-observation-report-1979-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj.org">[International Commission of Jurists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Commission of Jurists</span></span></span>

The enduring mystery is consequently not whether atrocities occurred. It is the missing map of them: names, dates, burial sites, orders and institutional responsibility. A credible national truth process, unrestricted archival access and systematic collection of testimony could answer some of these questions, but the passage of time has made recovery increasingly difficult.

## The embassy murder that became a legend

On 30 August 1971, Donald Leahy was killed inside the small United States embassy in Santa Isabel, now Malabo. The other American diplomat stationed there, Alfred Erdos, tied Leahy to a chair in the communications area and stabbed him with scissors. Erdos was removed from the country, tried in the United States and found not guilty by reason of insanity.

The identity of the killer is not disputed, so this is not an unsolved murder in the conventional sense. Its mystery lies in motive, mental state and the stories that accumulated around it. Erdos had broadcast warnings about an alleged communist conspiracy and appeared to believe that Leahy was part of a hostile plot. Later recollections debated whether he had suffered a genuine psychotic collapse, exaggerated or feigned illness, or broken under the isolation and fear surrounding Macías’s increasingly violent state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adst.org/2013/09/murder-in-an-embassy-part-ii-paranoid-psychotic-or-faked-insanity/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adst.org">[adst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on adst.org.</span></span></span>

The most detailed retrospective account was written by Lewis Hoffacker, the US ambassador responsible for the post. Hoffacker openly acknowledged that documentation was incomplete, important witnesses had died or remained silent, and his reconstruction relied partly on ageing memories. He regarded Erdos as a deeply troubled man who collapsed under exceptional strain, but he also recorded rumours and unresolved questions that could not be verified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Erdos%20Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adst.org">[adst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Microsoft Word</span></span></span>

One persistent story alleged a sexual relationship between the two men and treated the killing as a private dispute disguised as political paranoia. The surviving memoir shows how fragile that claim is: it rested on indirect reports, alleged autopsy information that the writer could not examine, and gossip transmitted through several people. It should not be presented as established fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Erdos%20Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: adst.org">[adst.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">adst.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Microsoft Word</span></span></span>

The case is valuable precisely because it demonstrates how a documented crime becomes mythologised. A closed diplomatic setting, a dictatorship, sensational rumours and incomplete records created an inviting space for confident explanations. The responsible historical position is narrower: Erdos killed Leahy during an apparent paranoid crisis, but the full interaction of personal illness, professional pressures and the surrounding political terror cannot now be reconstructed with certainty.

## The treaty that could not be proved

For decades, Equatorial Guinea and Gabon disputed sovereignty over the tiny islands of Mbanié, Cocotiers and Conga. Gabonese forces had occupied Mbanié since 1972, and the disagreement gained greater importance when offshore oil exploration raised the economic stakes. At the centre of the dispute was an archival puzzle: did a 1974 agreement known as the Bata Convention legally transfer the islands to Gabon?

Gabon relied on a copy of the supposed agreement. Equatorial Guinea denied that it was a valid treaty and argued that sovereignty instead followed the 1900 convention that divided French and Spanish colonial possessions. The controversy was unusually concrete. It did not depend on folklore or secret maps but on the status of a document for which no signed original capable of proving Gabon’s claim was produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/equatorial-guinea-tells-world-court-gabons-claim-islands-is-untenable-2024-09-30/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Equatorial Guinea tells World Court Gabon&#x27;s claim on islands is untenable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Equatorial Guinea tells World Court Gabon&#x27;s claim on islands is untenable</span></span></span>

The two countries eventually submitted the issue to the International Court of Justice. In May 2025, the Court held that the 1900 colonial convention supplied the applicable legal title and that the alleged 1974 convention did not establish Gabonese sovereignty. Equatorial Guinea was recognised as holding title to the three islands, and Gabon was required to withdraw from territory it had occupied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj-cij.org/node/205516" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj-cij.org">[International Court of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj-cij.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icj-cij.org.</span></span></span>

This mystery is therefore substantially resolved. Yet it remains an instructive part of Equatorial Guinea’s historical record because the outcome depended on archival authenticity. For more than half a century, military possession and competing national narratives persisted around a document whose legal existence could not be adequately demonstrated.

The case also offers a useful contrast with the country’s unresolved deaths and disappearances. Courts can settle a mystery when the relevant question is narrowly framed, the parties submit evidence and an independent tribunal has authority to decide. Cases such as Mañé’s disappearance are harder because witnesses have died, archives remain restricted and no comparable judicial process has compelled the state to disclose what it knows.

## Why the gaps endure

Equatorial Guinea’s mysteries share several causes. Spanish colonial authorities treated political dissent as a security issue, leaving crucial decisions inside police, military and diplomatic files. After independence, Macías dismantled the institutions that might have recorded crimes. His overthrow produced a rapid trial rather than a broad inquiry, while the succeeding government had little incentive to investigate every official who had served the previous regime.

The historical record is also geographically scattered. Relevant papers may lie in Equatorial Guinea, Spain, France, the United States, United Nations collections or the private archives of former officials and missionaries. Refugees carried memories across several countries, but oral testimony was not always collected before witnesses died. Research on Equatorial Guinea has repeatedly described a history obscured by political silence and limited archival access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3145115/view" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl">[Leiden University Scholarly Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on universiteitleiden.nl.</span></span></span>

This makes disciplined language essential. Several levels of certainty should not be confused:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Documented fact:** Mañé disappeared after contact with colonial authorities; Ndongo fell from a palace window; Macías ordered murders; Erdos killed Leahy.
* **Strong inference:** Mañé was probably the victim of an enforced disappearance linked to the Spanish colonial state.
* **Disputed interpretation:** whether the March 1969 coup was wholly independent, infiltrated, provoked or later magnified.
* **Unverified claim:** precise stories about bodies being thrown into the sea, private motives in the embassy murder or exact nationwide death totals unsupported by named records.
* **Resolved archival dispute:** the alleged 1974 island agreement was insufficient to displace Equatorial Guinea’s title under the 1900 convention.

</div>

The greatest national enigma is therefore the absence of a full public account of political violence across the transition from colonial rule to dictatorship. Individual cases can be investigated, but they form part of a wider unanswered question: how many lives were erased not only by killing and exile, but by the destruction or concealment of the records that should have preserved their names?
