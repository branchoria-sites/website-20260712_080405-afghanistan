## Introduction

The 2022 convictions over Thomas [Sankara]({{ 'sankara/' | relative_url }})’s killing established criminal responsibility at the highest level, but did not settle every allegation about the operation or possible foreign involvement. The murder of Norbert [Zongo]({{ 'zongo/' | relative_url }}) remains an unfinished struggle for justice more than a quarter of a century later. At Loropéni, archaeology has connected imposing ruins to regional gold production, while leaving their precise origins, functions and occupational history open to debate. Together, these cases show how political obstruction, lost evidence and incomplete excavation can turn documented history into enduring national mystery.

## Who ordered and supported the killing of Thomas Sankara?

Thomas Sankara, Burkina Faso’s president and revolutionary leader, was shot dead with twelve companions in Ouagadougou on 15 October 1987. The attack occurred during the coup that brought his former ally Blaise Compaoré to power. For decades, the basic sequence was clear while judicial accountability remained blocked: Sankara was killed by an armed unit, Compaoré became president immediately afterwards, and the new government buried the victims hastily and discouraged serious investigation. An inquiry reopened after Compaoré’s fall. Sankara’s presumed remains and those of his companions were exhumed in 2015, and a military trial began in October 2021. In April 2022, the court convicted Compaoré in absentia of complicity in Sankara’s murder and sentenced him to life imprisonment. General Gilbert Diendéré, a leading figure in the former presidential security apparatus, and Hyacinthe Kafando, accused of commanding the attacking unit, also received life sentences. Other defendants were convicted of offences including attacks on state security and concealing bodies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cfr.org/articles/blaise-compaores-conviction-momentous-victory-rule-law-and-citizen-power-africa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cfr.org">[Council on Foreign Relations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cfr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas Sankara, marks an important victory for the rule of law</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Council on Foreign RelationsBlaise Compaore&#x27;s Conviction Is a Momentous Victory for...April 6, 2022 — 6 Apr 2022 — Blaise Compaore&#x27;s sen...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 6, 2022</span></span></span>

That verdict transformed the case. It is no longer accurate to describe Sankara’s death simply as an unsolved assassination whose perpetrators are wholly unknown. A court has found that the killing formed part of a conspiracy involving Compaoré and senior members of his security system. The mystery that remains concerns the operation’s complete architecture: who devised every stage, which political or military figures were aware of it, and whether outside governments or regional actors gave encouragement, assistance or prior approval.

### What the trial established

The strongest evidence points towards an organised seizure of power rather than a spontaneous confrontation. Sankara and his colleagues were gathered at the headquarters of the National Revolutionary Council when gunmen arrived. One survivor, Alouna Traoré, provided testimony about the attack, while former members of the security establishment described the political and military tensions preceding it. The immediate transfer of authority to Compaoré and the subsequent concealment of the victims’ bodies supported the prosecution’s case that the deaths were integral to the coup.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2021/oct/11/thomas-sankara-trial-burkina-faso" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sankara, who became an icon for his Marxist, pan-African views, was killed in a 1987 coup. The later treatment of the dead also mattered. The bodies were buried rapidly, and Sankara’s death certificate reportedly recorded “natural causes”, an explanation incompatible with the circumstances of the shooting. For years, the authorities resisted exhumation and further inquiry. These actions do not by themselves identify every conspirator, but they are consistent with an institutional attempt to suppress evidence and prevent a judicial account from emerging.

The 2022 judgment therefore answered one of the central questions: Compaoré’s ascent was not merely an incidental consequence of an unexplained clash. The court held him criminally responsible for complicity in the assassination. His absence from the trial, however, meant that he was never questioned publicly under oath about the planning, the chain of command or his contacts before and after the coup.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cfr.org/articles/blaise-compaores-conviction-momentous-victory-rule-law-and-citizen-power-africa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cfr.org">[Council on Foreign Relations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cfr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas Sankara, marks an important victory for the rule of law</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Council on Foreign RelationsBlaise Compaore&#x27;s Conviction Is a Momentous Victory for...April 6, 2022 — 6 Apr 2022 — Blaise Compaore&#x27;s sen...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 6, 2022</span></span></span>

### What remains disputed

The most persistent unresolved question is whether foreign powers helped to remove Sankara. He had challenged French influence in Africa, criticised international debt, quarrelled with neighbouring leaders and pursued an independent foreign policy. These positions gave several governments reasons to regard him as difficult, but motive is not proof of participation.

Allegations have variously implicated France, Côte d’Ivoire, Libya and Liberian armed networks. Some accounts connect the coup to wider regional manoeuvring involving Charles Taylor, who later led Liberia. These theories rely on political relationships, testimony given years later and disputed reconstructions of meetings and movements. They have not produced a publicly available evidential record strong enough to establish that a foreign government ordered or directly assisted the shooting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thomassankara.net/who-killed-thomas-sankara-by-bruno-jaffre/?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thomassankara.net">[Thomas Sankara Website - Officiel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thomassankara.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas Sankara Website</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by Bruno Jaffré - My BlogSankara was killed in an agreement that Burkina and Libya would help Charles Taylor and his men seize power in L...</span></span></span>

France promised to declassify relevant records, and several batches of documents were transmitted to Burkina Faso. Yet campaigners and Sankara’s family have argued that important military and intelligence files remain unavailable or insufficiently disclosed. The existence of withheld or incomplete archives sustains suspicion, but it does not prove that those archives contain evidence of French complicity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2021/oct/11/thomas-sankara-trial-burkina-faso" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sankara, who became an icon for his Marxist, pan-African views, was killed in a 1987 coup. The most defensible conclusion is therefore narrower than either complete denial or grand conspiracy. The domestic conspiracy has been judicially established. Possible foreign knowledge, encouragement or involvement remains unproven in the public record. Resolving that question would require fuller archival disclosure, access to surviving participants and a careful distinction between diplomatic hostility towards Sankara and operational participation in his death.

## Why is Norbert Zongo’s murder still unresolved?

Norbert Zongo was Burkina Faso’s best-known investigative journalist when he was killed on 13 December 1998. His burned vehicle was found near Sapouy, roughly 100 kilometres from Ouagadougou. Inside were the bodies of Zongo and three companions: his brother Ernest Zongo, Blaise Ilboudo and Abdoulaye Nikiéma. Evidence indicated that the four had been shot before the vehicle was set alight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justiceinfo.net/en/31060-burkina-faso-still-awaiting-justice-for-sankara-and-zongo-murders.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justiceinfo.net">[justiceinfo.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justiceinfo.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">31060 burkina faso still awaiting justice for sankara and zongo murders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">31060 burkina faso still awaiting justice for sankara and zongo murders</span></span></span>

The probable motive has never been especially mysterious. Zongo had been investigating the death of David Ouedraogo, a driver employed by François Compaoré, the president’s brother and adviser. Ouedraogo had been accused of theft, detained by members of the presidential security apparatus and reportedly tortured. He died in January 1998. Zongo continued reporting on the case despite threats and pressure to stop.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.african-court.org/ajudata/download-file/2310/640b12be286b4899759202.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: african-court.org">[African Court on Human Rights]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">african-court.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">African Court on Human Rights THE MATTER OF BENEFICIARIES OF LATE NORBERT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">African Court on Human Rights THE MATTER OF BENEFICIARIES OF LATE NORBERT</span></span></span>

The real mystery is why such a heavily scrutinised murder has taken so long to reach trial. An official commission identified several members of the presidential guard as serious suspects, but proceedings repeatedly stalled. Charges against one leading suspect were dismissed in 2006, and for years the investigation made little visible progress. The case came to symbolise not simply an unknown killer, but the ability of a powerful political system to prevent evidence from becoming a final judicial finding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rsf.org/en/african-court-says-burkina-faso-failed-investigate-murder-properly" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rsf.org">[rsf.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rsf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">african court says burkina faso failed investigate murder properly</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">african court says burkina faso failed investigate murder properly</span></span></span>

### The evidence points towards the presidential circle

No final criminal judgment has yet established who ordered the killings. Nevertheless, several facts form a coherent pattern. Zongo was examining a death connected to the president’s brother. The alleged torture of Ouedraogo involved members of the presidential security structure. Zongo had received threats. The four victims were killed in a manner suggesting a planned ambush followed by an attempt to destroy evidence.

Investigators focused on personnel linked to the Presidential Security Regiment. François Compaoré was later sought by Burkina Faso in connection with allegations of incitement to murder. French authorities arrested him in 2017 and approved his extradition in principle, but the European Court of Human Rights ruled in 2023 that extradition without adequate assurances would violate the prohibition against inhuman or degrading treatment because of the conditions and risks he might face in Burkina Faso. The ruling concerned extradition safeguards, not whether he was guilty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rsf.org/en/burkina-faso-extradition-order-major-step-toward-trial-norbert-zongo-s-murder" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rsf.org">[rsf.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rsf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">burkina faso extradition order major step toward trial norbert zongo s murder</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">burkina faso extradition order major step toward trial norbert zongo s murder</span></span></span>

The distinction is important. François Compaoré’s position, the subject of Zongo’s investigation and the conduct of security personnel create a strong basis for suspicion, but they do not replace a criminal trial. Claims that the case is already “solved” risk confusing a convincing theory with a legally tested account.

### A landmark judgment without a murder verdict

In 2014, the African Court on Human and Peoples’ Rights found that Burkina Faso had failed to investigate the murders diligently and had violated the victims’ families’ right to have their case heard. The Court criticised the prolonged proceedings, the delay in questioning relatives and the failure to pursue further investigation after charges against a principal suspect were dropped. It later ordered reparations and publication of its judgment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.african-court.org/cpmt/details-case/0132011" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: african-court.org">[african-court.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">african-court.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">details case</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">details case</span></span></span>

This was historically significant because it treated state inaction itself as a human-rights violation. It also connected the failure to identify and prosecute the killers with the wider chilling effect on journalism. A state need not have carried out a murder directly to bear responsibility for denying justice afterwards.

The decision did not identify the gunmen or mastermind. Its achievement was different: it established that the official response had been inadequate and that delay could not be dismissed as an unfortunate investigative failure.

The proceedings have remained active. Reporting in June 2026 said that a new hearing was being held after defendants appealed an order committing the case for trial, suggesting that the file had advanced further than at any earlier stage. Yet procedural movement is not the same as a final resolution, and the central accusations still require testing before a competent court.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apanews.net/burkina-to-hold-new-hearing-in-norbert-zongo-case/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apanews.net">[APAnews - Agence de Presse Africaine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apanews.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Agence de Presse Africaine Burkina to hold new hearing in Norbert Zongo case</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Agence de Presse Africaine Burkina to hold new hearing in Norbert Zongo case</span></span></span>

### Why the case became part of national memory

Zongo’s death provoked mass protests and united journalists, students, unions and civil-society organisations. Annual commemorations have kept the case visible even during periods when formal proceedings appeared dormant. The demand for justice became inseparable from opposition to impunity under Compaoré.

Its historical importance also lies in what Zongo was investigating. The murder did not silence only one reporter; it exposed the danger faced by anyone attempting to examine abuses connected to the presidency. That is why the unresolved question is broader than “Who pulled the trigger?” It also asks who protected the perpetrators, which institutions failed, and how long political power can delay a public reckoning.

## Who built the walls of Loropéni?

In south-western Burkina Faso, near the town of Loropéni, stand high laterite-stone walls enclosing the remains of a substantial settlement. Some walls rise to about six metres. The ruins are the best preserved of a group of large stone enclosures in the region and became Burkina Faso’s first UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1225/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Unlike the political killings, Loropéni is an archaeological mystery rather than a suspected crime. No surviving inscription identifies its founders, rulers or original name. Written records are scarce, oral traditions were recorded long after the settlement’s earliest phases, and much of the interior remains unexcavated. UNESCO therefore describes it as a site surrounded by significant uncertainty and expected to yield further information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1225/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The ruins should not be presented as an inexplicable “lost city”. Archaeology and regional history provide a broad interpretation. The settlement was connected to communities involved in the extraction, processing and trade of gold. Its most prosperous phase probably fell between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries, when south-western Burkina Faso was linked to commercial networks extending towards major West African markets. The site was occupied, abandoned and reoccupied at different times before its final desertion in the early nineteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1225/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

### The builders cannot yet be named with certainty

UNESCO associates Loropéni with Lohron or Koulango peoples who controlled regional gold production during its height. That does not necessarily mean a single community designed and constructed every wall in one period. Long occupation, rebuilding and changing political control may have produced the site visible today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1225/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

Different local traditions have connected the enclosure with Gan, Koulango, Lohron and other communities. Such traditions are historically valuable, but they do not always preserve exact construction dates or distinguish an original builder from later occupants. Archaeologists must therefore compare oral accounts with wall sequences, pottery, radiocarbon samples, metallurgical remains and settlement patterns.

Even the word “fortress” can mislead. The walls certainly offered protection and controlled access, but defence may not have been their only purpose. They could also have marked political authority, protected stored goods, enclosed specialist workshops or regulated the movement of people and valuable material. Claims that the enclosure was simply a vault for gold go beyond the available evidence.

### Why so little is known

Loropéni’s mystery reflects the uneven survival of historical records. West African societies transmitted much knowledge orally, while colonial researchers often recorded local histories selectively and interpreted monuments through assumptions imported from elsewhere. Organic materials decay, occupation layers become disturbed, and later communities reuse stone or build over earlier remains.

The site also covers more than its most famous enclosure. Loropéni belongs to a wider landscape of related walled settlements, only some of which have been examined in detail. Understanding who built it requires comparison across the whole region rather than treating one impressive monument in isolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/345651181_Loropeni_and_Other_Large_Enclosed_Sites_in_the_South-West_of_Burkina_Faso_An_Outside_Archaeological_View" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate Loropéni and Other Large Enclosed Sites in the South- ...The World Heritage site at Loropéni is the best-preserved of about</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate Loropéni and Other Large Enclosed Sites in the South- ...The World Heritage site at Loropéni is the best-preserved of about</span></span></span>

What is mysterious, then, is not whether human beings could have built the walls. The construction falls comfortably within the capabilities of the region’s historical communities. The genuine questions concern chronology, identity, social organisation and function: which groups occupied the site at different times, how gold shaped their power, why such large enclosures were needed, and what caused the final abandonment.

## What separates evidence from modern myth?

Burkina Faso’s mysteries are especially vulnerable to oversimplification because each contains an obvious dramatic story. Sankara becomes the flawless revolutionary destroyed entirely by foreign agents. Zongo becomes the journalist whose murder is universally understood but somehow never investigated. Loropéni becomes an impossibly ancient fortress guarding lost treasure. Each version contains fragments of truth, but each erases important distinctions.

**In the Sankara case**, the domestic conspiracy is supported by testimony, physical evidence and criminal convictions. Allegations of direct foreign participation remain possible but publicly unproven. Sankara’s continuing popularity should not prevent acknowledgement that his government also restricted parts of the press and political opposition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2021/oct/11/thomas-sankara-trial-burkina-faso" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sankara, who became an icon for his Marxist, pan-African views, was killed in a 1987 coup. His former friend Blaise Compaoré, who subsequ...</span></span></span>

**In the Zongo case**, the likely motive and suspected political setting are strongly evidenced. What remains absent is a final criminal judgment identifying the complete chain of responsibility. The African Court’s ruling proved a denial of justice, not the guilt of every person named in public debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.african-court.org/cpmt/details-case/0132011" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: african-court.org">[African Court on Human Rights]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">african-court.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">details case</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">details case</span></span></span>

**At Loropéni**, gold production and long occupation are credible historical conclusions. A hidden royal treasury, an unknown civilisation or a single securely identified founding people are not. Further excavation could substantially alter present interpretations, which is normal in archaeology rather than evidence of deliberate concealment.

The most responsible way to approach these cases is to separate five levels of certainty:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Documented fact:** an event, object or legal finding supported by reliable contemporary or institutional evidence.
2. **Strong inference:** an explanation that fits several independent pieces of evidence but has not been conclusively demonstrated.
3. **Disputed interpretation:** a claim supported by some testimony or scholarship but challenged by other evidence.
4. **Tradition or memory:** an account valuable for understanding identity and remembrance, though not necessarily precise in every historical detail.
5. **Modern mythmaking:** a simplified or embellished story repeated without a traceable evidential basis.

</div>

This distinction does not make the cases less compelling. It reveals where the real mystery lies.

## Why these mysteries still matter

The Sankara and Zongo cases concern more than two famous deaths. They record how state power can shape what a country is allowed to know about its own past. Investigations were obstructed, bodies were concealed or burned, witnesses lived under political pressure, and relevant archives remained inaccessible. Later trials and international judgments recovered part of the truth, but only after public protest and decades of persistence.

Sankara’s memory has also become part of contemporary political legitimacy. His remains were reburied at the assassination site in 2023, and a new mausoleum and memorial complex have placed him at the centre of Burkina Faso’s official national story. Recent governments have invoked his anti-imperialist language, even as critics debate whether present-day policies reflect his programme or merely borrow his image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/116d368d145b036a84087d3253b9c7d8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sankara, known as &quot;Africa&#x27;s Che Guevara,&quot; was killed in a 1987 coup led by his former ally Blaise Compaore. Zongo represents a different form of remembrance: the citizen who continued asking questions despite threats. His case remains a test of whether political change can produce accountability for crimes committed close to the state. A completed trial would not restore the four victims, but it could establish an authoritative record and show that extreme delay does not guarantee permanent impunity.

Loropéni matters because it challenges the assumption that Burkina Faso’s important history begins with colonial archives or modern coups. Its walls preserve evidence of sophisticated settlement, commerce and metallurgy within older West African networks. The unanswered questions arise not from an absence of history, but from the survival of material evidence without enough accompanying written testimony.

These three mysteries therefore have different possible endings. The Sankara case may be clarified by archives and testimony. The Zongo case requires a fair criminal process capable of identifying and judging those responsible. Loropéni demands patient excavation and comparison with neighbouring sites. None is served by conspiracy for its own sake. Their enduring power comes from the harder truth that history can be partly recovered while still refusing to become complete.
