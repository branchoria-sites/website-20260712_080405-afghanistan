## Introduction

Cas en Bas, on [Saint Lucia]({{ 'which-stories-shaped-saint-lucias-hidden/' | relative_url }})’s north-eastern Atlantic coast, is one of the island’s most important archaeological landscapes because it preserves rare evidence of Indigenous life before European colonisation. The best-known part of the area is the pre-Columbian settlement at Lavoutte (also called Anse Lavoutte), where excavations have uncovered houses, cooking areas, burials, pottery, shell ornaments and food remains dating mainly to the Late Ceramic Age, roughly AD 1000–1500. Rather than confirming older stories that one Indigenous population simply replaced another, the site reveals a complex coastal community connected to wider Caribbean exchange networks, adapting to local conditions while maintaining long-distance relationships. At the same time, Cas en Bas has become a cautionary tale. Coastal erosion, hurricanes and modern development have destroyed parts of the site before archaeologists could investigate them fully, meaning some of Saint Lucia’s most valuable evidence about its earliest inhabitants has already been lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1179/0093469012Z.00000000020" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[tandfonline.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineLife and death at precolumbian Lavoutte, Saint Lucia, Lesser Antilles: Journal of Field Archaeology: Vol 37, No 3N...</span></span></span>

## What lies beneath Cas en Bas?

The archaeological remains at Cas en Bas extend across a landscape that has been recognised since the 1960s as one of Saint Lucia's richest Indigenous sites. Excavations by the Bullens in the twentieth century established its importance, while rescue excavations led by researchers from Leiden University and Saint Lucian partners in 2009–2010 dramatically expanded understanding after erosion exposed human remains and domestic features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

What makes Cas en Bas especially significant is that it preserves evidence of everyday life rather than only ceremonial objects. Archaeologists have documented:

* Hearths and cooking pits.
* Refuse deposits filled with pottery, shells and food remains.

* Decorative objects, including shell ornaments placed with some burials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

## Life at the Lavoutte settlement

One of the most revealing findings is that Lavoutte was an organised settlement rather than a temporary camp. Postholes indicate sizeable structures capable of withstanding the strong Atlantic winds, while nearby hearths and pits point to repeated domestic activity over generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

Food remains show a community that relied heavily on the sea. Fish, turtles and other marine resources dominate the archaeological deposits, reflecting a population skilled in exploiting coastal environments. This does not mean farming was absent, but marine food clearly formed a major part of daily life. Stable isotope analysis of human remains broadly supports this picture, showing diets that combined marine resources with cultivated plants and varied according to individual circumstances rather than following a single uniform pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

Pottery recovered from the site also tells an important story. Archaeologists identified ceramics belonging to several cultural traditions spanning centuries. Rather than demonstrating abrupt replacement by a completely different people, these changing pottery styles suggest gradual cultural development, interaction and regional exchange. Modern Caribbean archaeology increasingly treats ceramic traditions as evidence of evolving communities instead of simple ethnic labels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

A famous artefact known as the "Lady of Lavoutte" has attracted particular attention because some researchers have noted stylistic similarities with artistic traditions elsewhere in the Caribbean. While intriguing, it is better understood as evidence of broad cultural connections than proof of migration from any single place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

## Burials, ancestry and regional connections

The greatest archaeological surprise at Cas en Bas emerged when erosion exposed large numbers of graves. Rescue excavations eventually documented dozens of burials, many of which would probably have disappeared without emergency intervention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

These burials reveal that the living and the dead occupied closely connected spaces. Some graves lay near houses, while others were associated with different parts of the settlement. Certain individuals were buried with shell ornaments or other grave goods, suggesting that funerary practices carried social or symbolic meaning beyond simple disposal of the dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

The burial evidence also challenges older historical assumptions. Earlier colonial narratives often described Caribbean Indigenous populations as separate peoples who replaced one another through conquest. The Lavoutte evidence instead points towards more complex patterns of continuity and interaction. Researchers have combined skeletal analysis with radiocarbon dating, strontium isotope analysis and other scientific techniques to investigate where individuals grew up and how they lived. These methods suggest that at least some people moved between islands during their lifetimes, reinforcing the idea of the Lesser Antilles as a connected maritime world rather than isolated communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

Not every question has been answered. Scientific analysis continues to refine understanding of mobility, ancestry and social organisation, and archaeologists remain cautious about drawing broad conclusions from a single site. Even so, Cas en Bas strongly supports the view that Indigenous Caribbean societies maintained extensive regional networks long before European arrival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X15301905" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Human dietary assessment in the Pre-colonial Lesser Antilles: New stable isotope evidence from Lavoutte, Saint Lucia - Scien...</span></span></span>

## Erosion, development and lost evidence

Cas en Bas is important not only because of what has been discovered, but also because of what has already disappeared.

The Atlantic coastline is naturally vulnerable to wind, heavy rainfall and hurricanes, all of which steadily erode archaeological deposits. Researchers comparing surveys over several decades concluded that substantial parts of the original site have already been lost. Hurricane Dean in 2007 accelerated coastal damage, exposing graves and settlement features that had previously remained buried.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

Human activity has increased the threat. Vehicle tracks, tourism, construction work and landscape modification have disturbed archaeological layers and, in some cases, cut directly through concentrations of burials. Rescue excavations were organised specifically because researchers believed valuable evidence would otherwise vanish before it could be recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266485477_Interim_Report_on_the_Results_of_the_2009_May_and_June_Rescue_Excavations_at_the_Lavoutte_Site_Cas-En-Bas_St_Lucia_Faculty_of_Archaeology_Leiden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Interim Report on the Results of the 2009 (May and June) Rescue Excavations at the Lavoutte Site (Cas-En-Bas), St. Luci...</span></span></span>

More recently, proposed tourism development in the wider Cas en Bas area has renewed debate over how archaeological heritage should be protected. The Saint Lucia National Trust and the Archaeological and Historical Society have argued that thorough archaeological investigation should precede major construction so that surviving evidence is properly documented and, where possible, preserved. Additional investigations in 2021 recovered further artefacts from designated archaeological priority areas, illustrating that important discoveries remain possible despite decades of damage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintlucianationaltrust.com/archaeological-investigations-at-cas-en-bas/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintlucianationaltrust.com">[saintlucianationaltrust.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintlucianationaltrust.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archaeological Investigations at Cas en Bas – Saint Lucia National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archaeological Investigations at Cas en Bas – Saint Lucia National Trust</span></span></span>

The greatest mystery surrounding Cas en Bas is therefore not the existence of a lost civilisation but the extent of the information that has already been erased. Every storm, landslip or construction project risks destroying evidence that could answer questions about settlement patterns, family relationships, trade networks and daily life that written colonial records can never provide.

## Why Cas en Bas changes our understanding of Indigenous Saint Lucia

Cas en Bas demonstrates why archaeology has become central to understanding Saint Lucia's earliest history. Written records begin with European observers, but the settlement at Lavoutte provides direct evidence from Indigenous lives themselves.

The site shows that pre-colonial Saint Lucia was home to established coastal communities with durable houses, sophisticated pottery traditions, maritime economies and burial customs rooted within their settlements. Scientific analyses increasingly point towards mobility and exchange across the Lesser Antilles rather than isolated populations or simple stories of conquest and replacement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1179/0093469012Z.00000000020" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[tandfonline.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineLife and death at precolumbian Lavoutte, Saint Lucia, Lesser Antilles: Journal of Field Archaeology: Vol 37, No 3N...</span></span></span>

Equally important, Cas en Bas illustrates how historical mysteries can arise from missing evidence rather than hidden secrets. The debate today is not whether Indigenous people flourished there—they clearly did—but how much of their story can still be recovered before erosion and development erase what remains.
