## Introduction

These mysteries differ sharply in character. Some may eventually yield to documentary or scientific research. Others endure because the surviving evidence supports several interpretations, or because later political struggles have reshaped how the past is remembered. The essential distinction is between a genuine evidential gap and a mystery enlarged by legend. In Montenegro, the two often overlap: incomplete records leave room for stories, while stories influence which questions later generations choose to ask.

## Who was Šćepan Mali?

In the autumn of 1766, an obscure man appeared in the Venetian-controlled coastal region near Montenegro. Within little more than a year, he had become Montenegro’s effective ruler. His rise depended on an extraordinary rumour: that he was Peter III, the Russian emperor deposed and reported dead in 1762.

The newcomer called himself Šćepan Mali, although neither element can be treated confidently as his birth name. He did not issue a clear declaration that he was Peter III. Instead, he used ambiguous remarks, emotional gestures and silence to allow the identification to spread. Montenegro’s admiration for Orthodox Russia, combined with weak central authority and persistent conflict between clans, made the story politically powerful. Even warnings from people who knew that Peter III was dead failed to destroy the belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%A0%C4%87epan_Mali" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Šćepan Mali</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Šćepan Mali</span></span></span>

Once accepted as ruler, Šćepan did more than play a theatrical part. He tried to curb blood feuds, strengthened central authority and introduced institutions intended to settle disputes. His ability to impose a degree of order helps explain why many Montenegrins continued to support him after Russian representatives exposed him as an impostor. The attraction was therefore not simply credulity. His supposed identity supplied legitimacy, but his usefulness helped preserve it.

The central mystery is not whether he was Peter III; he was not. The unresolved questions are his real name, birthplace, previous career and original purpose. During a Russian interrogation, he reportedly gave contradictory accounts, at one point claiming to be from Ioannina and later describing himself as a Dalmatian named Rajčević. The inconsistencies suggest deliberate concealment rather than confusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%A0%C4%87epan_Mali" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Šćepan Mali</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Šćepan Mali</span></span></span>

One modern theory identifies him with Jovan Stefanović Baljević, an educated Montenegrin who had studied abroad, served in Russia and possessed the linguistic and political skills required for the deception. Supporters of the theory point to uncertain details surrounding Baljević’s later life and disappearance from clear documentation. The identification remains unproved, however, and Montenegro’s National Library has described Šćepan’s identity as an unsolved riddle despite repeated attempts to settle it from surviving records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nb-cg.me/en/events/892-national-library-of-montenegro-durde-crnojevic-and-matica-crnogorska-branch-cetinje-organize-presentation-of-the-proceedings-scepan-mali-in-documents-1767-1773-prepared-by-marijan-maso-miljic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nb-cg.me">[nb-cg.me]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nb-cg.me</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Despite numerous attempts to establish his ...Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">national library of montenegro “đurđe crnojević” and matica ...31 May 2022 — With his personality and state activities, Šćepan Mali left...</span></span></span>

Šćepan was murdered in 1773 by a servant commonly said to have been bribed by an Ottoman official. His violent end created an additional layer of legend, but the real historical puzzle lies in the success of the imposture. A stranger managed to turn a false imperial association into practical political authority, then remained influential even after the fraud was publicly demonstrated.

The most plausible explanation is that Šćepan was an intelligent and experienced adventurer who understood Montenegro’s political needs and its emotional attachment to Russia. There is no good evidence that he was a secretly surviving emperor, a supernatural figure or the agent of a grand international conspiracy. His true identity may have been less dramatic than his career, but without a decisive contemporary document it remains Montenegro’s clearest unresolved personal mystery.

## Where did Montenegro’s first printing press operate?

The Crnojević printing house produced some of the earliest Cyrillic printed books in south-eastern Europe during the 1490s. It was established under Đurađ Crnojević, ruler of Zeta, as Ottoman expansion threatened the remaining independence of his state. Its first completed book, a liturgical volume dated 1494, survives in scattered copies and fragments. The machinery and workplace do not.

The disputed question is whether the press operated at Obod, near present-day Rijeka Crnojevića, at Cetinje, or at both locations in succession. Local tradition strongly associates the enterprise with Obod. Cetinje, meanwhile, became the political and religious centre of the Crnojević state and is identified by many accounts as the press’s principal or final location. Even historical surveys sympathetic to the Obod tradition acknowledge disagreement over where the books were actually produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crnojevi%C4%87_printing_house" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crnojević printing house</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crnojević printing house</span></span></span>

The uncertainty exists because no printing workshop has been excavated and securely identified. The known books give names, dates and religious information, but not the kind of precise workshop address that would settle a modern archaeological dispute. Later tradition may also have blended the history of the press with the wider symbolic importance of Obod and Cetinje.

A reasonable reconstruction is that equipment was obtained through Venetian connections and that printing activity may have shifted as political and military conditions changed. Some historians have therefore proposed a two-stage history: an initial operation at Obod followed by work at Cetinje. This fits the unsettled final years of the Crnojević state, but it remains a reconstruction rather than a demonstrated sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.njegos.org/medieval/crnprint.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: njegos.org">[njegos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">njegos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">There some disagreements about the place of working of the Printing office, whether it is Obod or Cetinje. Claims that the surviving ruins at Obod are unquestionably the foundations of the original printing house should consequently be treated with caution. A memorial site can preserve an authentic regional tradition without supplying archaeological proof of the precise workshop. The mystery is not whether the press existed; its books establish that beyond doubt. It concerns where the machinery stood, how the workshop was organised and what became of it after production ended around 1496.

The question matters because the press occupies a major place in Montenegro’s cultural memory. Locating it is therefore not merely an antiquarian exercise. Obod and Cetinje represent different geographical claims to one of the country’s foundational achievements in literacy, religion and state-sponsored culture.

## Did Montenegro legally disappear in 1918?

Montenegro entered the First World War as an independent kingdom. It was occupied by Austria-Hungary in 1916, while King Nikola I and his government went into exile. When the Central Powers collapsed in 1918, an assembly convened in Podgorica deposed the king and approved unconditional union with Serbia. Within days, the enlarged Serbian kingdom became part of the new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes.

The basic chronology is clear. The lasting mystery concerns political legitimacy: did the Podgorica Assembly express the will of Montenegro’s population, or was it a managed gathering created to legitimise a result already secured by Serbian military and political power?

The assembly was not elected under Montenegro’s existing constitutional procedures. Its selection rules were drawn up amid military occupation, administrative breakdown and intense pressure for South Slav unification. Supporters of the decision argued that most Montenegrins regarded themselves as Serbs and desired union. Opponents contended that they might have supported a common state while rejecting Montenegro’s unconditional absorption and the removal of its dynasty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sav.sk/journals/uploads/01151354SPS_1_2017_Z%20Polackova_P%20C%20%20Duin.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sav.sk">[sav.sk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sav.sk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Montenegro&#x27;s contested identity and the failure of Yugoslavia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Montenegro&#x27;s contested identity and the failure of Yugoslavia</span></span></span>

This distinction is crucial. Support for some form of union does not automatically prove support for the exact terms adopted in November 1918. Available scholarship continues to disagree over whether procedural defects made the decisions legally void, whether revolutionary circumstances permitted an alternative basis of legitimacy, and how freely delegates were chosen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.openstarts.units.it/handle/10077/37256" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openstarts.units.it">[Openstarts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openstarts.units.it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on units.it.</span></span></span>

The Christmas Uprising of January 1919 shows that resistance was substantial enough to produce armed conflict. The insurgents, commonly associated with the pro-autonomy “Greens”, opposed supporters of unconditional union known as the “Whites”. Yet the uprising does not by itself reveal what a free national vote would have produced. The rebels were divided between complete independence, restoration of the Petrović dynasty and a more equal federal relationship within a South Slav state.

Later governments and historians turned 1918 into a test of national identity. One narrative presented the assembly as the fulfilment of unity between two branches of the same Serbian people. Another described it as the unlawful destruction of the Montenegrin state. After Montenegro restored independence in 2006, its parliament formally annulled the assembly’s decisions and characterised them as illegitimate. That act expressed the judgement of a later sovereign legislature, but it could not recreate the choices available to people living amid the military and political chaos of 1918.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podgorica_Assembly" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Podgorica Assembly</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Podgorica Assembly</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusion is that the assembly achieved a real political objective but did not provide a reliable democratic measurement of public opinion. There was genuine support for union, genuine opposition to unconditional annexation and no adequately free plebiscite capable of distinguishing between the available constitutional options. The enduring uncertainty is therefore not whether union occurred, but whether it possessed sufficient popular and legal authority.

## Why were a horse and dog buried at Doclea?

In 2024, archaeologists working at ancient Doclea, near Podgorica, reported an unusual burial containing the skeletons of a horse and a dog. Researchers described it as the only known Roman-period animal burial of its kind discovered in Montenegro. The animals had apparently been deliberately placed rather than casually discarded, but the reason remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rferl.org/a/montenegro-doclea-roman-illyria-animal-grave/33056103.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rferl.org">[RadioFreeEurope/RadioLiberty]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rferl.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rferl.org.</span></span></span>

Doclea was an important Roman urban centre, with public buildings, houses, roads, temples and burial grounds. Animal bones are common at ancient sites because animals were eaten, worked, traded and sacrificed. A carefully arranged burial is different: it suggests that the animals possessed a specific social, ritual or emotional meaning.

Several explanations are possible. The horse and dog may have belonged to an elite owner and been buried as valued companions. They may have formed part of a religious ceremony, a funerary offering or a symbolic act connected to beliefs about protection and the afterlife. It is also possible that the burial reflects a local custom not described in surviving Roman texts.

At present, none of these theories can be preferred with confidence. The animals’ age, sex, injuries, diet and relationship to nearby human burials may eventually provide clues. Radiocarbon dating, analysis of the bones and close study of the burial layer could determine whether both animals died at the same time and whether they were deliberately killed. Until that work is complete, the grave should be described as unexplained rather than ritual.

The discovery is a useful example of how an archaeological mystery differs from folklore. There is a physical deposit, a documented excavation and a limited set of testable questions. The mystery has not been created by the absence of evidence altogether, but by the inability of the surviving evidence to reveal intention.

It also highlights the fragility of Montenegro’s archaeological record. Investigative reporting has documented looting, illegal metal detecting and trafficking in antiquities, all of which remove objects from the layers that give them meaning. Once an artefact is detached from its archaeological setting, questions about date, ownership and purpose can become impossible to answer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalift.org/uncovering-montenegros-hidden-issue-smuggling-of-national-archaeological-treasures/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalift.org">[Journalift]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalift.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalift.org.</span></span></span>

## Who ordered the murder of Duško Jovanović?

On the night of 27 May 2004, Duško Jovanović, editor-in-chief of the opposition newspaper *Dan*, was shot as he left his office in Podgorica. He died from his wounds. His newspaper and family said that he had previously received threats, while the newspaper’s offices had been attacked by fire the previous year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fom.coe.int/alerte/detail/36717359?langue=en-GB" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fom.coe.int">[fom.coe.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fom.coe.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on coe.int.</span></span></span>

The killing became Montenegro’s most significant unresolved crime against a journalist. Damir Mandić was eventually convicted of complicity, but the identities of the gunman or gunmen, accomplices and those who commissioned the murder were not established. In a 2024 report, the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe stated that the masterminds and actual perpetrators remained unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pace.coe.int/files/33197/html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pace.coe.int">[Parliamentary Assembly Council of Europe]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pace.coe.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on coe.int.</span></span></span>

The case has generated numerous theories because Jovanović and his newspaper had reported on organised crime, political corruption and allegations involving state institutions. Those subjects supply possible motives, but they do not establish who ordered the attack. Public claims have often moved further than the evidence available in court.

Several factors have prolonged the uncertainty: disputed forensic work, allegations that evidence was mishandled, questions about the scope of the original investigation and the difficulty of determining whether the murder was connected to one particular publication or to Jovanović’s broader editorial activity. International press-freedom organisations have repeatedly treated the failure to identify the organisers as evidence of continuing impunity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fom.coe.int/alerte/detail/36717359?langue=en-GB" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fom.coe.int">[fom.coe.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fom.coe.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on coe.int.</span></span></span>

The humane approach is to resist turning the case into a conspiracy story. It is well established that Jovanović was deliberately assassinated and that the full chain of responsibility has not been proved. It is reasonable to suspect a connection with his work, but there is insufficient public evidence to identify a particular political, criminal or institutional sponsor as fact.

Unlike the mystery of Šćepan Mali, this is not primarily an intellectual puzzle from a remote past. It remains a question of accountability. Its importance lies in what an unresolved political murder communicates to journalists, witnesses and citizens: that identifying one participant is not equivalent to explaining the crime.

## What these cases reveal about Montenegro

Montenegro’s historical mysteries tend to cluster around moments when authority was weak, changing or disputed. Šćepan Mali emerged from fragmented eighteenth-century rule. The Crnojević press disappeared as a medieval state faced Ottoman expansion. The controversy of 1918 followed occupation, exile and the collapse of empires. The Doclea burial survives without the beliefs that once explained it. The Jovanović case reflects the difficulty of establishing responsibility where journalism, politics and organised crime may intersect.

They also show why different kinds of uncertainty require different standards.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Documented mystery:** Šćepan Mali’s false imperial identity is established, but his real identity is unknown.
* **Archaeological uncertainty:** The Doclea animals were deliberately buried, but their meaning has not yet been demonstrated.
* **Historiographical dispute:** The events of 1918 are known, while their legality and representativeness remain contested.
* **Location dispute:** The Crnojević books survive, but the precise history of their workshop is unresolved.
* **Unsolved crime:** Jovanović’s murder is proven, but the full group responsible has not been identified.

</div>

Folklore and political memory can preserve valuable clues, but neither should be confused with proof. Local tradition may point researchers towards a printing site; it cannot authenticate a building by itself. National memory may explain why one interpretation of 1918 became influential; it cannot replace examination of electoral procedures and contemporary records. Suspicion may identify possible motives in a murder; it cannot establish guilt.

The most enduring Montenegrin enigmas matter because they concern legitimacy: who had the right to rule, where cultural authority was created, whether a state was freely dissolved, how ancient people understood death, and whether modern institutions can uncover the organisers of a political killing. Their fascination lies not in hidden supernatural forces, but in the difficult distance between what the evidence proves and what Montenegro still wants to know.
