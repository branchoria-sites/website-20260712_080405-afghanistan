## Introduction

In 1863, the small atolls that now form [Tuvalu]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-tuvalus-greatest/' | relative_url }}) suffered one of the greatest demographic disasters in their recorded history. Peruvian labour recruiters, part of the Pacific "blackbirding" trade, removed hundreds of people from Funafuti and Nukulaelae by deception, coercion and outright kidnapping. Most never returned. The broad outline of the tragedy is well documented, yet one central mystery remains unresolved: what became of the individual islanders who disappeared into the labour system, and exactly how many were taken in the first place?



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Tuvalu_9a2248_tuvalu_1863_labour_r_d1212a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Missing Islanders illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Tuvalu_9a2248_tuvalu_1863_labour_r_d1212a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Tuvalu_9a2248_tuvalu_1863_labour_r_d1212a-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The uncertainty is not because historians doubt that the raids happened. Instead, it stems from fragmentary shipping records, conflicting eyewitness reports, incomplete colonial documentation and the fact that many of those taken died anonymously in Peru or during failed repatriation attempts. More than 160 years later, the event remains one of Tuvalu's most profound historical losses because hundreds of lives effectively vanish from the historical record.

## How recruiters removed people from the atolls

The raids on Tuvalu occurred during the final months of the Peruvian recruitment drive across Polynesia. Peru faced severe labour shortages, particularly for work on the guano-rich Chincha Islands, and private recruiters sailed across the Pacific seeking workers. Although contracts theoretically existed, many recruiters relied on fraud or violence rather than genuine consent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.persee.fr/doc/jso_0300-953x_1990_num" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: persee.fr">[Persée]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">persee.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">jso 0300 953x 1990 num</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PerséeThe Peruvian slavers in Tuvalu, 1863: how many did they kidnap? - Persée...</span></span></span>

Missionary Archibald Wright Murray, who arrived in the region soon afterwards, described recruiters promising islanders education, Christianity or employment before carrying them away to Peru instead. Resident European traders on Funafuti and Nukulaelae also appear to have assisted recruiters by acting as intermediaries, making the deception easier because islanders often trusted familiar local figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbirding" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

For isolated atolls with only a few hundred inhabitants, losing even dozens of adults was catastrophic. On Nukulaelae, the removal of large numbers of working-age people disrupted family life, food production and traditional leadership. Unlike larger Pacific societies that could absorb some population loss, these communities had little demographic resilience.

## Why estimates of the missing still differ

One of the enduring puzzles is not whether the kidnappings occurred but how many people disappeared.

Different historical sources produce noticeably different totals:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Murray estimated roughly 180 people taken from Funafuti and about 200 from Nukulaelae.
* Tuvaluan historian Laumua Kofe later argued for approximately 171 from Funafuti and around 250 from Nukulaelae.
* Population specialists Richard Bedford, Barrie Macdonald and Doug Munro broadly supported the higher Nukulaelae estimate.
* Modern government publications often summarise the event as more than 400 people taken from the two islands combined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbirding" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

These differences arise for understandable reasons rather than historical negligence.

First, there was no complete census immediately before the raids. Population figures came from missionary observations, visiting captains and later colonial estimates, all collected for different purposes.

Second, recruiters themselves had little incentive to maintain accurate records. Ships sometimes exchanged captives between vessels, and surviving manifests rarely identify every individual by name.

Third, later writers often reconstructed totals by comparing estimated populations before and after the raids rather than counting named victims directly. That method is valuable but inevitably produces different answers depending on which baseline figures are accepted. Doug Munro's detailed re-examination of surviving documentation demonstrates just how difficult it is to reconcile all the surviving evidence into one definitive total.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.persee.fr/doc/jso_0300-953x_1990_num" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: persee.fr">[Persée]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">persee.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">jso 0300 953x 1990 num</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PerséeThe Peruvian slavers in Tuvalu, 1863: how many did they kidnap? - Persée...</span></span></span>

## What records reveal about death and failed return

The greatest mystery concerns individual fate rather than the overall event.

Peru suppressed the Polynesian labour trade in April 1863 after growing international criticism. The government ordered surviving islanders returned to their homes, but by then disease, malnutrition and mistreatment had already caused enormous mortality. Smallpox and dysentery spread rapidly through labour depots and repatriation vessels, killing many who had survived the initial voyages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbirding" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Because the surviving documentation is incomplete, historians can reconstruct only fragments of what happened after islanders left Tuvalu.

What is reasonably well established includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* many Tuvaluans died during transport or shortly after arrival in Peru;
* others died before repatriation ships departed;
* some probably died during return voyages;
* very few, if any, successfully reached their home islands again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbirding" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

This lack of individual records explains why the victims remain, in many respects, "missing". Unlike military casualties or emigrants recorded in passenger lists, most disappeared into a poorly documented labour system thousands of kilometres from home.

Some official Tuvaluan publications state categorically that none returned. Other historians are more cautious, noting that surviving records are too incomplete to identify every repatriated individual with certainty. What is beyond dispute is that no significant group ever re-established itself in Tuvalu after the raids.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=W%2FzKJkBJbjfdxCA5a3DGcoAh7trNFfJ%2F%2B0rtZ1%2FFxjFmDhqjkpvFO6EdgXFjK385Mi6hxB%2BXuwfHh7G1CfC%2FUA%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: docstore.ohchr.org">[Docstore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">docstore.ohchr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Docstore Etpu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Docstore Etpu</span></span></span>

## Why the disappearance remains historically unresolved

Unlike many historical mysteries, this case is not driven by competing conspiracy theories. The uncertainty comes from the nature of nineteenth-century record keeping.

Several factors continue to frustrate researchers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Missing passenger lists:** Many recruitment manifests have been lost or never existed.
* **Changing names:** Polynesian names were often misspelled, shortened or replaced in Spanish-language documents.
* **High mortality:** Large numbers died before officials could record identities accurately.
* **Sparse local records:** Tuvalu had few written records before missionaries established more systematic documentation.
* **Scattered archives:** Relevant evidence survives across archives in Peru, Britain, New Zealand and the Pacific rather than in one comprehensive collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.persee.fr/doc/jso_0300-953x_1990_num" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: persee.fr">[Persée]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">persee.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">jso 0300 953x 1990 num</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PerséeThe Peruvian slavers in Tuvalu, 1863: how many did they kidnap? - Persée...</span></span></span>

</div>

As a result, historians can reconstruct the scale of the tragedy far more confidently than the biographies of the people who experienced it.

## Why the mystery still matters in Tuvalu

For Tuvalu, the labour raids are remembered not simply as an episode of forced migration but as a collective disappearance. Entire family lines were interrupted, communities were permanently reduced and countless individuals vanished from both their homeland and the documentary record.

The mystery therefore lies in two interconnected questions. One is statistical: precisely how many islanders were taken from Funafuti and Nukulaelae? The other is profoundly human: what happened to each of those men, women and children after the ships disappeared beyond the horizon?

Modern historical research has narrowed the numerical uncertainty and clarified how the recruitment system operated, but it has not restored the identities or final stories of most of those taken. For many Tuvaluan families, that absence is itself the lasting historical mystery.
