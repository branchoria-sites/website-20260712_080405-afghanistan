## Introduction

Other famous enigmas, such as the supposed fortune of Hetman Pavlo Polubotok in a British bank, are better understood as national legends than open investigations. Together, these cases reveal that Ukraine’s unresolved history is often a struggle over evidence, identity and the survival of records.

## Why so many Ukrainian mysteries begin with missing evidence

Ukraine has spent much of its recorded history within larger political structures, including the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Russian Empire, the Habsburg monarchy and the Soviet Union. Documents and archaeological finds were consequently dispersed among institutions in Kyiv, Lviv, Moscow, Saint Petersburg, Warsaw, Vienna and elsewhere. Wars, revolutions, censorship and border changes repeatedly interrupted the custody of archives and museum collections.

This history does not mean that every missing document or displaced object is evidence of a conspiracy. Records are lost through fire, hurried evacuation, poor cataloguing and ordinary administrative failure. Yet deliberate concealment also occurred. Soviet security institutions classified investigations, suppressed information about political repression and sometimes presented convenient official narratives in place of transparent public inquiries. The same caution applies to Ukraine’s archaeological heritage. Artefacts excavated on the territory of present-day Ukraine were often transferred to imperial or Soviet centres, while objects looted during the Second World War did not always return to their original museums. A large-scale investigation of Russian museum catalogues identified tens of thousands of objects removed from the territory of modern Ukraine over different periods, although the exact legal and historical circumstances vary from collection to collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.europeanpressprize.com/article/the-stolen-treasures-the-110000-artifacts-from-ukraine-found-in-two-russian-museums/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: europeanpressprize.com">[European Press Prize]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">europeanpressprize.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">European Press PrizeThe Stolen Treasures – The 110000 Artifacts From Ukraine...19 Sept 2023 — 110,000 artifacts have been exported from...</span></span></span>

## What happened to the remains of Yaroslav the Wise?

Yaroslav the Wise, the eleventh-century ruler associated with the rise of medieval Kyiv, was traditionally believed to have been buried in a marble sarcophagus inside Saint Sophia Cathedral. The tomb survived, but the identity and subsequent fate of the human remains placed inside it have become one of Ukraine’s clearest archival mysteries.

When researchers examined the burial in the twentieth century, they encountered a confused collection of bones rather than an untouched princely grave. Earlier investigations reportedly identified remains belonging to both a man and a woman, along with smaller fragments. The bones were removed for study during the Soviet period and later returned to Kyiv—or at least official paperwork indicated that they had been returned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://edu.lvivcenter.org/en/reflection-text/portrait-of-kyivan-prince-yaroslav-the-wise-by-soviet-historiography/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: edu.lvivcenter.org">[REESOURCES. Переосмислюючи Східну Європу]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">edu.lvivcenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Переосмислюючи Східну ЄвропуPortrait of Kyivan Prince Yaroslav the Wise by Soviet...7 Jan 2025 — At the bottom of the sarcophagus, they...</span></span></span>

A new examination in 2009 produced the central surprise. The sarcophagus contained remains identified as female, but the male skeleton previously associated with Yaroslav was absent. This raised several possibilities: the bones had been misplaced during Soviet-era scientific work, stored under another catalogue entry, deliberately substituted, or removed from Kyiv during the disruption of the Second World War. None has been conclusively demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wsj.com/world/europe/russia-ukraine-conflict-lies-in-the-bones-of-an-11th-century-prince-11641052801" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wsj.com">[The Wall Street Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wsj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Wall Street Journal Russia-Ukraine Conflict Lies in the Bones of an 11th</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Wall Street Journal Russia-Ukraine Conflict Lies in the Bones of an 11th</span></span></span>

Claims that Yaroslav’s remains were secretly taken abroad have circulated widely, but the surviving evidence is incomplete. Even institutional records contain signs of uncertainty: a 1964 document referred to a box placed in the sarcophagus with a label that questioned whether the contents were really Yaroslav’s. Recent archival reconstruction confirms that male and female remains existed during earlier examinations, but does not establish where the male bones ultimately went.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openwarstudies.org/en/dossiers/yaroslav-relics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openwarstudies.org">[openwarstudies.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openwarstudies.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">yaroslav relics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">yaroslav relics</span></span></span>

The disappearance matters because Yaroslav is claimed within several overlapping historical traditions. His rule predates modern national borders, but medieval Kyiv occupies a central place in Ukrainian history and in competing Russian narratives about the origins of statehood. Recovering the remains could permit further dating, medical study and genetic comparison, although DNA alone would not settle every historical argument. For now, the most responsible conclusion is simple: a male skeleton once associated with the tomb is missing, and the surviving chain of custody is too weak to identify when or why it disappeared.

## Is Bohdan Khmelnytsky still buried at Subotiv?

Bohdan Khmelnytsky, leader of the seventeenth-century Cossack uprising that transformed the political order of eastern Europe, is traditionally said to have been buried in the Church of Saint Elias at Subotiv in central Ukraine. Unlike Yaroslav’s case, the central uncertainty is not the loss of documented bones after a modern excavation. It is whether Khmelnytsky’s body remained in the church at all.

Later accounts claimed that Polish forces opened or desecrated the grave, while another tradition held that Cossacks secretly moved the body to protect it. These stories developed long after Khmelnytsky’s death and differ in significant details. The lack of a securely identified grave has allowed them to flourish, but repetition is not proof that either version is correct.

Modern researchers have used ground-penetrating radar, a technique that sends signals beneath a surface to identify structural differences, to investigate the church without immediately disturbing its floor and walls. A 2020 study combined radar work, historical review, modelling and limited test drilling. It detected concealed structural features that might relate to a burial space, but such readings cannot by themselves identify an individual or prove that Khmelnytsky’s remains are present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.uran.ua/geofizicheskiy/article/view/204709" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.uran.ua">[Ukrainian Scientific Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.uran.ua</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uran.ua.</span></span></span>

The plausible explanations therefore remain modest:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Khmelnytsky was buried in or near the church, and his grave survives in a concealed or altered space.
* His burial was disturbed during later conflict, after which the remains were destroyed or scattered.
* Supporters moved the body to an undisclosed location to prevent desecration.
* The traditional location is broadly correct, but later rebuilding obscured the original grave.

</div>

The mystery cannot be resolved by folklore alone. A convincing answer would require a carefully excavated burial context, credible dating and, ideally, comparative biological evidence. Even then, identifying a seventeenth-century individual without an uncontested family reference sample would be difficult. The case remains important because Khmelnytsky is remembered variously as a state-builder, military leader and instigator of catastrophic violence against Jewish and Polish communities. Finding his grave would not resolve those conflicting memories, but it would provide a rare physical anchor for a heavily contested life.

## Who murdered Andriy Yushchinskyi?

In March 1911, the body of 13-year-old Andriy Yushchinskyi was found near Kyiv. The murder itself was never conclusively solved. Instead, imperial authorities and antisemitic campaigners turned it into the Beilis affair, one of the most notorious political trials of the late Russian Empire.

Mendel Beilis, a Jewish clerk at a brickworks, was accused of killing the boy as part of an alleged religious ritual. The accusation revived the “blood libel”, the false claim that Jews murder Christian children for religious purposes. Contemporary medical evidence did not establish such a motive, and the prosecution’s case relied on prejudice, compromised testimony and highly questionable expert claims. Archival research has since shown that officials knew the evidence against Beilis was weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bsb-muenchen.de/mikro/lit471.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bsb-muenchen.de">[bsb-muenchen.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bsb-muenchen.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bsb-muenchen.de.</span></span></span>

After a widely watched trial in Kyiv in 1913, the jury acquitted Beilis. Yet the wording of the questions put to the jurors created a lasting distortion. They rejected Beilis’s guilt while answering another compound question in a manner later exploited by antisemites to pretend that the court had validated the ritual-murder allegation. It had not established any genuine religious explanation for the crime.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mjhnyc.org/events/mendelbeilis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mjhnyc.org">[Jewish Heritage Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mjhnyc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mjhnyc.org.</span></span></span>

Suspicion has often fallen on a criminal group connected with Vera Cheberiak, with the proposed motive that Yushchinskyi knew too much about its activities. That theory is plausible and was discussed by investigators and later historians, but it was never tested through a fair, complete prosecution. The state’s determination to pursue Beilis damaged the original murder inquiry, intimidated or compromised witnesses and directed attention away from other suspects.

The enduring mystery therefore has two levels. Who physically killed Yushchinskyi remains uncertain. Why Beilis was prosecuted is much clearer: the case was shaped by institutional antisemitism and political efforts to turn a child’s death into propaganda. Treating the affair merely as an unsolved crime misses its larger significance. It is also a documented example of how a government can manufacture certainty around a false theory while leaving the real crime unresolved.

## Was Alla Horska murdered by the Soviet security services?

Alla Horska was an artist, human-rights activist and prominent figure among the Ukrainian cultural dissidents of the 1960s. She opposed political repression and helped draw attention to mass graves at Bykivnia near Kyiv, where victims of the Soviet secret police had been secretly buried. She was also repeatedly monitored by the Committee for State Security, commonly known as the KGB.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://svidomi.in.ua/en/page/alla-horska-the-artist-murdered-for-creating-ukrainian-art-and-fighting-for-human-rights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: svidomi.in.ua">[Svidomi]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">svidomi.in.ua</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on in.ua.</span></span></span>

On 28 November 1970, Horska travelled to her father-in-law Ivan Zaretsky’s house in Vasylkiv and disappeared. Her body was later found there with fatal head injuries. Zaretsky was found dead on railway tracks. Soviet investigators rapidly concluded that he had killed Horska because of a personal dispute and then taken his own life.

The official scenario was doubted almost immediately. Horska was under surveillance; the investigation was completed with unusual speed; public reporting was tightly controlled; and her funeral became a rare open gathering of Ukrainian dissidents. Several participants in that cultural movement later faced arrest and imprisonment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alla_Horska" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alla Horska</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alla Horska</span></span></span>

Declassified security records strengthened suspicion of state involvement, but they did not produce an unambiguous surviving order naming Horska as the target of an assassination. The files show that the KGB closely followed her activities and remained informed about the murder investigation. Critics of the official account also point to inconsistencies in the proposed sequence of deaths and to the political usefulness of removing a highly visible dissident while attributing the crime to a family quarrel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://n-ost.org/article/who-killed-alla-horska" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: n-ost.org">[n-ost]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">n-ost.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">who killed alla horska</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">who killed alla horska</span></span></span>

Three broad interpretations remain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Zaretsky killed Horska and then died by suicide, as the Soviet investigation claimed.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://n-ost.org/article/who-killed-alla-horska" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: n-ost.org">[n-ost.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">n-ost.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">who killed alla horska</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">who killed alla horska</span></span></span>
* Security officers or their agents murdered Horska and staged the two deaths to resemble a private tragedy.
* The security services did not directly carry out the killing but influenced, exploited or prematurely closed the investigation.

</div>

The second explanation is widely regarded as credible within Ukrainian public memory, particularly given the documented persecution of Horska and her associates. Still, credibility is not the same as forensic proof. Unless additional operational files, witness testimony or physical evidence emerge, the precise mechanism and chain of command will remain uncertain. The case illustrates the central problem of investigating a suspicious death under an authoritarian system: the institution most capable of preserving the truth may also have had the strongest motive to conceal it.

## Did Hetman Polubotok leave Ukraine a fortune in Britain?

One of Ukraine’s best-known treasure stories claims that Pavlo Polubotok, an eighteenth-century Cossack leader imprisoned by Peter I, secretly deposited a huge quantity of gold in a British bank. According to later versions, most of the growing fortune was to be paid to an independent Ukraine, with the remainder going to Polubotok’s descendants.

The story is memorable because compound interest turns the alleged deposit into an impossibly vast sum. Yet the basic details vary. Different tellings name different amounts, financial institutions and interest rates. No authenticated will, bank receipt or contemporary transfer record has been produced.

The legend became widely known only in the early twentieth century, long after Polubotok’s death in 1724. Descendants and imperial officials made unsuccessful inquiries in Britain, while later Soviet and post-Soviet investigators also failed to locate the account. Historian Dmytro Yavornytsky dismissed the story as a hoax, and modern scholarship has treated it mainly as a political myth about lost sovereignty and future national reward.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_of_Polubotok" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gold of Polubotok</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gold of Polubotok</span></span></span>

There may have been some historical seed for the legend. Russian officials investigating Polubotok reportedly searched for wealth associated with him, and elite Cossack families did move money and property across borders. But this is far from proof of an enormous British deposit. The supposed fortune is best classified not as a treasure awaiting discovery, but as folklore built around a real political prisoner.

Its cultural meaning is more revealing than its banking plausibility. At moments of Ukrainian political change, the tale promised that independence would bring not only freedom but the recovery of wealth withheld for centuries. In that sense, Polubotok’s gold belongs beside other national treasure legends: an imagined inheritance that expresses historical grievance more clearly than it records a financial transaction.

## When does a displaced artefact become a national mystery?

Some Ukrainian “lost treasure” cases are not mysteries about whether objects exist. The objects are catalogued and displayed. The uncertainty concerns how they left Ukraine, whether their provenance has been fully acknowledged and where they should ultimately be housed.

Artefacts excavated in Ukraine were transferred to Russian imperial and Soviet institutions over many decades. Some removals followed the legal practices of their time; others involved coercive centralisation, wartime looting or opaque redistribution. Modern digital investigations have found large numbers of Ukrainian-origin objects in major Russian museum databases, sometimes with geographical descriptions that obscure their connection to present-day Ukraine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.europeanpressprize.com/article/the-stolen-treasures-the-110000-artifacts-from-ukraine-found-in-two-russian-museums/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: europeanpressprize.com">[European Press Prize]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">europeanpressprize.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">European Press PrizeThe Stolen Treasures – The 110000 Artifacts From Ukraine...19 Sept 2023 — 110,000 artifacts have been exported from...</span></span></span>

The Crimean gold dispute provides a particularly clear example. In early 2014, archaeological treasures from museums in Crimea were on loan to the Allard Pierson Museum in Amsterdam. Russia’s occupation and annexation of Crimea created competing claims: the Crimean museums sought the objects’ return, while the Ukrainian state argued that national cultural property should not be delivered to institutions under occupying control.

Dutch courts ultimately ruled that the collection should be transferred to Ukraine. In June 2023, the Dutch Supreme Court upheld the decision, ending the litigation; the objects were subsequently returned to Kyiv. The judgment resolved legal custody, but not every cultural question about their eventual home once Crimea is no longer occupied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hogeraad.nl/actueel/nieuwsoverzicht/2023/januari/advisory-opinion-advocate-general-to-the-supreme-court-court-appeal/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hogeraad.nl">[hogeraad.nl]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hogeraad.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hogeraad.nl.</span></span></span>

This is different from the disappearance of Yaroslav’s bones or the uncertainty surrounding Horska’s death. There is no doubt that the Crimean objects exist or that the courts examined their status. Yet the case belongs within Ukraine’s history of contested heritage because it shows how archaeological finds can become evidence in arguments over territory, political authority and historical continuity.

The continuing war has made such questions more urgent. Archaeological sites and museums have been damaged, looted or rendered inaccessible, while specialists race to document collections and excavations. Every destroyed catalogue, disturbed burial mound or unrecorded removal creates the possibility of a future mystery—not because the past was inherently unknowable, but because its evidence was damaged in the present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2023/dec/26/battle-for-the-past-the-ukrainians-trying-to-save-their-archaeological-treasure-amid-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Battle for the past: the Ukrainians trying to save their</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Battle for the past: the Ukrainians trying to save their</span></span></span>

## What the best evidence supports

Ukraine’s major historical enigmas do not all deserve equal confidence. The evidence supports a practical distinction between genuine unresolved cases, strongly suspected political crimes and stories that mainly belong to folklore.

**Well-documented mystery:** Yaroslav the Wise’s male remains are missing from the sarcophagus with which they were once associated. The surviving records establish a broken chain of custody, but not the destination of the bones.

**Open archaeological question:** Khmelnytsky’s traditional burial place is credible, and subsurface structures have been detected at the church in Subotiv. No publicly established discovery has yet identified his remains.

**Unsolved crime distorted by prejudice:** Beilis was acquitted, and the ritual-murder accusation was baseless. The murder of Yushchinskyi itself remains unresolved because the politically driven prosecution diverted the investigation.

**Highly suspicious political death:** The official account of Horska’s murder is weakened by the context of KGB surveillance, repression and secrecy. Direct state responsibility is plausible but not proven by a surviving conclusive order or complete forensic record.

**National legend rather than recoverable fortune:** Polubotok’s British gold lacks contemporary documentary support. Its historical importance lies in what the story expressed about independence, dispossession and hoped-for restitution.

**Documented heritage dispute:** The Crimean treasures were real, catalogued objects whose legal custody was settled by the Dutch courts. The deeper issue concerns cultural ownership under occupation, not the physical existence of the collection.

## Why these mysteries still matter

These cases remain powerful because they concern more than missing bones, an unidentified killer or a legendary bank account. Each asks who had the authority to record the past and whose version was allowed to survive.

The Yaroslav and Khmelnytsky mysteries show the fragility of physical links to foundational rulers. The Beilis affair demonstrates how prejudice can hijack a criminal investigation. Horska’s death shows why official conclusions from a secretive political system cannot always be accepted without examining the institutions behind them. Polubotok’s gold reveals how folklore can turn historical loss into a promise of future restoration. Disputes over museum objects show that control of heritage remains inseparable from control of territory.

The most useful approach is neither automatic belief nor automatic dismissal. Ukraine’s archival history provides real reasons for suspicion, but it also demands disciplined standards of proof. Some answers may still emerge from newly opened files, scientific analysis or archaeological work. Others may remain permanently beyond recovery because the crucial evidence was destroyed, displaced or deliberately concealed. That uncertainty is not an invitation to invent conspiracies. It is part of the history itself.
