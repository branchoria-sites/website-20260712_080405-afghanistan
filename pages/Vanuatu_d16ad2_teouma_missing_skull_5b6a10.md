## Introduction

The mystery of Teouma Cemetery is not that archaeologists found an ancient graveyard, but that many of its dead were deliberately altered after burial. Discovered on the island of Efate in [Vanuatu]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-vanuatus/' | relative_url }}), Teouma is the oldest known large Lapita cemetery in the Pacific, dating to around 3,000 years ago. Instead of complete skeletons, researchers uncovered numerous burials in which skulls had been removed, rearranged or replaced by the skulls of other people. These discoveries provide some of the clearest evidence yet found for complex funerary rituals among the first settlers of Remote Oceania, but they also raise questions that remain unresolved. Why were heads removed? Why were some placed on the bodies of different individuals? And what do these unusual burials reveal about the beliefs of the people who carried them out? The archaeological evidence is exceptionally rich, yet the meaning behind these practices is still debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440314002441" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radiocarbon dating of burials from the Teouma Lapita cemetery, Efate, Vanuatu - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## The discovery of the Lapita cemetery

Teouma was discovered in 2004 during quarrying on Efate, just outside Port Vila. Rescue excavations quickly revealed that the site was extraordinary. Archaeologists uncovered dozens of burials associated with Lapita pottery, making it the earliest and largest cemetery yet identified from the Lapita cultural horizon, whose people were responsible for the first permanent settlement of Vanuatu and much of Remote Oceania.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.univ.edu.vu/en/search/88-humanities-social-sciences/6839" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.univ.edu.vu">[NUV Digital Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.univ.edu.vu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NUV Digital Research Portal Search</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NUV Digital Research PortalSearch - NUV Digital Research Portal...</span></span></span>

Radiocarbon dating places the cemetery at approximately 3000 years before present, during the earliest phase of human occupation in Vanuatu. Before Teouma, relatively few skeletons from this crucial period had survived anywhere in the Pacific. The discovery increased the available sample of early Lapita human remains dramatically, allowing archaeologists, biological anthropologists and genetic researchers to investigate not only who these settlers were, but also how they treated their dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440314002441" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radiocarbon dating of burials from the Teouma Lapita cemetery, Efate, Vanuatu - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Unlike many prehistoric cemeteries, Teouma preserves repeated patterns rather than isolated oddities. The unusual treatment of skulls appears often enough that archaeologists regard it as an intentional mortuary tradition rather than the result of accidental disturbance or later damage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/lapita-burial-practices-evidence-for-complex-body-and-bone-treatm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[The Australian National University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

## How the bodies and skulls were rearranged

The mystery centres on what happened after the initial burials.

Detailed analysis shows that many individuals were first buried normally in pits or wrapped containers that allowed the bodies to decompose without being immediately covered by compact soil. After decomposition, relatives or community members appear to have reopened many graves and removed the skull together with other upper-body bones before re-arranging them or placing them elsewhere. This sequence can be reconstructed from the positions of joints, the state of bone articulation and the surrounding sediments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/lapita-burial-practices-evidence-for-complex-body-and-bone-treatm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[The Australian National University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

Some of the most striking discoveries include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Skeletons whose skulls were completely absent.
* Burials in which several skulls had been carefully placed together.
* Individuals whose own skull had been replaced by that of another person.
* Graves containing skulls arranged on the chest or beside the body rather than in anatomical position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/lapita-burial-practices-evidence-for-complex-body-and-bone-treatm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[The Australian National University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

</div>

One widely discussed burial involved an elderly man's headless skeleton with three separate skulls deliberately positioned on his chest. Osteological analysis showed that the skulls belonged to different individuals rather than representing accidental mixing. Although dramatic, archaeologists caution that it should not be interpreted in isolation; it forms part of a broader pattern of deliberate secondary burial practices across the cemetery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/lapita-burial-practices-evidence-for-complex-body-and-bone-treatm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[The Australian National University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

The evidence also indicates that not every burial was treated identically. Some skeletons remained largely intact, while others experienced extensive rearrangement, suggesting that ritual treatment may have varied according to age, family tradition, social standing or circumstances that are now impossible to reconstruct with confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/lapita-burial-practices-evidence-for-complex-body-and-bone-treatm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[The Australian National University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

## Why were the skulls removed?

This remains the cemetery's central unresolved question.

The strongest evidence indicates that skull removal formed part of a planned sequence of funerary rites rather than violence, warfare or grave robbery. The bones show no consistent signs that heads were removed at the time of death. Instead, the bodies had already decomposed before the skulls were taken, implying deliberate exhumation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/lapita-burial-practices-evidence-for-complex-body-and-bone-treatm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[The Australian National University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

Several explanations have been proposed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Ancestor veneration.** In many societies, the skull is viewed as the seat of identity or ancestral power. Removing and preserving heads could have allowed ancestors to remain present within the community.
* **Secondary burial rituals.** The removal and later reburial of selected bones is documented ethnographically in various parts of Oceania. Teouma may represent an early form of this broader tradition.
* **Kinship or social relationships.** The placement of different individuals' skulls together may have symbolised family ties, marriage alliances or community membership rather than biological relationships.
* **Status differences.** The variation between graves raises the possibility that certain individuals received distinctive mortuary treatment because of age, prestige or ritual importance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/lapita-burial-practices-evidence-for-complex-body-and-bone-treatm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[The Australian National University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

</div>

None of these explanations has been conclusively proven. Archaeologists emphasise that Teouma predates written history by nearly three millennia, leaving interpretation dependent on physical evidence rather than direct testimony. Modern Pacific traditions may offer useful comparisons, but they cannot simply be projected backwards unchanged across thousands of years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/lapita-burial-practices-evidence-for-complex-body-and-bone-treatm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[The Australian National University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

## What the burials reveal about early Pacific settlers

The mystery of the missing skulls is only one reason Teouma has transformed understanding of Pacific prehistory.

The cemetery demonstrates that the first Lapita settlers practised elaborate funerary ceremonies rather than simple disposal of the dead. The reopening of graves required people to remember burial locations, return after decomposition and conduct carefully organised rituals involving selected bones. Such practices point to enduring social bonds and sophisticated ceremonial traditions among communities that had only recently established themselves in Remote Oceania.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/lapita-burial-practices-evidence-for-complex-body-and-bone-treatm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[The Australian National University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

Scientific studies have also used the cemetery to investigate the origins of these settlers. Isotopic analyses suggest that while most individuals grew up locally or nearby, a small number may have spent their childhood elsewhere, reflecting movement during the Lapita expansion. Craniofacial research has likewise contributed to debates over the ancestry of the first Pacific colonists and their relationship to later Polynesian populations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/lapita-migrants-in-the-pacifics-oldest-cemetery-isotopic-analysis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research-information.bris.ac.uk">[University of Bristol]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research-information.bris.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of BristolLapita migrants in the Pacific&#x27;s oldest cemetery: Isotopic analysis at Teouma, Vanuatu - University of BristolOctobe...</span></span></span>

Teouma has revealed further surprises beyond the missing heads. Researchers identified what appears to be the earliest known Lapita cremation, showing that funerary practices within the same community could vary considerably. Rather than representing a single rigid ritual, the cemetery preserves evidence for multiple ways of treating the dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305440309004385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identification of the first reported Lapita cremation in the Pacific Islands using archaeological, forensic and contemporary...</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery endures

The physical evidence from Teouma is unusually clear, yet its meaning remains partly hidden.

Archaeologists can reconstruct the sequence of burial, decomposition, exhumation and skull relocation with considerable confidence. What they cannot determine is the symbolic significance behind those actions. Whether the missing skulls represented ancestor worship, family remembrance, social identity or beliefs now lost to history remains unknown.

That uncertainty is precisely what makes Teouma one of Vanuatu's most compelling historical mysteries. Unlike legends that depend on folklore alone, the puzzle rests on tangible archaeological evidence that can be examined repeatedly as new techniques emerge. Every advance in dating, skeletal analysis, ancient DNA and isotope research has improved understanding of who these people were, yet the deepest question—why they chose to separate the heads of their dead—continues to invite careful interpretation rather than definitive answers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440314002441" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radiocarbon dating of burials from the Teouma Lapita cemetery, Efate, Vanuatu - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>
