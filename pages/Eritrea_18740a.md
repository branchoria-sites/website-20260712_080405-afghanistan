## Why did Adulis disappear?

Adulis was one of the most important ports of the ancient Red Sea. Situated near the Gulf of Zula, it connected the kingdom of Aksum and the African interior with Arabia, the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean. Imported ceramics, coins, marble and monumental church remains show that it was not an isolated coastal settlement but a cosmopolitan trading centre tied into long-distance commercial and religious networks. Recent radiocarbon work dates two excavated churches mainly to the sixth and early seventh centuries, when the city was still wealthy enough to use imported architectural materials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/an-archaeology-of-conversion-evidence-from-adulis-for-early-christianity-and-religious-transition-in-the-horn-of-africa/03A2348242869346ED43E5B91B44D064" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment An archaeology of conversion?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evidence from Adulis for...by G Castiglia · 2022 · Cited by 10 — This article focuses on the excavation of two of the three known early...</span></span></span>

Yet after the early seventh century, Adulis largely disappears from the archaeological and written record. There is no securely documented single catastrophe that explains its end. This absence has encouraged several competing explanations.

One possibility is **war or deliberate destruction**. Ancient Red Sea politics were violent, and Aksum was involved in military campaigns across the water in southern Arabia. Later writers and popular accounts have sometimes imagined Adulis being abruptly sacked. Archaeology, however, has not yet established a clear destruction layer proving that one attack ended the city.

A second explanation is **the reorganisation of Red Sea trade**. Aksum’s overseas power weakened during late antiquity, while new political and commercial centres emerged after the rise of Islam. Historians have long connected Aksum’s decline with the loss or redirection of maritime trade, although current scholarship treats this as part of a complicated regional transition rather than a simple story in which Muslim expansion suddenly closed the sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/ethiopia-and-the-world-3301500-ce/9050009D75ED529C859364EE3308EF0B" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment&#x27;Ethiopia&#x27; and the World, 330–1500 CE11 May 2024 — The PES mentions Aksum in the context of discus...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2024</span></span></span>

Environmental change may also have mattered. Adulis stood inland from its harbour, and the usefulness of the port depended on navigable channels, reliable water and access between coast and settlement. Silting, shoreline movement or changing river courses could have made the harbour progressively less practical. These processes can ruin a port without leaving the dramatic traces of a conquest.

The best-supported conclusion is therefore cumulative: Adulis probably declined through some combination of political fragmentation, shifting trade routes and local environmental change. Archaeological investigations have established occupation reaching back into the second millennium BC and a substantial Aksumite city flourishing into the sixth or early seventh century, but the final phases remain poorly understood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/RedSea/Adulis-Zazzaro2014.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ancientportsantiques.com">[Ancient Ports Catalogue]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ancientportsantiques.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">At the begin-.Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ancient Ports CatalogueTowards a Chronology of the Eritrean Red Sea Port of...June 12, 2014 — 12 Jun 2014 — The Eritrean coastal site of...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 12, 2014</span></span></span>

Adulis is also an archival mystery. British excavators removed architectural pieces, sculptures and coins in 1868, and further Italian campaigns followed in the early twentieth century. Objects from the site now survive in overseas collections, including the British Museum, while old field records must be reconciled with modern excavations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/search?keyword=adulis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishmuseum.org.</span></span></span> The mystery is therefore not that Adulis was “lost” in the romantic sense. Its location is known. What remains lost is a reliable account of how one of the Red Sea’s great ports ceased to function.

## Was Qohaito really the ancient city of Koloe?

High on a plateau in southern Eritrea, Qohaito contains dense remains of buildings, dams, roads, tombs, rock art and cultivated land. UNESCO’s tentative-list description presents it as a large cultural landscape, approximately 32 square kilometres in area, with major settlement particularly during the second to seventh centuries AD. It appears to have occupied a strategic position between Adulis and the highland centres of the Aksumite world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5600/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The central puzzle is whether Qohaito was **Koloe**, a market settlement mentioned in ancient geographical and commercial writing. The identification makes sense geographically. A trading station on the route from Adulis into the highlands would fit both the written description and Qohaito’s imposing ruins.

It remains an identification rather than a proved fact. No inscription found at the site has definitively declared, in effect, “this is Koloe”. Earlier investigators sometimes interpreted the ruins through Greek or Mediterranean assumptions, while later surveys placed them more firmly within the regional cultures of the Eritrean and Ethiopian highlands. Even modern archaeological literature generally says Qohaito has been “suggested” as Koloe rather than treating the matter as settled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/86865462/Archaeological_survey_in_the_Adi_Qeyeh_area_Eritrea" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Archaeological survey in the Adi Qeyeh area, Eritrea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) Archaeological survey in the Adi Qeyeh area, Eritrea</span></span></span>

Several other questions remain open. The plateau includes structures from different periods, but much of the settlement has not been excavated deeply enough to establish how the city developed. The age and purpose of the Sahira Dam, the function of prominent monumental buildings and the relationship between urban areas, farming and surrounding villages all require further work. Qohaito may have been a compact city, a dispersed urban landscape or a sequence of settlements that expanded and contracted over centuries.

Claims that it served as an Aksumite “summer capital” are repeated in tourism and official popular accounts, but firm archaeological proof is lacking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shabait.com/2012/09/07/qohaito/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shabait.com">[Eritrea Ministry Of Information]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shabait.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eritrea Ministry Of Information QOHAITO – Eritrea Ministry Of Information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eritrea Ministry Of Information QOHAITO – Eritrea Ministry Of Information</span></span></span> Likewise, the survival of older rock art near later monumental remains does not demonstrate one unbroken civilisation occupying the plateau in the same form for thousands of years.

The most plausible interpretation is less spectacular but more interesting. Qohaito was probably a major highland centre whose fortunes depended on trade, water engineering and the Adulis–Aksum route. Whether its ancient inhabitants called it Koloe can be resolved only by stronger inscriptional evidence or excavations that connect the material remains more precisely with the chronology described in written sources.

## What do the Dahlak tombstones reveal?

The [Dahlak]({{ 'dahlak/' | relative_url }}) Archipelago, off Eritrea’s Red Sea coast, preserves the remains of a medieval Muslim trading society that is far less widely known than Adulis or Aksum. On Dahlak Kebir, archaeologists have recorded ruined coral-stone buildings, water cisterns, settlement mounds and hundreds of Arabic funerary inscriptions. These remains document a community linked to maritime commerce between north-east Africa, Arabia and the wider Indian Ocean world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/RedSea/Dahlak-Insoll2001.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ancientportsantiques.com">[Ancient Ports Catalogue]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ancientportsantiques.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ancientportsantiques.com.</span></span></span>

The tombstones are unusually valuable because written narrative sources for the Dahlak Sultanate are sparse. Names, dates, titles and places of origin preserved in stone provide fragments of biographies that would otherwise have disappeared. One inscription dated to 1093, for example, refers to a ruler named Sultan al-Mubarak. Archaeological research has identified more than 200 inscriptions covering several centuries, but they do not form a continuous political history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/RedSea/Dahlak-Insoll2001.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ancientportsantiques.com">[Ancient Ports Catalogue]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ancientportsantiques.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ancientportsantiques.com.</span></span></span>

This creates several linked mysteries. How powerful were the rulers of Dahlak? Did they control only the islands, or also sections of the mainland coast? How independent were they from Aksumite successors, highland rulers, Yemen and later Ottoman authorities? When did local coinage begin, and what does it reveal about the sultanate’s ability to tax or regulate trade?

The physical remains also raise practical questions. Fresh water is scarce on the islands, yet the medieval settlement supported a sizeable population through carefully constructed cisterns. Understanding when these systems were built and maintained could help reconstruct the scale and duration of occupation. Changes in shipping routes may explain the settlement’s eventual contraction, but political pressure and environmental limits probably contributed as well.

The Dahlak mystery is therefore not a vanished treasure waiting to be found. It is an incomplete archive carved into funerary stones. Individual inscriptions offer striking precision, but the political world surrounding them must be reconstructed from disconnected names, ruined buildings, imported objects and references in sources written elsewhere.

## What remains hidden in Eritrea’s manuscript collections?

Eritrea has a long tradition of parchment manuscript production associated with monasteries and churches. Some religious houses preserve substantial libraries containing biblical, liturgical, theological, historical and legal works. Scholars have described these collections as exceptionally important but insufficiently catalogued, meaning that their full contents, dates and relationships to other manuscript traditions remain uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/37656783/Monastic_Libraries_in_Eritrea_An_Agenda_for_the_Future" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Monastic Libraries in Eritrea: An Agenda for the Future</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) Monastic Libraries in Eritrea: An Agenda for the Future</span></span></span>

The word “hidden” can be misleading. The manuscripts are not necessarily secret, coded or deliberately concealed. Many remain little known because monasteries are remote, access is limited, preservation is difficult and comprehensive scholarly catalogues have not been completed. A manuscript may be familiar to the community that safeguards it while remaining effectively invisible to international researchers.

The principal puzzles concern provenance and chronology. A manuscript’s final pages may be damaged or missing, removing the note in which a scribe recorded when, where and for whom it was copied. Later repairs can combine leaves from different periods. Texts may preserve local versions that differ from better-known copies elsewhere, but without systematic comparison it is difficult to tell whether a variation is ancient, regional or simply the work of one scribe.

Political borders complicate the subject. Eritrea’s Christian manuscript heritage developed within cultural and religious networks extending across the northern Horn of Africa. Modern claims that every text or artistic style belongs exclusively to one present-day nation can distort a history created by mobile monks, patrons, scribes and trading communities.

There is no solid public evidence for sensational claims that Eritrean monasteries contain a single forbidden book capable of overturning established history. The real prospect is more credible: systematic cataloguing may identify unknown copies, unusual textual variants, ownership notes or local historical records. Such discoveries would refine the history of religion, literacy and community life rather than reveal an all-encompassing ancient secret.

## Where are the people detained in September 2001?

Eritrea’s gravest unresolved mystery concerns people rather than ruins. In September 2001, the government detained prominent political reformers, including former senior officials commonly known as the G-15, after they criticised President Isaias Afwerki and called for constitutional government. At roughly the same time, the authorities closed the independent press and arrested journalists, editors and writers. Contemporary reporting documented at least nine journalist arrests within days of the shutdown; further cases were confirmed later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cpj.org/africa/eritrea/2001/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cpj.org">[Committee to Protect Journalists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cpj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Committee to Protect Journalists Eritrea Archives New York,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Committee to Protect Journalists Eritrea Archives New York,</span></span></span>

The detainees were not given public trials, and their families were denied normal access. Over the following years, defectors and former detainees offered accounts of secret prisons, harsh conditions and alleged deaths in custody. Much of that testimony is difficult to verify independently because Eritrea has not permitted transparent inspections or released complete official records.

This distinction matters. It is well established that the arrests occurred and that the authorities held the detainees. It is also well established that no credible public judicial process followed. What remains uncertain is the fate of each individual.

## What these mysteries say about Eritrea’s past

Eritrea’s enduring enigmas arise from several different kinds of silence. Adulis fell silent when its trade and urban life ended. Qohaito remains partly unread because excavation has not yet matched the scale of the landscape. Dahlak Kebir speaks through inscriptions, but only in fragments. Monastic libraries preserve evidence that has not been fully catalogued. The disappeared of modern Eritrea are absent because information about them has been deliberately restricted.

These silences should not be treated as interchangeable. Archaeological uncertainty invites competing interpretations; it does not justify fantasies about erased super-civilisations. Fragmentary medieval records allow careful reconstruction; they do not prove a vast forgotten empire. Reports of modern prisoners require caution about unverified details, but caution must not obscure the documented arrests and continuing failure to account for those detained.

The strongest explanations are usually cumulative rather than dramatic. Ports decline through environmental and commercial change. Cities acquire several identities over centuries. Political histories survive unevenly in tombstones and manuscripts. Authoritarian secrecy turns known arrests into prolonged disappearances.

What would resolve these cases is equally concrete: sustained excavation at Adulis and Qohaito, full publication of field data, preservation and study of the Dahlak inscriptions, collaborative cataloguing of manuscript collections, access to prison records and truthful accounting for the missing. Until then, Eritrea’s mysteries remain important not because they are exotic, but because each marks a boundary between what the surviving evidence can establish and what history has not yet yielded.
