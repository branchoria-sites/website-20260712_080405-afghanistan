## Did prehistoric Serbia produce Europe’s first writing?

Small incised signs found on pottery, figurines and other objects associated with the Vinča culture have generated one of Europe’s longest-running archaeological arguments. The culture flourished across parts of present-day Serbia and neighbouring regions during the Neolithic period. Excavations led by archaeologist Miloje Vasić at Vinča, beside the Danube near Belgrade, uncovered many of the marked objects in the early twentieth century, although related signs had already been found elsewhere in the Balkans.

Because the signs are several thousand years older than the earliest generally accepted writing systems of Mesopotamia, they are sometimes presented as evidence that writing was invented in south-eastern Europe first. Popular accounts call them the “Vinča script” or “Danube script”, and attempts have been made to interpret them as religious statements, ownership marks or fragments of an unknown language.

The secure facts are more limited. The signs are real, ancient and repeated across a wide geographical area. Some occur singly, particularly on pottery, while shorter groups appear on other objects. Their makers were clearly using a shared symbolic tradition rather than making random scratches. What has not been demonstrated is that the symbols encoded spoken language, which is the usual dividing line between writing and other forms of visual communication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vin%C4%8Da_symbols" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Vinča symbols</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Vinča symbols</span></span></span>

The brevity of the inscriptions is a major obstacle. There is no long text, bilingual inscription or clearly recognisable sequence comparable with an administrative list, dedication or sentence. Statistical patterns alone cannot settle the matter because non-linguistic systems—including emblems, heraldry and religious symbols—can also display repetition and structured combinations. Linguist Richard Sproat’s comparative work has specifically treated the Vinča material as a non-linguistic sign system and cautioned against using apparently language-like statistics as proof of writing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://richardsproat.com/newindex/science_main.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: richardsproat.com">[richardsproat.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">richardsproat.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Written Language vs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Non-Linguistic Symbol SystemsMay 22, 2013 — by R Sproat —... Vinca symbols (non-linguistic). Note. 6 that the Pictish symbols are classi...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 22, 2013</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusion is that the Vinča signs formed an important system of symbolic communication, perhaps marking ownership, identity, ritual meaning, contents or production. Calling them “meaningless decoration” understates their consistency, but calling them a decipherable script goes beyond the surviving evidence. The mystery endures because the signs sit close to the boundary between pictures, codes and writing—and because proving that Europe possessed the world’s earliest script would radically change a familiar story about the origins of civilisation.

## What caused the Smederevo explosion?

On 5 June 1941, only weeks after Axis forces occupied Yugoslavia, a vast store of captured ammunition exploded inside Smederevo Fortress on the Danube. The blast devastated much of the lower town, wrecked buildings and destroyed a crowded passenger train standing near the fortress. Smederevo was unusually busy: it was a market day, pupils had come to collect school documents, refugees and officials were present, and the railway station was full. The explosion occurred at about 2.14 pm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smederevowelcome.com/discover-smederevo/june-5th-1941-explosion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smederevowelcome.com">[SmederevoWelcome]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smederevowelcome.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">june 5th 1941 explosion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">June 5th 1941 Explosion &#124; SmederevoWelcome...</span></span></span>

The exact death toll has never been established. Estimates range from several hundred to around 2,500, partly because bodies were badly damaged, visitors were difficult to identify and wartime record-keeping was incomplete. Later commemorative accounts sometimes repeat the highest figures as certain, but the disagreement itself is an essential part of the mystery. What is beyond dispute is that the German occupation authorities had concentrated an enormous and dangerous mixture of ammunition, explosives and fuel beside a populated town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smederevo_Fortress_explosion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smederevo Fortress explosion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smederevo Fortress explosion</span></span></span>

Witnesses described smoke and smaller detonations before the main explosion, suggesting that a fire spread through the depot before reaching the largest stores. Several explanations emerged:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **An accidental ignition.** Leaking gunpowder may have dried on routes used to move ammunition, allowing a cigarette, match, spark or gunshot to begin a spreading fire.
* **Negligence inside the depot.** Poorly organised storage and the mixture of volatile materials made a chain reaction possible without deliberate sabotage.
* **Resistance sabotage.** Later stories accused an unnamed patriot or the communist operative Mustafa Golubić of destroying matériel intended for Germany.
* **An air attack.** Some witnesses reported an aircraft or falling incendiary objects, although no convincing operational evidence has established a British attack.

</div>

The accident theory requires the fewest unsupported assumptions. Contemporary descriptions of smoke appearing before the great blast fit a fire spreading through exposed propellant, while the dangerous storage arrangements created obvious opportunities for ignition. The sabotage theories are possible in the abstract, but no verified order, confession, forensic finding or reliable eyewitness account identifies a saboteur. The aircraft theory is weaker still.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smederevowelcome.com/discover-smederevo/june-5th-1941-explosion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smederevowelcome.com">[SmederevoWelcome]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smederevowelcome.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">june 5th 1941 explosion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">June 5th 1941 Explosion &#124; SmederevoWelcome...</span></span></span>

Smederevo remains unresolved because the site was immediately transformed into a rescue zone under occupation, rather than preserved for an independent investigation. Evidence was scattered or destroyed by the blast, and later political systems had reasons to promote different explanations. Occupation authorities could minimise negligence; resistance narratives could elevate sabotage; post-war storytellers could connect the event to famous clandestine figures. The most likely answer is a preventable depot fire, but the missing ignition point has left room for every political generation to imagine its own culprit.

## Who lies beneath the Monument to the Unknown Hero?

The Monument to the [Unknown Hero]({{ 'unknown-hero/' | relative_url }}) stands on Mount Avala south of Belgrade. Designed by Ivan Meštrović and completed in 1938, it commemorates those killed in the Balkan Wars and the First World War. Beneath it are the remains of a soldier found after the fighting for Avala in October 1915, when Serbian forces resisted advancing German and Austro-Hungarian troops.

The earliest grave reportedly carried a German inscription identifying the dead man simply as an unknown Serbian soldier. When a commission examined the remains in 1921, it found no identity tag. Equipment recovered with the body—including ammunition and boots—suggested Serbian military service, while examination of the skeleton indicated a young, lightly built man. The commission reburied him in a local memorial, and the remains were moved again when the present monument was constructed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Unknown_Hero" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span></span></span>

The soldier’s anonymity invited attempts to give him a name. One recurring claim identifies him as Sulejman Balić, a Muslim soldier from the Sandžak region who served in the Serbian army. Other versions describe a much older man or attach the grave to different units. None has been confirmed by a surviving identity disc, service file, contemporary burial record or modern forensic identification. Some versions also conflict with the 1921 description of the skeleton.

The mystery is therefore real but narrower than later legend suggests. There is good evidence that the burial contained a Serbian serviceman killed during the 1915 battle. There is no reliable evidence establishing his name. The missing personal objects deepen the problem: items reportedly transferred to official custody later disappeared, removing material that might have helped reconstruct his identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Unknown_Hero" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span></span></span>

A second controversy concerns what was destroyed to create the memorial. King Alexander ordered the demolition of Žrnov, a medieval fortified site occupying Avala’s summit. The decision was criticised at the time, notably by the writer Branislav Nušić, and its full motivation remains debated. Practical and symbolic explanations are sufficient: the king wanted a commanding Yugoslav memorial, and the older ruins occupied the chosen location. Later stories involving secret chambers, occult geometry or foreign plots lack comparable evidence. Academic analysis instead treats the project as a deliberate redefinition of Avala, replacing an older historical landmark with a new state monument centred on sacrifice and Yugoslav unity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Unknown_Hero" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monument to the Unknown Hero</span></span></span>

The unknown soldier matters precisely because anonymity was transformed into symbolism. His individual life disappeared, while his grave became a national space onto which changing ideas of Serbia and Yugoslavia could be projected.

## What happened to Đorđe Martinović?

On 1 May 1985, Đorđe Martinović, a Serbian farmer from Kosovo, was admitted to hospital with severe internal injuries caused by a broken bottle. He initially said that two Albanian men had attacked him in a field. Investigators later reported that he had admitted causing the injury himself during a sexual act, although he subsequently withdrew or disputed that account.

Medical opinions conflicted. One team at the Military Medical Academy in Belgrade concluded that the injury was unlikely to have been self-inflicted and suggested that more than one attacker would have been required. A later forensic assessment argued that Martinović could have caused it himself by placing the bottle on a support and falling or lowering his weight onto it. Yugoslav security bodies reportedly favoured the self-infliction explanation, but the authorities did not produce a publicly persuasive reconstruction that ended the argument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90or%C4%91e_Martinovi%C4%87_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Đorđe Martinović incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Đorđe Martinović incident</span></span></span>

The central facts were soon overwhelmed by politics. Serbian nationalists treated Martinović’s account as evidence that Kosovo’s Albanian majority was terrorising local Serbs and that federal authorities were suppressing the truth. Albanian commentators and defenders of the Yugoslav system regarded the assault story as an inflammatory fabrication. The incident was discussed in political institutions, circulated through newspapers and cultural works, and converted from an unresolved forensic case into a symbol of collective victimhood. Scholarly accounts of late Yugoslavia identify the affair as an important episode in the revival of Serbian nationalist mobilisation during the 1980s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.openedition.org/ceup/856?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.openedition.org">[OpenEdition Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.openedition.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Open Edition Booksbooks.openedition.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open Edition Booksbooks.openedition.org</span></span></span>

It is not possible to establish from the public record whether Martinović was assaulted or injured himself. Both main theories contain difficulties. A self-inflicted injury was physically possible according to one forensic reconstruction, but alleged confessions obtained during an intensely political investigation cannot simply be accepted without question. The assault claim was supported by another medical team’s interpretation, yet no attackers were identified and no independent evidence placed two men at the scene.

The lesson of the case lies less in choosing a medically satisfying answer than in understanding what happened to uncertainty. A private injury became a national allegory before the forensic dispute had been resolved. Later violence in Kosovo has encouraged retrospective certainty on both sides, but subsequent events do not prove what occurred in that field in 1985. Treating the case responsibly means preserving the distinction between an unresolved incident and the powerful political story built around it.

## Where was Draža Mihailović buried?

Dragoljub “Draža” Mihailović led the royalist Chetnik movement during the Second World War, a force whose record included resistance to Axis occupation, conflict with the communist Partisans, tactical collaboration with occupying forces and atrocities against civilians. Captured after the war, he was tried by the new Yugoslav authorities, convicted of treason and war crimes, and executed on 17 July 1946.

The place of execution and burial was kept secret. No publicly available contemporary record gives a conclusive location, and his family did not receive the body. Decades later, Serbian investigations concentrated on Ada Ciganlija in Belgrade, near the site of a former prison. A state commission concluded that this was probably the primary burial area, while acknowledging indications that the remains might subsequently have been moved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vreme.com/en/projekat/draza-sahranjen-na-adi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vreme.com">[Vreme]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vreme.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Draža buried on Ada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Draža buried on Ada</span></span></span>

The commission’s problem was not merely the passage of time. It reported finding no formal execution record and no direct archival statement identifying precisely where Mihailović was killed or buried. Its conclusion rested on indirect testimony and surviving documents. Investigators also believed that relevant material had been destroyed or removed from archives during the communist period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vreme.com/en/projekat/draza-sahranjen-na-adi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vreme.com">[Vreme]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vreme.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Draža buried on Ada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Draža buried on Ada</span></span></span>

Human bones found near the suspected site prompted intense media attention, but their discovery did not establish that they belonged to Mihailović. Secret burial grounds from the immediate post-war period existed in and around Belgrade, meaning that unidentified remains in the area could belong to other executed prisoners. Without a secure archaeological context and a reliable genetic comparison, proximity alone is insufficient.

The missing grave is inseparable from Serbia’s argument over Mihailović himself. Admirers regard him as an anti-fascist and victim of a political trial; critics point to extensive evidence of Chetnik collaboration and mass violence. Serbia’s courts annulled his conviction in 2015 because of procedural and political defects in the trial, but that legal rehabilitation did not amount to a historical finding that every wartime accusation was false. The physical search for the grave has consequently become entangled with demands for moral vindication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dra%C5%BEa_Mihailovi%C4%87" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Draža Mihailović</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Draža Mihailović</span></span></span>

The likeliest reconstruction is that Mihailović was executed in or near Belgrade and initially buried secretly in the Ada Ciganlija area. Whether his remains are still there, were destroyed or were transferred cannot yet be demonstrated. A credible resolution would require controlled excavation, documented chain of custody and forensic identification—not another anonymous bone, second-hand recollection or politically convenient announcement.

## Why these mysteries endure

Serbia’s major historical enigmas survive for different reasons. The Vinča signs are difficult because their language, if they encoded one at all, left no readable key. The [Smederevo blast]({{ 'smederevo-blast/' | relative_url }}) destroyed its own evidence. The Avala soldier was deliberately chosen to represent the unidentified dead. The Martinović affair was politicised faster than it could be investigated. Mihailović’s burial was concealed by a state that intended the location to remain unknown.

These cases also show several recurring warning signs:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Absence of evidence becomes evidence of conspiracy.** Missing records may reflect secrecy, wartime destruction, poor administration or later neglect rather than a perfectly coordinated cover-up.
* **Symbolic importance overwhelms the original question.** The identity of one soldier or the circumstances of one injury can become entangled with claims about an entire nation.
* **Possibility is confused with probability.** Sabotage at Smederevo or linguistic writing at Vinča cannot be ruled out completely, but neither is proved merely because it remains possible.
* **Later politics reshapes older uncertainty.** Communist secrecy, Serbian nationalism, Yugoslav state-building and post-Yugoslav memory have each influenced which explanations gained attention.

</div>

The best evidence does not eliminate mystery from Serbia’s past. It defines its proper boundaries. Some questions may eventually be answered by excavation, forensic testing or newly opened archives. Others may remain permanently unresolved because the decisive material has vanished. In both situations, the historically responsible approach is the same: begin with what can be documented, separate interpretation from fact, and resist turning every gap in the record into a grand design.
