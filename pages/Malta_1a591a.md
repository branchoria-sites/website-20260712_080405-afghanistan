## Who made Malta’s cart ruts?

Across Malta and Gozo, pairs of parallel grooves run through exposed limestone. They divide, intersect and sometimes form dense networks, most famously at Misraħ Għar il-Kbir. Their resemblance to railway tracks earned that site the informal English nickname “Clapham Junction”, although the grooves pre-date railways by centuries or millennia.

The basic facts are clear. The tracks occur at numerous sites, their dimensions show a broad degree of standardisation, and many were worn or cut deeply into the bedrock. Their distribution suggests repeated movement along established routes rather than decorative carving. Yet archaeologists still disagree over precisely when they were made, what travelled along them and whether all examples belong to one period. A detailed morphological study found enough consistency to suggest shared methods of creation or use, but shape alone cannot provide a secure date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X21004995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This suggests a coherence to them in terms of age, function, and modification.Read more...</span></span></span>

The most plausible explanation is that the grooves resulted from vehicles, sledges or wheeled platforms repeatedly carrying heavy loads. Softer limestone could have been worn down by sustained traffic, especially when wet. Some researchers have linked the tracks to quarrying, arguing that stone, soil or agricultural produce was moved between extraction areas, settlements and fields. Others have proposed that at least some grooves were deliberately cut to guide vehicles over difficult ground.

Dating is the central problem. Cart ruts are difficult to date directly because the grooves themselves rarely contain organic material suitable for scientific testing. Relationships with nearby tombs, quarries, pottery and buildings have produced proposed dates ranging from prehistory to the Roman and later periods. Recent reassessment has argued that some examples can be associated with the classical era, challenging the traditional assumption that the whole system is prehistoric. That may mean “the cart ruts” are not one network at all, but a type of transport feature created at different times.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6853483" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: papers.ssrn.com">[SSRN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">papers.ssrn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Chronology of the Maltese Cart Ruts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Chronology of the Maltese Cart Ruts - Joel Grimaby J Grima — A critical evaluation of all available dating evidence confirms that...</span></span></span>

Claims that tracks continue beneath the sea or end inexplicably at cliffs have encouraged theories involving submerged civilisations, astronomical alignments and even extraterrestrials. Such ideas add little explanatory value. Coastlines erode, cliffs retreat, sea levels change and later construction destroys connecting sections. A route that now appears to lead nowhere may once have ended at a quarry, field, landing place or settlement that no longer survives.

The honest conclusion is less spectacular but more useful: the grooves were probably related to transport, but their exact machinery, dates and economic purposes remain uncertain. The mystery survives because the vehicles, goods and communities that made the tracks left no written explanation.

## Why did the temple-building society disappear?

Between roughly the fourth and third millennia BC, Malta supported an extraordinary monument-building culture. Its communities constructed large stone complexes, elaborate underground burial places and ceremonial spaces on islands with limited soil, fresh water and timber. Then, around the middle of the third millennium BC, the archaeological culture associated with these monuments came to an end.

The popular phrase “the [temple builders]({{ 'temple-builders/' | relative_url }}) vanished” can be misleading. Archaeology does not show an entire population disappearing in a single event. It shows a substantial break in material culture: monumental construction stopped, burial customs changed and later Bronze Age communities lived differently. Whether the earlier inhabitants died, migrated, became part of incoming groups or reorganised their society cannot yet be established with certainty.

Several explanations have been proposed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Environmental degradation:** intensive farming, grazing and woodland clearance may have reduced soil fertility and increased erosion.
* **Prolonged dryness:** reduced rainfall would have placed severe pressure on crops and water supplies.
* **Social instability:** the labour and authority needed to maintain monumental centres may have broken down.
* **Disease or famine:** either could have weakened a small, relatively isolated population.
* **Migration or incoming groups:** later communities may have replaced, absorbed or displaced some of the existing population.

</div>

Environmental evidence makes a combined ecological crisis increasingly plausible. Studies of Maltese sediment and landscape history identify erosion and diminishing freshwater conditions near the end of the Temple Period. Researchers have also connected the transition with the wider “4.2-kiloyear event”, a period of climatic disruption affecting parts of the Mediterranean and neighbouring regions. However, the character and severity of that event varied by location, so it cannot simply be treated as a single drought that destroyed every society at the same time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/bitstream/123456789/87871/1/The_4_2_ka_event_and_the_end_of_the_Maltese_Temple_Period_2022.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: um.edu.mt">[L-Università ta&#x27; Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">um.edu.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">L-Università ta&#x27; MaltaThe 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”January 28, 2022 — by HS Groucutt · 2022 · Cited by 10 —...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 28, 2022</span></span></span>

There is no decisive evidence for a dramatic invasion or massacre. Nor does the archaeological record support the confident claim that an oppressive priesthood destroyed the society, although competition over religious or political authority remains possible. The strongest interpretation is cumulative: a population dependent on a restricted island environment faced worsening ecological conditions while its social system was already under strain. Monument building then ceased, communities changed or contracted, and later settlers introduced a different way of life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.771683/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frontiers The 4.2 ka Event and the End of the Maltese “Temple Period”</span></span></span>

The mystery matters because Malta’s monuments are easier to see than the society behind them. Stone survives; political structures, spoken beliefs and personal identities do not. The apparent “disappearance” is partly an illusion produced by the uneven nature of archaeological evidence.

## What is real about the Hypogeum legends?

The underground complex at Ħal Saflieni is one of Malta’s most remarkable prehistoric sites. Cut into limestone and used over a long period between approximately 4000 and 1500 BC, it contained burial chambers, architectural imitations carved in rock, painted decoration and the remains of thousands of people. Its original meanings are uncertain, but it was clearly associated with burial and ritual activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritagemalta.mt/explore/hal-saflieni-hypogeum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagemalta.mt">[Heritage Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagemalta.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hal saflieni hypogeum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">hal saflieni hypogeum</span></span></span>

Because the [Hypogeum]({{ 'hypogeum/' | relative_url }}) was found beneath a modern neighbourhood and contains dark passages unavailable to unrestricted exploration, it has attracted unusually persistent myths. Stories have circulated about missing children, secret tunnels extending across Malta, suppressed chambers and strange non-human skulls. The most famous claims concern supposedly elongated crania removed from public display.

There really were skulls from the Hypogeum, and the archaeologist Temi Zammit described some as belonging to a “long-headed” type. That older anthropological language was later transformed into claims of extreme deformity or non-human ancestry. Heritage Malta has stated that the skulls fall within normal human variation. Some were removed from exhibition during museum refurbishment in 1995, a routine curatorial decision that helped generate stories that evidence had been hidden.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritagemalta.mt/news/the-hypogeum-skulls-on-display-at-the-national-museum-of-archaeology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagemalta.mt">[Heritage Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagemalta.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heritagemalta.mt.</span></span></span>

The tale of children disappearing into underground passages is even less secure. It is generally repeated without names, a reliable contemporary report, an official inquiry or a recoverable list of victims. Its structure resembles a classic urban legend: a forbidden subterranean space, a group entering it and a warning that no one returned. Without documentary evidence, it should be treated as folklore rather than an unresolved missing-person case.

The Hypogeum does possess unusual acoustics. Particular chambers strongly reinforce certain frequencies, producing an impressive resonance when a low voice is used. Modern research has examined whether the effect may be partly intentional, suggesting that the builders shaped multiple surfaces in ways that influenced the sound. That possibility is scientifically interesting, but it does not prove claims about supernatural frequencies, altered consciousness or forgotten sound technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.13697" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The true mystery is cultural. Why were particular spaces carved to imitate above-ground architecture? Who was permitted to enter the deeper chambers? Did sound, darkness and collective burial form part of ceremonies connecting the living with ancestral remains? Archaeology can describe the structure and its contents, but the rituals performed there were never written down.

## How did Caravaggio escape Fort St Angelo?

The painter Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio arrived in Malta in 1607 while seeking protection and rehabilitation after killing a man in Rome. He gained the favour of Grand Master Alof de Wignacourt, produced major works for the Order of St John and was admitted as a knight. His recovery was brief.

In August 1608, Caravaggio became involved in a violent confrontation in which several knights were wounded. He was imprisoned at Fort St Angelo, the heavily defended stronghold overlooking the Grand Harbour. By early October he had escaped Malta and reached Sicily. The Order subsequently expelled him in absentia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/bitstream/123456789/108385/1/Caravaggio%20on%20the%20frontier%202014.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: um.edu.mt">[L-Università ta&#x27; Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">um.edu.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">L-Università ta&#x27; Malta Caravaggio on the Frontier</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">L-Università ta&#x27; Malta Caravaggio on the Frontier</span></span></span>

The documentary record establishes that the escape happened but does not fully explain how. On 6 October 1608, the Order’s officials reported his flight and began investigating. A prisoner could not casually walk out of Fort St Angelo, cross or enter the harbour and leave an island controlled by the same institution that had detained him. Some form of outside help, official negligence or deliberate complicity is therefore a reasonable inference, but the identity of any helper remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/bitstream/123456789/103777/1/Mid-Med%20Bank%20Limited%20Report%20and%20Accounts%201986_Caravaggio%20in%20Malta.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: um.edu.mt">[L-Università ta&#x27; Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">um.edu.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mid Med Bank Limited Report and Accounts 1986 Caravaggio in Malta</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mid Med Bank Limited Report and Accounts 1986 Caravaggio in Malta</span></span></span>

Caravaggio still possessed influential connections, and not every knight regarded him in the same way. His paintings had enhanced the prestige of the Order, while powerful patrons elsewhere continued to work for a papal pardon. It is possible that sympathetic individuals arranged a boat, bribed guards or ensured that supervision was weak. It is also possible that later retellings have exaggerated the physical difficulty of leaving the place where he was held.

What cannot be responsibly claimed is that a named senior figure certainly organised the escape. The surviving evidence does not reveal a complete plan. The puzzle endures because the Order recorded the embarrassing fact of Caravaggio’s flight without preserving—or perhaps without discovering—the full chain of assistance behind it.

The episode became part of the artist’s legend because it joins two different Caravaggios: the celebrated painter whose work still defines Malta’s artistic heritage and the unstable fugitive who repeatedly escaped the consequences of violence. Malta was both the high point of his attempt at respectability and the place where that attempt collapsed.

## What happened to HMS Urge?

HMS *Urge*, a British U-class submarine operating from Malta during the Second World War, disappeared in April 1942. It was believed to have sailed from Malta for Alexandria with its crew and additional passengers aboard, but no confirmed wreck was known for decades.

In the absence of physical evidence, competing accounts developed. The conventional explanation held that *Urge* struck a German mine shortly after leaving Malta. An alternative theory placed the submarine near Libya, where it was supposedly destroyed by Italian aircraft during a secret mission. The disagreement was not trivial. It concerned the final movements of the vessel, the reliability of wartime records and the resting place of those who died.

A remote-sensing survey located a submarine wreck outside Malta’s Grand Harbour in 2019. Divers from Heritage Malta and the University of Malta examined it in 2021 and identified lettering on the hull. The wreck’s design, location, damage and visible name confirmed it as HMS *Urge*. It lay close to the route expected for a submarine departing Malta, with damage consistent with striking a mine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.um.edu.mt/newspoint/news/2022/05/an-80-year-old-royal-navy-submarine-mystery-put-to-rest-with-memorial-unveiling-in-malta" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: um.edu.mt">[edu.mt]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">um.edu.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.mt.</span></span></span>

This discovery resolved most of the historical mystery. It strongly contradicts the claim that the submarine reached Libyan waters on an undisclosed mission. The wreck also demonstrates why absence from a known location should not be mistaken for evidence of secret activity. Wartime navigation was dangerous, minefields were imperfectly recorded and deep water could conceal a vessel within a short distance of a busy harbour for generations.

Some disagreement persisted after the identification, including renewed challenges to the accepted sinking narrative. Yet any alternative must explain the physical presence of the named submarine outside Malta, the mine damage and its position on the seabed. Those facts make the mine-loss interpretation considerably stronger than accounts based chiefly on disputed readings of operational records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/hms-urge-submarine-identified-no-secret-mission.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Live Science Wartime submarine &#x27;HMS Urge&#x27; identified, quashing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live Science Wartime submarine &#x27;HMS Urge&#x27; identified, quashing</span></span></span>

For relatives, the discovery was more than an archaeological solution. It identified a war grave and provided a location at which the crew and passengers could be commemorated. The case moved from unresolved disappearance to evidence-led reconstruction.

## What can the Xlendi shipwreck still reveal?

In 2007, a sonar survey off Xlendi Bay in Gozo detected an unusual feature on the seabed. Investigation revealed a remarkably well-preserved Phoenician merchant wreck lying at a depth of about 110 metres. Its mixed cargo, dating to the seventh century BC, includes ceramic containers and stone grinding equipment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://phoenicianshipwreck.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phoenicianshipwreck.org">[phoenicianshipwreck.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phoenicianshipwreck.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on phoenicianshipwreck.org.</span></span></span>

Unlike the cart ruts, the existence and broad date of the wreck are not seriously disputed. Its mysteries concern identity and movement. Where did the ship begin its journey? Which ports had it visited? Was Gozo its destination, an intermediate stop or simply the nearest land when disaster struck? What goods were carried inside containers whose organic contents have long disappeared? Why did the vessel sink?

The cargo shows connections across the central Mediterranean. Stone and pottery can be compared with known geological and manufacturing sources, allowing researchers to reconstruct trade networks. Yet a merchant ship rarely carried objects from only one place. Cargo might have been loaded and unloaded at several ports, while containers could be reused. The presence of an item therefore does not automatically reveal the ship’s home port or the identity of its crew.

Depth has preserved the site while making research difficult. Scientific divers have only a limited working period on the seabed and require lengthy decompression during ascent. Archaeologists have consequently relied on detailed photography, three-dimensional recording and selective recovery rather than attempting to raise the entire wreck. The project has been important not just for Maltese history but for the wider study of Phoenician seafaring, for which intact ships are exceptionally rare.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/bitstream/123456789/52798/1/Exploring_the_discovery_of_phoenician_shipwreck_in_gozo.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: um.edu.mt">[L-Università ta&#x27; Malta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">um.edu.mt</span><span class="citation-popover-title">L-Università ta&#x27; Malta Exploring the discovery of a Phoenician shipwreck in Gozo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">L-Università ta&#x27; Malta Exploring the discovery of a Phoenician shipwreck in Gozo</span></span></span>

The wreck is an unfinished investigation rather than a supernatural enigma. Each recovered vessel, residue sample or piece of timber may narrow the possible route, cargo and construction tradition. It offers the rare prospect that a Maltese mystery will become clearer gradually as technology improves.

## Why Malta attracts enduring mystery stories

Malta compresses an exceptional amount of history into a small landscape. Prehistoric burial chambers lie beneath modern streets; ancient tracks cross quarried rock; fortifications have been rebuilt by successive rulers; wartime tunnels penetrate older structures; and the surrounding seabed contains ships from many periods. Objects from different centuries can appear close together even when they have no historical connection.

This density encourages mistaken links. A groove approaching the coast becomes evidence of a drowned civilisation. A skull removed from display becomes proof of suppression. An undocumented tunnel becomes part of a vast underground network. A wartime vessel not yet found becomes a participant in a secret operation. The pattern is understandable: missing context invites narrative.

Three tests help distinguish a durable historical mystery from modern mythmaking:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Is there a contemporary record or physical object?** Caravaggio’s escape and the loss of HMS *Urge* are documented events. The disappearing-children story lacks comparable evidence.
2. **Does the theory explain more than ordinary historical processes do?** Transport, erosion and quarrying explain the cart ruts more economically than Atlantis or extraterrestrial engineering.
3. **Can new evidence change the conclusion?** The identification of HMS *Urge* did exactly that. A credible explanation must remain open to archaeology, archival discovery and scientific testing.

</div>

Malta’s mysteries are most compelling when uncertainty is preserved rather than decorated. The temple society’s transformation may never be reduced to a single cause. The cart ruts may belong to several periods. Caravaggio’s accomplices may remain nameless. Those limits do not weaken the history. They reveal how evidence survives: unevenly, accidentally and often without the voices needed to explain it.
