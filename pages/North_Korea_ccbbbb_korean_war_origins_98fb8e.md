## Introduction

Who really decided to start the Korean War? For decades, this question lay at the centre of Cold War propaganda. [North Korea]({{ 'what-is-true-in-north-koreas-hidden-past/' | relative_url }}) claimed that South Korea attacked first, while many early Western accounts portrayed the conflict as a straightforward act of Soviet aggression directed from Moscow. The opening of Soviet and Chinese archives after the Cold War transformed the debate. Declassified telegrams, meeting records and diplomatic reports now show that the decision emerged through negotiations between three communist leaders rather than a single command from above. The evidence strongly indicates that Kim Il Sung persistently pressed for permission to invade South Korea, Joseph Stalin eventually authorised the plan and supplied the military resources that made it possible, while Mao Zedong accepted the operation after consulting Stalin and later became deeply involved once the war expanded. Although historians continue to debate each leader's motives, there is now broad agreement about how the decision was reached.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/publication/NKIDP_eDossier_1_Origins_of_the_Korean_War.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wilson Center&#124; NORTH KOREA INTERNATIONAL DOCUMENTATION PROJECT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 31, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 31, 2025</span></span></span>

## Kim Il Sung's campaign for an invasion

The most important change in historical understanding concerns Kim Il Sung's own role. Earlier accounts often assumed he was simply carrying out Stalin's orders. Documents released from Soviet archives tell a different story.

From at least 1949, Kim repeatedly argued that military reunification was both necessary and achievable. He believed that South Korea's government was politically fragile, that many southerners would support a communist uprising and that the Republic of Korea's army would collapse quickly under attack. Soviet diplomatic messages record Kim asking Stalin several times for permission to launch an offensive, only to be refused while Stalin considered the international risks too great.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/korean-war-bibliography-wilson-center-publications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wilsoncenter.org.</span></span></span>

Kim's persistence mattered. Rather than receiving an unsolicited order from Moscow, he spent more than a year lobbying the Soviet leadership. This helps explain why many historians now describe the invasion as a North Korean initiative that required external approval rather than a Soviet plan imposed on Pyongyang.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/korean-war-bibliography-wilson-center-publications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wilsoncenter.org.</span></span></span>

This does not mean Kim acted independently. North Korea depended heavily on Soviet military equipment, advisers and political backing. Without that support, there was little realistic chance of launching a major offensive across the 38th parallel.

## Why Stalin changed his mind

Stalin's approval was the decisive turning point. Until early 1950 he consistently rejected Kim's requests because he feared provoking direct conflict with the United States.

Several developments altered his calculations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The communist victory in the Chinese Civil War in late 1949 strengthened the communist position in East Asia.
* The Soviet Union had successfully tested an atomic bomb in 1949, reducing its nuclear disadvantage against the United States.
* American Secretary of State Dean Acheson's January 1950 speech appeared to place South Korea outside the United States' primary Pacific defence perimeter, although historians disagree about how much this influenced Soviet thinking.
* Stalin believed that any American response would probably be limited or delayed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/korean-war-bibliography-wilson-center-publications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wilsoncenter.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

In April 1950, Stalin finally agreed to Kim's proposal. His approval, however, came with important conditions. Kim was expected to secure Mao Zedong's agreement, and the operation had to be prepared carefully with Soviet planning assistance.

Soviet involvement extended far beyond political approval. Moscow supplied tanks, artillery, aircraft, ammunition, military advisers and operational planning. North Korea's army entered the war far better equipped than its southern counterpart, largely because of Soviet assistance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gbv.wilsoncenter.org/publication/chinas-false-allegations-the-use-biological-weapons-the-united-states-during-the-korean" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gbv.wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gbv.wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wilsoncenter.org.</span></span></span>

One continuing scholarly debate concerns Stalin's deeper intentions. Some historians argue that he genuinely expected a rapid North Korean victory before the United States could react. Others point to later Soviet documents suggesting that Stalin may have anticipated American intervention but believed a limited Asian conflict would weaken the United States strategically without drawing the Soviet Union directly into war. This question remains debated because the surviving documents allow more than one interpretation of Stalin's reasoning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/publication/NKIDP_eDossier_1_Origins_of_the_Korean_War.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wilson Center&#124; NORTH KOREA INTERNATIONAL DOCUMENTATION PROJECT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 31, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 31, 2025</span></span></span>

## Mao's role and the miscalculations behind the attack

China's role before the invasion was smaller than Stalin's but still significant.

When Stalin agreed to Kim's proposal, he insisted that Mao be consulted. Communications between Moscow and Beijing show Stalin informing Mao of the plan and encouraging discussions between the Chinese and North Korean leadership. Mao accepted the proposal after these exchanges, although the available evidence suggests that he was not the original driving force behind the invasion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/korean-war-bibliography-wilson-center-publications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wilsoncenter.org.</span></span></span>

China also contributed indirectly by transferring experienced ethnic Korean veterans who had fought with communist forces during the Chinese Civil War back to North Korea. These troops helped strengthen the Korean People's Army before June 1950.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gbv.wilsoncenter.org/publication/chinas-false-allegations-the-use-biological-weapons-the-united-states-during-the-korean" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gbv.wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gbv.wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wilsoncenter.org.</span></span></span>

The greatest miscalculation shared by all three leaders concerned the international response.

Kim expected a swift military victory.

Stalin appears to have believed the United States would either stay out or intervene too slowly to prevent the collapse of South Korea.

Mao accepted the invasion without expecting that China would soon have to send hundreds of thousands of soldiers into Korea to prevent North Korea's destruction.

Instead, the United States rapidly secured United Nations backing, intervened militarily, and transformed what was expected to be a brief campaign into a major international war. After United Nations forces approached the Chinese border later in 1950, Mao ordered the Chinese People's Volunteer Army into the conflict, dramatically changing its course.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/korean-war-bibliography-wilson-center-publications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wilsoncenter.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the question remained controversial for so long

For decades, establishing responsibility proved difficult because the key documentary evidence remained secret.

North Korea maintained that South Korea initiated the conflict, a claim repeated in official histories and state propaganda. Soviet accounts similarly avoided acknowledging the planning that had preceded the invasion. Western historians, meanwhile, often lacked access to communist archives and relied heavily on intelligence reports, diplomatic observations and public statements.

The collapse of the Soviet Union fundamentally changed the debate. Russian archival releases during the 1990s, followed by additional Chinese and Eastern European documents, revealed extensive communications between Kim Il Sung, Stalin and Mao before June 1950. These records allowed historians to reconstruct the sequence of requests, approvals and military preparations with far greater confidence than had previously been possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/korean-war-bibliography-wilson-center-publications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wilsoncenter.org.</span></span></span>

## What is settled and what remains debated

Most specialists now regard several points as well established:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Kim Il Sung actively sought permission to reunify Korea by force.
* Stalin initially rejected these proposals before approving them in 1950.
* Soviet military support was indispensable to the invasion.
* Mao accepted the plan after consultation with Stalin and later committed Chinese forces once the war threatened North Korea's survival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/korean-war-bibliography-wilson-center-publications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wilsoncenter.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

Some questions, however, remain open to interpretation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Stalin's ultimate objective.** Historians disagree over whether he expected a quick victory, deliberately accepted the risk of American intervention, or hoped to strengthen Soviet strategic influence regardless of the outcome.
* **The weight of Mao's influence before June 1950.** Available documents show he approved the operation, but scholars continue to debate how much influence he exercised over the original decision compared with Kim and Stalin.
* **The extent of miscalculation.** There is no consensus on whether the communist leadership simply underestimated the United States or knowingly accepted a high-risk gamble.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wilsoncenter.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/publication/NKIDP_eDossier_1_Origins_of_the_Korean_War.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wilsoncenter.org">[Wilson Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wilsoncenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wilson Center&#124; NORTH KOREA INTERNATIONAL DOCUMENTATION PROJECT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 31, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 31, 2025</span></span></span>

</div>

The mystery today is therefore no longer about who crossed the border first. Declassified archives have largely resolved that question. The enduring historical puzzle lies in understanding why three experienced revolutionary leaders believed a limited invasion would produce a rapid political victory when it instead triggered one of the twentieth century's most destructive international wars.
