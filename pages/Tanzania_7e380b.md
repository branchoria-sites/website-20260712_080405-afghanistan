## Why was Engaruka abandoned?

Below the Rift Valley escarpment in northern Tanzania lie the remains of Engaruka, an extensive network of stone-lined fields, terraces, settlements and irrigation channels. Archaeologists describe it as the largest known abandoned irrigated and terraced landscape in East Africa. Its cultivation system covered thousands of acres in a dry region where farming depended on careful control of water flowing from the highlands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/quaternary-research/article/geoarchaeological-evidence-for-the-construction-irrigation-cultivation-and-resilience-of-15th18th-century-ad-terraced-landscape-at-engaruka-tanzania/05DBDE9BE807417CB5A0FD6A564943EA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment18th century AD terraced landscape at Engaruka, Tanzaniaby C Lang · 2017 · Cited by 49 — Our case...</span></span></span>

Research indicates that the main agricultural landscape was constructed and intensively used between roughly the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries. Farmers diverted water, trapped fertile sediment and built terraces that could be maintained and expanded over generations. The settlement was not an isolated curiosity: imported beads and shells point to wider exchange networks, while the scale of its works suggests organised labour and detailed environmental knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/geoarchaeological-evidence-for-the-construction-irrigation-cultiv" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.york.ac.uk">[york.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.york.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">geoarchaeological evidence for the construction irrigation cultiv</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pure YorkGeoarchaeological evidence for the construction, irrigation...by C Lang · 2017 · Cited by 49 — Geoarchaeological evidence for t...</span></span></span>

The puzzle is why such a successful system ceased operating. Earlier accounts often blamed a simple ecological collapse: the water failed, the climate dried or the population exhausted the land. More recent geoarchaeological work complicates that explanation. Engaruka’s fields appear to have remained productive through extended dry periods, and the irrigation system was resilient enough to intensify under changing conditions. Climate stress may have contributed to its decline, but it does not by itself explain a sudden or inevitable abandonment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249377320_The_development_of_the_ancient_irrigation_system_at_Engaruka_northern_Tanzania_Physical_and_societal_factors" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) The development of the ancient irrigation system at...December 1, 2010 — Once established, the land-use system at Enga...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span>

Several causes may instead have acted together:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Changing water access:** even a durable system could fail if streams shifted, channels were damaged or upstream communities gained greater control of the supply.
* **Altered trade routes:** Engaruka may have benefited from feeding travellers and caravans. Changes in regional exchange could have reduced its economic importance.
* **Political insecurity:** conflict, migration or pressure from incoming pastoral communities might have made maintaining labour-intensive fields less attractive.
* **Gradual relocation:** “abandonment” need not mean that everyone fled at once. Families may have moved over decades as livelihoods and alliances changed.

</div>

The identity of the builders has also been debated. Attempts to assign the entire complex to one modern ethnic group risk treating a landscape used across centuries as the product of a single, unchanging community. The most cautious conclusion is that Engaruka grew through local knowledge, trade and adaptation involving populations whose precise relationships cannot yet be reconstructed from archaeology alone.

Engaruka therefore remains mysterious in a specific sense: scholars understand much more about how the fields worked than about the social decisions that brought their use to an end. There is no convincing evidence that the settlement vanished in one dramatic catastrophe.

## Who left the footprints at Laetoli?

At Laetoli in northern Tanzania, volcanic ash preserved one of the most vivid scenes in the study of human origins. Around 3.66 million years ago, early hominins walked across a damp ash surface. Subsequent ashfall buried their tracks, preserving impressions of heels, toes and repeated steps over tens of metres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

The best-known Site G trail, excavated by Mary Leakey’s team in the late 1970s, contains about 70 footprints. It established beyond reasonable doubt that members of the human lineage were walking habitually on two legs long before the appearance of modern humans. Fossils found in the same geological formation make *Australopithecus afarensis* the leading candidate, although footprints cannot be matched to a species as securely as a skeleton can.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/behavior/footprints/laetoli-footprint-trails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: humanorigins.si.edu">[si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">humanorigins.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">laetoli footprint trails</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">laetoli footprint trails</span></span></span>

The deeper mystery emerged from another group of prints at Site A. Discovered in 1976, they were unusual, including steps that crossed in front of one another. Some researchers suspected that a bear walking briefly on its hind legs might have produced them. Because only a small section was exposed and the prints were subsequently reburied, the argument remained unresolved for decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

Researchers returned to the site in 2019 and re-excavated the trackway. Measurements of the footprints, comparisons with bears, chimpanzees and humans, and analysis of the crossing gait led the team to conclude that a bear was an unlikely maker. The prints were most consistent with a bipedal hominin whose foot shape and manner of walking differed from those seen in the better-known Laetoli tracks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04187-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span>

This raises an extraordinary possibility: two different kinds of early hominin may have crossed the same Tanzanian landscape at about the same time. Yet the evidence does not reveal the Site A walker’s species. The trackway is short, no matching foot skeleton has been recovered, and the strange gait may partly reflect uneven ground or an individual’s momentary movement rather than a species-wide walking style.

Even the Site G tracks continue to generate debate. Studies agree that their makers walked upright, but differ over exactly how closely their gait resembled that of modern humans. Footprints are shaped by anatomy, speed, body mass, soil moisture and the way sediment collapses after the foot is lifted. Researchers can model these factors, but they cannot replay the original walk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5013756/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The central Laetoli mystery is therefore no longer whether an early human relative walked there. It is how many kinds of hominin were present, which species they belonged to and how their different feet moved across the ash.

## Who painted the Kondoa rocks?

Central Tanzania’s Kondoa district contains hundreds of rock shelters with painted human figures, animals, hunting scenes, cattle and geometric forms. UNESCO inscribed the area as a World Heritage Site in 2006, recognising both the concentration of images and their continuing cultural importance to nearby communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1183/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The paintings were not all made at one time. Some fine red images are commonly associated with hunter-gatherer traditions, while later white or mixed-colour works often show cattle and have been linked to pastoral or farming societies. Certain shelters contain layers of images painted over one another, demonstrating repeated use across long periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/the-rock-art-of-kondoa-district-tanzania/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research-repository.uwa.edu.au">[the UWA Profiles and Research Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research-repository.uwa.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the rock art of kondoa district tanzania</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the rock art of kondoa district tanzania</span></span></span>

Yet specialists still lack a broadly accepted chronology. Pigments on exposed rock are difficult to date directly, and nearby charcoal, tools or occupation deposits do not automatically belong to the artists. A shelter may have been inhabited centuries before or after a particular image was painted. Stylistic comparisons can suggest sequences, but similar designs may persist or be revived over generations.

Authorship is equally complicated. Earlier researchers sometimes treated painting styles as labels for fixed ethnic groups. Modern studies are more cautious. The region has experienced long histories of migration, intermarriage, exchange and changing subsistence. Hunter-gatherers, pastoralists and farmers were not sealed communities whose art can always be separated by colour or subject. After nearly a century of formal research, scholars still report no clear agreement on either the full stylistic sequence or the makers of many paintings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/287418493_The_rock_art_of_Kondoa_District_Tanzania" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) The rock art of Kondoa District, Tanzania</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) The rock art of Kondoa District, Tanzania</span></span></span>

Nor is the meaning of the imagery easy to reduce to a single explanation. Hunting magic was once a popular interpretation, but rock art may also have been connected with healing, rainmaking, initiation, storytelling, clan identity or encounters with spiritual beings. Recorded examples of more recent painting traditions show that images could be created for several different purposes, even within the same community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kondoa_Rock-Art_Sites" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kondoa Rock-Art Sites</span></span></span>

The most responsible reading of Kondoa accepts multiple periods and meanings. The mystery is not a secret code awaiting one clever solution. It results from the long, overlapping use of sacred and social places by communities whose explanations were only partly recorded.

## What really happened during the Zanzibar Revolution?

In the early hours of 12 January 1964, insurgents overthrew the recently independent Sultanate of Zanzibar. Police positions were attacked, the Sultan fled, and a revolutionary government headed by Abeid Karume took power. The revolution transformed the islands and helped set the stage for Zanzibar’s union with Tanganyika later that year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanzibar_Revolution" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Zanzibar Revolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zanzibar Revolution</span></span></span>

The broad political outline is established, but some of the revolution’s most important details remain contested.

One uncertainty concerns John Okello, the Ugandan-born organiser whose radio broadcasts made him the most recognisable voice of the uprising. Okello presented himself as its commanding force, yet the extent to which he planned the revolution, acted for established political leaders or benefited from preparations made by others remains debated. Karume was absent from Zanzibar when the uprising began, but soon became president and rapidly pushed Okello aside. This has encouraged rival accounts in which Okello appears either as the revolution’s indispensable commander or as a volatile outsider whose importance was exaggerated by his own broadcasts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanzibar_Revolution" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Zanzibar Revolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zanzibar Revolution</span></span></span>

The death toll is even more disputed. Revolutionary violence was directed particularly at Arab and South Asian civilians, and killings, assaults, detention and looting are well documented. Estimates of those killed range from hundreds to several thousand, with some much higher figures circulating in political writing and public memory. Okello’s inflammatory broadcasts, hurried foreign reporting, population flight and the absence of a reliable contemporary count all contributed to the uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanzibar_Revolution" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Zanzibar Revolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zanzibar Revolution</span></span></span>

The lack of a precise total should not be used to minimise the suffering. Nor should the highest estimate automatically be repeated as settled fact. The responsible conclusion is that serious mass violence occurred, but available evidence does not support an exact number accepted across the historical literature.

Memory of the revolution remains divided because different communities experienced it in profoundly different ways. For many African Zanzibaris, it ended a political and social order associated with racial hierarchy and economic exclusion. For many Arab and South Asian families, it is remembered through killings, dispossession, exile and silence. Later state repression added another layer of trauma. Research on Zanzibar’s public memory describes how violence during the revolutionary period and the years that followed was frequently discussed indirectly or excluded from official commemoration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shs.hal.science/halshs-00856968/document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shs.hal.science">[SHS HAL Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shs.hal.science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hal.science.</span></span></span>

This is not simply a contest between truth and fabrication. It is a case in which political power shaped which testimony was preserved, which losses were publicly acknowledged and which interpretations became dangerous to voice. Archival gaps, exile narratives, propaganda and family memories must all be assessed without assuming that any one source is neutral.

## What happened to Azory Gwanda?

On 21 November 2017, freelance journalist Azory Gwanda disappeared in Tanzania’s Kibiti area. He had been reporting on a disturbing series of killings involving local officials, police officers and civilians. According to accounts collected by press-freedom organisations, unknown men took him away from his home, and he has not been seen publicly since.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cpj.org/whereisazory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cpj.org">[cpj.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cpj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cpj.org.</span></span></span>

No transparent investigation has established who took him, why he was targeted or what happened afterwards. In 2019, Tanzania’s foreign minister referred in an interview to Gwanda as one of several people who had “disappeared and died”, but gave no supporting explanation. The remark intensified concern rather than resolving the case. Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, Reporters Without Borders and the Committee to Protect Journalists all called for an independent or credible inquiry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cpj.org/whereisazory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cpj.org">[cpj.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cpj.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cpj.org.</span></span></span>

The most plausible public theory links his disappearance to his reporting in Kibiti, where violence and security operations had created an atmosphere of fear. That connection remains an inference, however. Publicly available evidence does not identify the men who removed him or demonstrate whether they were state officers, members of an armed group or individuals acting for another interest.

Gwanda’s case belongs in Tanzania’s historical record because it illustrates how a disappearance becomes an archival void. The basic facts are spare: a journalist entered a vehicle with unidentified men, vanished and was later casually described by a minister as dead. Without access to official files, witness testimony or forensic evidence, even the most basic questions remain unanswered.

Unlike an archaeological puzzle, this is not uncertainty created by thousands of years of decay. It concerns a modern person with a family and colleagues who have sought answers. Calling it a mystery should never obscure the possibility of criminal responsibility or the state’s duty to investigate.

## What the strongest evidence supports

Tanzania’s enduring enigmas vary greatly, and they should not be treated as though they demand the same kind of solution.

At Engaruka, archaeology has largely overturned the easy story of a society destroyed by simple environmental failure. The settlement’s decline was more likely produced by interacting changes in water, trade, security and population.

At Laetoli, the footprints are genuine and securely ancient. The unresolved question is the identity and diversity of the walkers, not whether early hominins moved upright.

At Kondoa, uncertainty is built into the nature of rock art. The paintings accumulated over long periods, and neither one date nor one people can explain the whole landscape.

In Zanzibar, the revolution unquestionably involved extensive violence, but casualty figures, individual roles and political responsibility remain entangled with propaganda, trauma and selective commemoration.

In Azory Gwanda’s disappearance, the absence of answers reflects a failure of public accountability rather than an unavoidable loss of ancient evidence.

Together, these cases show why historical mysteries endure. Physical traces may survive without names; state records may be incomplete or inaccessible; witnesses may disagree; and later generations may turn uncertainty into legend or political argument. The task is not to force a dramatic solution, but to separate what the record establishes from what it merely allows us to suspect.
