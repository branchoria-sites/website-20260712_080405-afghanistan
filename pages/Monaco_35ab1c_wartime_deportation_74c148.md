## Introduction

[Monaco]({{ 'which-monaco-mysteries-survive-the/' | relative_url }})'s wartime deportations are not a mystery because historians doubt that they happened. The central questions are different: how much responsibility did the principality's own authorities bear, why were crucial records unavailable for so long, and how have missing or scattered archives complicated efforts to identify victims, trace confiscated property and establish accountability? Over recent decades, official investigations have replaced long-standing silence with a more evidence-based picture. Even so, important gaps remain because wartime documentation was dispersed across Monaco, France, Germany and international archives, while some records were never preserved or were inaccessible for decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/aug/28/prince-albert-apologises-for-monacos-role-in-deporting-jews-to-nazi-camps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianPrince Albert apologises for Monaco&#x27;s role in deporting Jews to Nazi camps &#124; Holocaust &#124; The GuardianAugust 27, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 27, 2015</span></span></span>

Rather than exposing a hidden conspiracy, the surviving evidence reveals the difficulties of reconstructing events in a tiny state caught between formal neutrality, foreign occupation and pressure from larger powers. The continuing archival work is therefore as much about recovering individual lives as it is about judging government responsibility.

## The Arrests and Deportations of 1942 and 1944

The best documented episode occurred on the night of 27–28 August 1942, when police arrested dozens of Jewish refugees and residents in Monaco. Many had fled persecution elsewhere believing that the principality offered relative safety. Instead, they were transferred to the French transit camp at Drancy before deportation to Auschwitz. Later arrests followed during the German occupation after Italy's surrender in 1943, extending the persecution into 1944.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/aug/28/prince-albert-apologises-for-monacos-role-in-deporting-jews-to-nazi-camps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianPrince Albert apologises for Monaco&#x27;s role in deporting Jews to Nazi camps &#124; Holocaust &#124; The GuardianAugust 27, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 27, 2015</span></span></span>

Modern historical investigations have refined the figures as additional documentation has emerged. While earlier public accounts often referred to around 66 or 67 people arrested during the August 1942 roundup, expert research commissioned by Monaco concluded that approximately 90 people connected with Monaco were ultimately deported during the war, with only a small number surviving. Different totals appear because historians distinguish between those arrested inside Monaco, those later deported after leaving or being transferred from neighbouring France, and those whose residence status changed during the occupation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/aug/28/prince-albert-apologises-for-monacos-role-in-deporting-jews-to-nazi-camps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianPrince Albert apologises for Monaco&#x27;s role in deporting Jews to Nazi camps &#124; Holocaust &#124; The GuardianAugust 27, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 27, 2015</span></span></span>

The uncertainty is therefore largely one of classification rather than of historical fact. The deportations themselves are firmly established through police records, transport documentation, survivor testimony and French deportation archives.

## How Much Responsibility Did Monaco Bear?

For many years, public discussion often portrayed Monaco as a powerless victim of German and Vichy pressure. More recent scholarship presents a more nuanced picture.

The principality was exceptionally vulnerable because of its size and dependence on neighbouring powers. It was first influenced by Fascist Italy and later occupied by Germany. That external pressure was real, but expert investigations found that Monegasque institutions—including elements of the local police and administration—did participate in arrests and transfers. Responsibility therefore cannot be assigned solely to occupying authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/aug/28/prince-albert-apologises-for-monacos-role-in-deporting-jews-to-nazi-camps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianPrince Albert apologises for Monaco&#x27;s role in deporting Jews to Nazi camps &#124; Holocaust &#124; The GuardianAugust 27, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 27, 2015</span></span></span>

At the same time, the archival record also contains evidence of individual resistance. Researchers have identified examples of officials, clergy and private citizens who attempted to shield Jews, secure false documentation or intervene on behalf of threatened families. One surviving document records a Catholic baptism certificate issued in September 1942 for a Jewish girl, illustrating how religious paperwork could sometimes be used in efforts to protect individuals, although such attempts rarely guaranteed safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.monacostatistics.mc/content/download/188420/2168781/file/Report%20" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: monacostatistics.mc">[monacostatistics.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">monacostatistics.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Expert group</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Expert group</span></span></span>

This mixed record explains why historians avoid simple labels. Monaco was neither wholly innocent nor uniformly collaborative; decisions varied across institutions and individuals while taking place under escalating occupation.

## Why Are the Records Still Incomplete?

The most enduring archival mystery is not that the records disappeared entirely, but that they remained fragmented, dispersed and in some cases inaccessible for decades.

Several factors contributed to this situation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Multiple jurisdictions.** Records relating to one victim may be spread across Monaco's government archives, French police files, Drancy transport lists, German administrative records and post-war restitution files.
* **Administrative disruption.** Occupation, liberation and post-war institutional change interrupted record keeping and preservation.
* **Restricted access.** Some government files covering the critical years from 1942 to 1944 were not publicly accessible for many decades, limiting independent historical research.
* **Loss of personal documentation.** Families destroyed papers while fleeing, and confiscated possessions often became separated from their owners' files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archimag.com/archives-patrimoine/2020/01/23/monaco-accepte-ouvrir-archives-periode" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archimag.com">[archimag.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archimag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">monaco accepte ouvrir archives periode</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monaco accepte enfin d&#x27;ouvrir ses archives sur la période de 1942 à 1944 &#124; Archimag...</span></span></span>

</div>

As a result, reconstructing individual biographies frequently depends upon combining dozens of separate archival fragments rather than discovering one complete case file.

## Archives, Confiscated Assets and the 2015 Apology

Pressure from historians, particularly Serge and Beate Klarsfeld, encouraged Monaco to examine its wartime record more systematically. An expert commission reviewed surviving evidence, including police records, administrative correspondence and foreign archives, producing a much fuller account of arrests, deportations and confiscations than had previously been available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/aug/28/prince-albert-apologises-for-monacos-role-in-deporting-jews-to-nazi-camps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianPrince Albert apologises for Monaco&#x27;s role in deporting Jews to Nazi camps &#124; Holocaust &#124; The GuardianAugust 27, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 27, 2015</span></span></span>

The investigation also examined the seizure of Jewish property. Although Monaco's scale meant confiscations were smaller than in occupied France, authorities identified cases involving frozen or confiscated assets. The government later approved compensation claims relating to property taken from deported Jews, acknowledging that financial injustice had accompanied the deportations themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.worldjewishcongress.org/en/news/monaco-apologizes-for-world-war-ii-era-deportations-of-jews" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldjewishcongress.org">[World Jewish Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldjewishcongress.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Jewish CongressMonaco apologizes for World War II-era deportations of Jews - World Jewish Congress...</span></span></span>

In August 2015, Albert II, Prince of Monaco publicly apologised for Monaco's role in the deportations. During a ceremony unveiling a memorial listing the names of deported Jews, he acknowledged that the principality had failed to protect people who had sought refuge there and explicitly asked forgiveness for the actions of the authorities. The speech marked the first official acceptance of state responsibility rather than attributing events solely to foreign occupation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/aug/28/prince-albert-apologises-for-monacos-role-in-deporting-jews-to-nazi-camps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianPrince Albert apologises for Monaco&#x27;s role in deporting Jews to Nazi camps &#124; Holocaust &#124; The GuardianAugust 27, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 27, 2015</span></span></span>

## Opening the Files After Decades of Silence

Historical debate did not end with the 2015 apology. Researchers continued to argue that wider archival access was necessary to verify remaining questions about arrests, administrative decisions and property claims.

In 2020, following requests from the Simon Wiesenthal Center and continuing public interest, Monaco agreed to open previously inaccessible archives relating to the years 1942–1944. Historians viewed this as an important step because it allowed independent examination of records that had long remained effectively closed. The expectation was not that dramatic new revelations would overturn existing history, but that additional documentation would help clarify individual cases, resolve discrepancies in victim lists and better understand administrative decision-making.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archimag.com/archives-patrimoine/2020/01/23/monaco-accepte-ouvrir-archives-periode" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archimag.com">[Archimag]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archimag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">monaco accepte ouvrir archives periode</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monaco accepte enfin d&#x27;ouvrir ses archives sur la période de 1942 à 1944 &#124; Archimag...</span></span></span>

The archival opening also illustrates a broader feature of Holocaust research: historical certainty often increases gradually as separate collections become linked rather than through the discovery of a single decisive document.

## Questions left unresolved by Wartime Files
The unresolved element of Monaco's wartime history is no longer whether deportations occurred. That is established beyond reasonable doubt. Instead, the enduring questions concern documentation: whether every victim has been identified, whether every confiscated asset has been traced, and whether surviving archives fully reveal how decisions were made inside the principality.

For descendants, these questions remain deeply personal because missing files can prevent families from reconstructing the final movements of relatives or recovering knowledge of property that disappeared during persecution. For historians, Monaco provides a reminder that even one of Europe's smallest states became entangled in the administrative machinery of the Holocaust, and that the work of recovering historical truth often depends as much on reopening archives as on discovering new evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.monacostatistics.mc/content/download/188420/2168781/file/Report%20" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: monacostatistics.mc">[monacostatistics.mc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">monacostatistics.mc</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Expert group</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Expert group</span></span></span>
