## Introduction

The puzzle lies in the gaps that remain — and in determining whether those gaps can still be closed.

## What happened to Kuwait’s missing people?

When Iraqi forces invaded Kuwait on 2 August 1990, civilians and military personnel were arrested, abducted or separated from their families. Some were transported to detention sites in Iraq; others vanished amid fighting, resistance activity and the disorder surrounding the Iraqi withdrawal in early 1991. After liberation, families were often left with little more than witness accounts, prison rumours or confirmation that a relative had last been seen in Iraqi custody.

The issue quickly became an international responsibility rather than an ordinary missing-person investigation. United Nations resolutions required Iraq to provide information about Kuwaitis and third-country nationals removed during the occupation. A Tripartite Commission, working under the auspices of the International Committee of the Red Cross, was established in 1991 to investigate cases from both sides of the conflict. Its work has involved exchanging files, interviewing witnesses, examining suspected burial sites and comparing DNA from remains with samples supplied by relatives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/135867" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[un.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library SystemSituation of human rights in Kuwait under Iraqi occupation:Requests the Government of Iraq to provi...</span></span></span>

The investigation has produced real results. Remains recovered from mass graves and burial sites in Iraq have been transferred for forensic examination, and some families have finally received confirmed identifications decades after the invasion. In one major development, remains excavated near Samawa in 2019 and 2020 were analysed using DNA; 59 sets were identified as Kuwaiti or third-country nationals, while six unmatched sets were returned to Iraq. Other identifications have allowed individual cases formally to be closed and the dead to be buried by their families.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/kuwait/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[icmp.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuwait - International Commission on Missing Persons...</span></span></span>

Yet hundreds of cases have remained unresolved. Figures vary slightly according to the date and whether third-country nationals are included, but reporting in 2025 placed the outstanding total at 315 people, 308 of them on Kuwait’s official list. The variation does not indicate that the overall mystery is invented; it reflects differences between national lists, commission files and cases closed after later identifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kuwaittimes.com/article/29316/kuwait/whats-next-for-kuwaits-missing-persons-file-after-unami-ends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kuwaittimes.com">[Kuwait Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kuwaittimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kuwait Times What&#x27;s next for Kuwait&#x27;s missing persons file after UNAMI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuwait TimesWhat&#x27;s next for Kuwait&#x27;s missing persons file after UNAMI...June 11, 2025 — 11 Jun 2025 — Despite these efforts, 315 individ...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 11, 2025</span></span></span>

### Why has the search taken so long?

The obstacles are practical as well as political. Burial sites may have been poorly recorded, altered by construction or damaged during later conflicts in Iraq. Witnesses have died, memories have faded and documents may have been destroyed, hidden or dispersed. Even when remains are found, identification can depend on usable DNA from relatives and sufficiently preserved genetic material from the dead.

Iraq’s instability after 1991 also repeatedly interrupted fieldwork. Wars, sanctions, internal uprisings, the 2003 invasion and subsequent violence made access to suspected sites difficult and sometimes dangerous. Possible information may also have been held within security bodies associated with Saddam Hussein’s government, whose records and personnel were scattered after that government fell.

The strongest explanation is therefore not a single secret plot to conceal every prisoner. The evidence points to a mixture of executions, deaths in custody, wartime killings, undocumented burials and the destruction or loss of records. Some individual cases may still involve deliberate concealment, but there is no reliable basis for treating all of the missing as one centrally hidden group.

The mystery remains active at the highest diplomatic level. In September 2025, the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 2792, continuing international oversight and encouraging further excavation, technological assistance and cooperation between Iraq and Kuwait. After the end of the UN Assistance Mission for Iraq, the Secretary-General appointed a senior representative in 2026 to pursue the missing-person and property files. UN reporting is scheduled to continue at six-month intervals, showing that the matter is considered unresolved rather than merely commemorative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unscr.com/en/resolutions/doc/2792/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unscr.com">[unscr.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unscr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unscr.com.</span></span></span>

For Kuwait, the unanswered cases are the most painful national mystery because they concern identifiable people rather than an abstract wartime statistic. Each forensic match solves one case, but it also demonstrates how much may depend on a single grave, witness statement or surviving fragment of paperwork.

## Where are Kuwait’s missing archives and treasures?

The Iraqi occupation also created an immense cultural-property puzzle. Collections from the Kuwait National Museum, including archaeological and ethnographic material and the privately owned Al-Sabah Collection of Islamic art, were removed to Iraq. Libraries, government offices and other institutions were stripped of records and objects. The removals were organised enough that large quantities reached official storage in Baghdad, but the later trail of individual pieces was often confused.

The scale was remarkable. A detailed study of the recovery operation records that the Al-Sabah Collection contained about 3,000 objects and a library of roughly 3,000 volumes. Kuwait National Museum staff initially estimated that about 26,000 objects had been taken to Iraq; during recovery, the number proved to exceed 40,000. The museum library alone had held approximately 15,000 to 20,000 books.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cool.culturalheritage.org/jaic/articles/jaic39-01-011.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cool.culturalheritage.org">[cool.culturalheritage.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cool.culturalheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JAI C, Volume 39, Number 1, Article 11 (pp. to</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JAI C, Volume 39, Number 1, Article 11 (pp. to</span></span></span>

After Kuwait’s liberation, a United Nations-supervised operation recovered much of the museum material from Baghdad. This was not simply a matter of loading identifiable crates onto lorries. Kuwaiti specialists had to verify objects, assess damage and match them to inventories under difficult conditions. One major weakness soon became clear: the museum’s catalogues and archives had also been kept in Kuwait and had themselves been removed. With no complete duplicate inventory stored elsewhere, officials lacked the basic reference system normally used to prove ownership and identify missing pieces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cool.culturalheritage.org/jaic/articles/jaic39-01-011.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cool.culturalheritage.org">[cool.culturalheritage.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cool.culturalheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JAI C, Volume 39, Number 1, Article 11 (pp. to</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JAI C, Volume 39, Number 1, Article 11 (pp. to</span></span></span>

The successful return of tens of thousands of items can create the misleading impression that the case was solved. It was not. Some pieces were absent from Iraqi lists or could not be found at the recovery sites. Others may have been separated before arriving at official repositories, distributed among members of the Iraqi government or military, sold abroad, or looted again during Iraq’s upheaval after 2003.

Public attention has often focused on conspicuous jewels and Mughal-era objects from the Al-Sabah Collection. A jewelled dagger and dish appeared in a London auction catalogue in the 1990s and were returned, showing that at least some stolen pieces entered the international art market. Other items reported missing have included large engraved emeralds, jewellery, manuscripts and archaeological material. In 2010, museum and collection officials told *The Guardian* that hundreds of objects remained unaccounted for, although such historical figures should not be treated as a precise current inventory because returns and reclassifications have continued.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2010/jun/21/kuwait-lost-treasures-stolen-riches-rift-iraq" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Kuwait&#x27;s lost treasures: how stolen riches remain central to rift with Iraq</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Kuwait&#x27;s lost treasures: how stolen riches remain central to rift with Iraq</span></span></span>

### The deeper mystery is the missing paperwork

Kuwait’s national archives matter at least as much as valuable works of art. State records can document property, diplomacy, administration, family history and decisions made before the invasion. Without them, the loss is not only physical. It creates holes in the country’s institutional memory and makes later historical research more difficult.

Returns have continued long after the war. Kuwait received further batches of documents and property through UN-facilitated transfers, including another handover in 2025. Nevertheless, UN Security Council discussions still describe Kuwaiti property and the national archives as outstanding issues. The continued appointment of international officials to this file indicates that neither side regards the archive problem as closed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kuwaittimes.com/article/35298/kuwait/other-news/kuwait-recovers-stolen-archives/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kuwaittimes.com">[kuwaittimes.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kuwaittimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">kuwait recovers stolen archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">kuwait recovers stolen archives</span></span></span>

Several explanations may apply to different missing items:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Official removal followed by dispersal:** objects transported to Iraq may later have been divided among institutions, ministries or senior officials.
* **Private theft during the occupation:** some property may never have entered an official Iraqi catalogue.
* **A second wave of looting after 2003:** items stored in Iraq could have disappeared again amid the collapse of state control.
* **Identification failure:** poorly catalogued books, records and ordinary-looking artefacts may survive without being recognised as Kuwaiti property.
* **Destruction:** some documents and fragile objects may have been burned, damaged beyond recognition or discarded.

</div>

Claims that all of Kuwait’s missing treasures remain together in a secret warehouse are attractive but weakly supported. The known history of returns, auction appearances and later looting suggests a scattered trail rather than one intact hidden cache.

## What does Failaka Island still conceal?

Failaka Island, about 20 kilometres from the Kuwaiti mainland, contains a different kind of mystery. Archaeology has shown that it was occupied or used by a succession of societies linked to Mesopotamia, the Dilmun civilisation, Hellenistic kingdoms, early Christian communities and later Islamic settlements. Its position near the head of the Gulf made it a natural stopping point on maritime routes connecting southern Mesopotamia with Arabia and lands farther east.

Bronze Age remains include houses, temples, kilns and administrative structures associated with the Dilmun cultural sphere. Later remains include a Hellenistic fortress and religious buildings. UNESCO’s description of the island emphasises precisely this layering: Failaka is not the remnant of one lost civilisation but a landscape repeatedly occupied, adapted and reinterpreted over thousands of years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5800/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The best-known clue is the Ikaros Inscription, a limestone slab found by a Danish archaeological expedition in 1960. Its 44 lines of Greek text contain a royal administrative letter addressed to the inhabitants of “Ikaros”. The inscription is generally dated to the reign of the Seleucid king Seleucus II, between 246 and 226 BC, and refers to government, religion and the island’s population. It supplied firm evidence that the island was known as Ikaros during the Hellenistic period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/ikaros-nomination-mow-2023116-1-1108.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ikaros nomination mow 2023116</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ikaros nomination mow 2023116</span></span></span>

The inscription answers one question but raises several others. How large was the Hellenistic population? Were settlers from the Greek-speaking world living beside an established local community, or did the island’s inhabitants adopt Greek administrative forms while retaining older identities? How directly did distant Seleucid rulers control the settlement? The decree’s very existence suggests that authority had to be stated publicly, perhaps because the island’s government, sanctuary or population required regulation.

Religious evidence is equally intriguing. The text refers to a sanctuary associated with the Greek goddess Artemis, while earlier and neighbouring sites belonged to cultural worlds with their own religious traditions. Archaeologists therefore have to distinguish between replacement, coexistence and the reinterpretation of local beliefs through Greek names. A temple labelled Greek by an inscription may still have served a mixed community whose practices drew on several traditions.

Even the later fate of the inscription carries traces of conflict. It was found broken, and specialists have proposed that it may have been pulled down and defaced during struggles that weakened Seleucid authority. That is a scholarly reconstruction rather than a proven account: physical damage can establish that the stone was broken and displaced, but not with certainty who did it or why.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/ikaros-nomination-mow-2023116-1-1108.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ikaros nomination mow 2023116</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ikaros nomination mow 2023116</span></span></span>

Failaka’s archaeological puzzle is still developing. New research programmes and excavations continue to re-examine old sites with improved recording techniques, while Kuwait is pursuing recognition of the island’s wider cultural landscape through UNESCO. The aim is no longer merely to find spectacular artefacts, but to understand how settlements, harbours, temples, water supplies and trade networks changed over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arscan.parisnanterre.fr/failaka-ikaros-excavation-and-study-program/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arscan.parisnanterre.fr">[ArScAn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arscan.parisnanterre.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ar Sc An Failaka-Ikaros: excavation and research program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ar Sc An Failaka-Ikaros: excavation and research program</span></span></span>

War has complicated that work. Failaka was evacuated during the 1990 invasion and suffered military occupation and damage. Its ruins therefore preserve overlapping evidence of ancient settlement and modern conflict. In some places, the island’s twentieth-century abandonment has itself become part of the landscape that archaeologists and heritage authorities must interpret.

## Which stories are evidence and which are folklore?

Kuwait’s documented mysteries can easily accumulate dramatic embellishments. Stories of buried treasure, secret prison survivors, concealed warehouses or ancient supernatural sites often begin with a real absence: a missing person, an unreturned object or an unexplained ruin. The mistake is to treat that absence as proof of the most dramatic explanation.

A useful assessment begins by separating four levels of claim.

**Established fact:** Iraqi forces removed people, archives and museum collections from Kuwait; mass graves have yielded identifiable remains; many museum objects were recovered from Iraq; and Failaka contains settlements from several historical periods.

**Strong inference:** many missing Kuwaitis died in custody, were executed or were buried without proper records; some cultural property was dispersed into private hands; and Hellenistic Failaka contained a mixed community linked to wider Gulf trade.

**Open question:** the location and circumstances of every unresolved death, the whereabouts of each missing document or artefact, and the exact relationship between local and incoming populations on ancient Failaka.

**Folklore or speculation:** claims that all missing prisoners secretly survived for decades, that the entire national collection remains intact in one concealed vault, or that archaeological gaps prove the existence of a suppressed civilisation.

This distinction does not make Kuwait’s mysteries less compelling. It makes them more human. The missing-person investigation shows how a decades-old question can be narrowed through a grave excavation and a DNA comparison. The museum story shows how an object can travel from an occupied country to a foreign auction catalogue and then home again. Failaka shows how a single damaged inscription can establish an ancient name while leaving the nature of the community behind that name uncertain.

## Why these mysteries still matter

Kuwait’s unresolved history is concentrated around the problem of interrupted memory. In 1990, people were removed from their families, objects from their collections and records from the institutions that explained them. On Failaka, the interruption is far older: communities vanished or changed, leaving buildings, pottery and inscriptions without a complete narrative.

The war cases also remain central to relations between Kuwait and Iraq. Returning remains or archives is not merely an archaeological or administrative exercise. It can provide families with a grave, restore evidence of ownership and demonstrate that the two states are willing to confront the consequences of the invasion. That is why the United Nations continues to connect progress on [missing people]({{ 'missing-people-61f98c/' | relative_url }}) and property with confidence and mutual trust.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unscr.com/en/resolutions/doc/2792/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unscr.com">[UNSCR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unscr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unscr.com.</span></span></span>

Failaka carries a broader national significance. Its ruins challenge the idea that Kuwait’s history began with oil or even with the modern state. They place the country within thousands of years of Gulf trade, migration, imperial competition and religious change. The remaining uncertainties are therefore productive ones: each excavation has the potential to alter how Kuwait’s place in the ancient Gulf is understood.

The strongest conclusion is not that Kuwait hides one great secret. It is that several incomplete records — forensic, archival and archaeological — are still being reconstructed. Some questions may be solved by DNA or a returned file. Others may remain matters of interpretation. In each case, the mystery survives because the missing evidence once belonged to a real person, institution or community whose story is not yet complete.
