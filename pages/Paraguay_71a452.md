## The war whose numbers still do not add up

The Paraguayan War of 1864–1870, fought against the alliance of Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay, is the country’s defining national catastrophe. Its military chronology is well documented, but three fundamental questions remain contested: why the war escalated, how many Paraguayans died, and how much of the disaster resulted from enemy policy rather than the decisions of President Francisco Solano López.

The immediate crisis grew from struggles over political power and influence in Uruguay and the wider River Plate region. Brazil intervened in Uruguay in 1864; López responded by seizing a Brazilian vessel and invading Brazilian territory. When Argentina refused to let Paraguayan forces cross its province of Corrientes, López declared war on Argentina as well. Paraguay consequently faced states with far greater combined populations and resources. A particularly persistent theory claims that Britain engineered the war to destroy an economically independent Paraguay and open it to foreign capital. The theory became influential in nationalist and dependency-based interpretations during the twentieth century. Yet historians examining diplomatic and financial evidence have found no documentary proof that the British government ordered, organised or desired the conflict. Britain traded with the combatants and British financiers later lent money to the allies, but commercial benefit is not evidence of prior orchestration. The claim survives largely because it offers a simple outside culprit for an extraordinarily complicated disaster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguayan_War" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paraguayan War</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paraguayan War</span></span></span>

### How many people died?

The demographic mystery is more substantial. Paraguay did not possess a modern, consistently reliable census system before the war, and many administrative records were destroyed, removed or disrupted. Historians therefore have to reconstruct the population from censuses of uncertain quality, parish registers, military records, household counts and later demographic estimates.

Older accounts commonly stated that Paraguay lost roughly half its total population and an overwhelming majority of its adult men. Some estimates have been even higher. Revisionist demographic studies have argued for a smaller pre-war population and therefore a lower proportional loss, while other scholars have defended estimates indicating catastrophic mortality. The disagreement is not over whether the country was devastated; it concerns the scale of that devastation and the reliability of the figures used to calculate it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/183BB00AD4DC960F707B14756908E6C9/9781107050563c9_p194-228_CBO.pdf/richardson_on_war.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resolve.cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resolve.cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentRichardson on warParaguay lost an incredible 56% of its total population and 80% of its men... (I...</span></span></span>

Deaths also cannot be attributed to battle alone. Disease, hunger, forced movement, collapsing agriculture and the mobilisation of increasingly young or elderly recruits contributed to the toll. By the final phase, López’s government continued resistance while the surviving population retreated northwards. The result was not simply a defeated army but a society stripped of people, livestock, infrastructure and secure records.

This uncertainty matters because population figures support competing moral and political interpretations. Very high estimates can underpin descriptions of the war as extermination or genocide. Lower estimates do not make the conflict less brutal, but they complicate claims that the allies followed a systematic plan to eliminate the Paraguayan people. The available evidence firmly establishes demographic catastrophe; it does not produce one uncontested percentage or a universally accepted legal description.

### What happened at Cerro Corá?

The war ended on 1 March 1870 at Cerro Corá, where Brazilian troops caught López and the remains of his force. López was wounded and killed, but patriotic memory transformed the confused final encounter into a national martyrdom scene. The words traditionally attributed to him are commonly rendered as a declaration that he died with his country.

Exactly what López said, and whether he was attempting to surrender, resisting capture or seeking death, cannot be established with certainty. Accounts were produced by participants with political and military interests, and later governments elevated the episode into a foundational patriotic narrative. The factual core is clear: López refused to abandon the struggle and died during the final confrontation. The polished final words belong to a less secure layer of remembrance.

## The treasure said to lie beneath Paraguay

The most famous Paraguayan treasure legend concerns valuables supposedly buried during the Paraguayan War. Stories describe state funds, family wealth, church objects or military treasure hidden as allied armies advanced. Treasure hunters have searched farms, forests, old roads and former encampments for generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://roadsandkingdoms.com/2016/all-aboard-the-treasure-train/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: roadsandkingdoms.com">[roadsandkingdoms.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">roadsandkingdoms.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roads &amp; Kingdoms All Aboard the Treasure Train</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roads &amp; KingdomsAll Aboard the Treasure TrainJuly 20, 2016 — 20 Jul 2016 — For 140 years, Paraguayans have searched for plata yvyguy—buri...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 20, 2016</span></span></span>

The legend has a plausible historical foundation. During an invasion, civilians and officials commonly hide portable property. Paraguay’s population repeatedly evacuated settlements, while López’s government moved its administrative centre and remaining resources away from advancing forces. Some individual caches almost certainly existed, and occasional discoveries of coins or valuables would have strengthened the wider belief.

What lacks strong evidence is the idea of a single enormous national hoard waiting to be recovered. Contemporary documentation has not revealed a dependable map, inventory or chain of testimony leading to such a treasure. The stories often combine several different possibilities:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* personal savings hidden by families who never returned;
* church or municipal valuables removed during evacuations;
* military funds lost or concealed during retreat;
* valuables looted and then reburied;
* later inventions attached to unexplained wealth or unusual landscape features.

</div>

Over time, the treasure acquired supernatural guardians. Folklore describes strange lights, apparitions and dangerous spirits marking or protecting burial sites. These elements are cultural evidence about how Paraguay remembered the war, not reliable directions to an archaeological deposit. The treasure becomes accessible only to a worthy finder in some versions, while greed, betrayal or death punishes the wrong person in others.

The legend’s durability is understandable. The war left abandoned homes, broken family lines and possessions whose owners had vanished. Buried wealth offers an imaginative reversal of that history: something valuable may have survived beneath the ruined landscape even when so many people did not. Modern films and popular storytelling continue to use the treasure hunt as a way of exploring poverty, hope and the unresolved inheritance of the war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latas_vac%C3%ADas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Latas vacías</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Latas vacías</span></span></span>

The most reasonable conclusion is therefore divided. Small caches are historically credible and may occasionally have been found. A centrally organised fortune of extraordinary size remains unproved. Claims based on dreams, lights, secret maps without provenance or second-hand family stories should be treated as folklore unless supported by datable objects and a properly recorded archaeological context.

## The archive that solved one mystery and exposed many more

For decades, victims of Alfredo Stroessner’s dictatorship, which lasted from 1954 to 1989, described surveillance, illegal detention, torture, exile and disappearance. The regime denied or obscured much of this repression. That changed dramatically on 22 December 1992, when lawyer and former political prisoner Martín Almada and Judge José Agustín Fernández located a vast collection of police documents in Lambaré, near Asunción.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://plancondor.org/en/node/1474" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: plancondor.org">[plancondor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">plancondor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on plancondor.org.</span></span></span>

The collection became known as the [Archives of Terror]({{ 'terror-archives/' | relative_url }}). It contains official records produced by Paraguay’s security institutions, including surveillance reports, detention files, correspondence and material documenting cooperation among South American dictatorships. UNESCO placed the archive on its Memory of the World register in 2009, recognising its importance as evidence of systematic repression and Operation Condor, the cross-border system used to pursue political opponents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/archives-terror" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archives of Terror</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archives of Terror</span></span></span>

The discovery resolved a broad historical dispute. State terror was not merely an allegation assembled from hostile testimony: the state’s own bureaucracy had recorded it. Yet the archive did not answer the most painful individual questions. A detention form can show that someone entered official custody without revealing where that person was killed or buried. Other operations were undocumented, recorded under misleading language, or managed through institutions whose papers did not survive.

Paraguay’s Truth and Justice Commission later documented thousands of victims of detention, torture, execution, disappearance and exile. The surviving records also indicated that many victims were absent from the principal police files, showing why an archive cannot be treated as a complete list of crimes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archives_of_Terror" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archives of Terror</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archives of Terror</span></span></span>

### Where are the disappeared?

The continuing mystery is the location and identity of the disappeared. Recent reporting has described roughly 500 disappearances connected with the Stroessner period, while only a small number of bodies have been recovered. Families have spent decades searching military properties, police sites, rural burial areas and places identified through witness testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e05ea3172d4611887d083d62bad9106e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">One prominent figure in the fight for justice is Rogelio Goiburu, who has dedicated 47 years to searching for his father&#x27;s remains. As th...</span></span></span>

Rogelio Goiburú, whose father Agustín was abducted in Argentina in 1977, has become one of the central figures in these searches. His work illustrates both the possibilities and limits of investigation. Witness accounts can identify a suspected burial site, but landscapes change, buildings are erected, graves are disturbed and memories deteriorate. Even when remains are recovered, identification requires forensic expertise, family reference samples and a functioning genetic database.

The lack of answers is not simply an archaeological problem. Stroessner’s Colorado Party survived the dictatorship as Paraguay’s dominant political force, and former officials or their associates retained influence. Researchers and victims’ relatives argue that institutional continuity contributed to delayed investigations, limited funding and few prosecutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/e05ea3172d4611887d083d62bad9106e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">One prominent figure in the fight for justice is Rogelio Goiburu, who has dedicated 47 years to searching for his father&#x27;s remains. As th...</span></span></span>

This case should not be romanticised as an eerie secret. Each missing body represents a person whose relatives were denied the ordinary facts of death and burial. The Archives of Terror are remarkable because they expose the machinery of repression, but their silences are equally important. They show how a state can generate enormous quantities of paperwork while still concealing the final fate of its victims.

## How Josef Mengele escaped through Paraguay

Josef Mengele, the Auschwitz doctor responsible for selections and lethal medical experiments, escaped Europe after the Second World War. He reached Argentina in 1949 under a false identity, later resumed using his own name and moved to Paraguay as international pressure for his arrest increased. He obtained Paraguayan citizenship in 1959.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/josef-mengele" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopedia.ushmm.org">[Holocaust Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopedia.ushmm.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">josef mengele</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">josef mengele</span></span></span>

Mengele’s Paraguayan years generated one of the twentieth century’s most notorious manhunts. Reports placed him among German-speaking communities in the south and east of the country, where sympathetic residents, expatriate networks and political protection could make surveillance difficult. Paraguay under Stroessner was strongly anti-communist, authoritarian and receptive to some European immigrants with far-right connections.

The basic outline is now known. Mengele did not remain in Paraguay permanently and did not die there. He eventually moved into Brazil, lived with the assistance of protectors and drowned after suffering a medical emergency while swimming in 1979. His grave, under an assumed name, was found in 1985. Forensic examination and later DNA testing established that the remains were his.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2017/jan/11/josef-mengele-bones-brazil-forensic-medicine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Nazi doctor Josef Mengele&#x27;s bones used in Brazil forensic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Nazi doctor Josef Mengele&#x27;s bones used in Brazil forensic</span></span></span>

What remains uncertain is the full network that protected him. Intelligence agencies received numerous tips, but some were false, delayed or contradictory. Paraguay’s remote settlements and borders allowed sightings to multiply faster than they could be verified. Later stories have sometimes exaggerated narrow escapes or assigned precise locations and dates without documentary support.

A declassified United States intelligence file shows how strongly the international search once focused on Paraguay, but intelligence reports must be read carefully: they record claims under investigation, not necessarily confirmed facts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP87M01152R000100100007-8.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cia.gov.</span></span></span> The distinction is crucial because Mengele became a kind of phantom onto whom almost any rumour about an elderly German man in South America could be projected.

The real scandal is less mysterious than the rumours. Mengele survived because he had money, documents, family support and people willing to shelter him, while governments repeatedly failed to arrest him. Paraguay was an important part of that escape system, even though the final chapter occurred in Brazil. The enduring unanswered question is not whether he survived, but how many officials and private citizens knowingly enabled him.

## How to judge Paraguay’s other mystery claims

Paraguay’s landscape attracts claims about unidentified ruins, coded rock markings, secret tunnels and pre-Columbian visitors from Europe or the Middle East. Such ideas can appear convincing when marks resemble familiar alphabets or when a site lacks a clear written history. Similarity alone, however, is weak archaeological evidence.

A credible interpretation requires a recorded discovery context, secure dating, comparison with local Indigenous material, evidence that the markings are human-made and independent examination by relevant specialists. A supposed inscription cannot establish a Viking presence merely because selected lines resemble runes. Natural fractures, modern carving, copying and visual coincidence must first be excluded.

This standard does not diminish Paraguay’s genuine archaeological heritage. It protects it from being replaced by imported fantasies. Extraordinary migration claims often give outside adventurers the role of creator while treating Indigenous societies as passive or incapable. Until a controversial inscription is supported by securely dated associated material and peer-reviewed analysis, it is better classified as an unverified interpretation than a rewritten chapter of Atlantic history.

The same discipline applies across Paraguay’s mysteries. The Paraguayan War’s population loss is a legitimate scholarly dispute because researchers can compare demographic methods and records. The disappeared remain an active forensic problem because named people entered systems of repression and their bodies are still missing. Individual caches of wartime treasure are plausible, but a vast hidden fortune lacks documentary confirmation. Mengele’s presence is established, although many stories about his movements remain uncertain.

Paraguay’s deepest enigmas therefore occupy different levels of credibility. Some are questions that surviving evidence may eventually answer. Some are disputes unlikely ever to produce an exact figure. Others are legends attached to real suffering. Recognising those differences makes the history more compelling, not less: it replaces vague mystery with a clearer understanding of what was hidden, what was destroyed and why the search still matters.
