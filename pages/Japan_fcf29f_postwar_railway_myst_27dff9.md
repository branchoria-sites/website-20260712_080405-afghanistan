## Introduction

The summer of 1949 remains one of the most unsettling periods in modern Japanese history. Within just six weeks, the newly created Japanese National Railways (JNR) was shaken by three extraordinary incidents: the disappearance and death of its first president, Sadanori Shimoyama; the deadly runaway train at Mitaka; and the sabotage-induced derailment at Matsukawa. Together they became known as [Japan]({{ 'japan/' | relative_url }})'s "Three Great Railway Mysteries".



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Japan_fcf29f_postwar_railway_myst_27dff9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Railway Cases illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Japan_fcf29f_postwar_railway_myst_27dff9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Japan_fcf29f_postwar_railway_myst_27dff9-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

These cases mattered far beyond the railways. They unfolded during the Allied occupation, amid mass redundancies, fierce labour disputes and growing Cold War tensions. Investigations were criticised almost immediately, prosecutions later collapsed in important respects, and competing explanations—ranging from suicide and industrial sabotage to political murder and intelligence operations—have never been conclusively resolved. More than seventy-five years later, the cases remain symbols of how political pressure, incomplete forensic evidence and public distrust can leave historical mysteries permanently open.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ejrcf.or.jp/jrtr/jrtr10/history.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejrcf.or.jp">[or.jp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejrcf.or.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span></span></span>

## What Really Happened in Japan's Railway Summer?

The three cases followed one another with remarkable speed.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **5 July 1949:** Sadanori Shimoyama, president of the newly reorganised Japanese National Railways, disappeared in Tokyo.
* **6 July:** His body was discovered on railway tracks north of the city.
* **15 July:** An unmanned electric train ran out of control through Mitaka Station, killing six people and injuring around twenty.
* **17 August:** A passenger locomotive derailed near Matsukawa in Fukushima Prefecture after deliberate tampering with the track, killing three railway workers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ejrcf.or.jp/jrtr/jrtr10/history.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejrcf.or.jp">[or.jp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejrcf.or.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span></span></span>

</div>

The proximity of these events encouraged many people to see them as connected. Whether they actually were has never been demonstrated, but the coincidence profoundly influenced public opinion.

At the time, Japan was undergoing rapid political and economic restructuring under Allied occupation. The government was implementing severe spending cuts associated with the "Dodge Line" economic programme, while thousands of railway workers faced dismissal. The occupation authorities had also sharply restricted strikes by public-sector unions, creating an atmosphere in which labour conflict became deeply politicised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ejrcf.or.jp/jrtr/jrtr10/history.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejrcf.or.jp">[EJRCF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejrcf.or.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span></span></span>

## The Disappearance and Death of Sadanori Shimoyama

The first mystery centred on Sadanori Shimoyama, the inaugural president of Japanese National Railways.

Only weeks after taking office, Shimoyama announced plans for major staff reductions. On the morning of 5 July 1949 he disappeared while travelling through Tokyo. His mutilated body was found the following day on railway tracks near Kita-Senju.

The central question has never been resolved: **did Shimoyama take his own life, or was he murdered before being placed on the tracks?**

The physical evidence proved difficult to interpret. Because the body had been struck by a train, determining whether death occurred before or during the impact became a crucial forensic problem. Different medical experts reached different conclusions, and contemporary forensic techniques could not settle the matter beyond doubt.

Several explanations have been proposed over the decades:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Suicide**, prompted by overwhelming pressure from the impending redundancies.
* **Murder by political extremists**, either from the far left or the far right.
* **An operation linked to Cold War intelligence activity**, an idea that has attracted attention in books and documentaries but remains unsupported by conclusive documentary evidence.
* **A killing arising from labour conflict**, intended either to intimidate railway management or to discredit organised labour.

</div>

No theory has achieved scholarly consensus because none accounts convincingly for all the available evidence. The case officially remains unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ejrcf.or.jp/jrtr/jrtr10/history.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejrcf.or.jp">[EJRCF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejrcf.or.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span></span></span>

## Labour Conflict and Occupation-Era Pressure

Understanding why these mysteries acquired such political importance requires looking at the circumstances of 1949 rather than only the crimes themselves.

Japanese National Railways had just been reorganised as a public corporation. Tens of thousands of jobs were scheduled to disappear as part of economic reforms supported by the occupation authorities. At the same time, the emerging Cold War transformed labour disputes into ideological confrontations.

Communist influence within parts of the railway unions became a major concern for both the Japanese government and Allied authorities. Public-sector strikes were heavily restricted, union leaders were dismissed, and what became known as the "Red Purge" removed many left-wing activists from public employment.

Against this backdrop, each railway incident was immediately interpreted through political assumptions. Some officials viewed the cases as communist sabotage. Others suspected anti-communist provocations designed to justify wider repression. The speed with which political narratives developed often exceeded the available evidence, helping to create decades of suspicion about whether investigations remained genuinely impartial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ejrcf.or.jp/jrtr/jrtr10/history.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejrcf.or.jp">[or.jp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejrcf.or.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EJRCFDawn of Japanese National Railways</span></span></span>

## How Mitaka and Matsukawa Deepened Suspicion

### The Mitaka Incident

On 15 July 1949 an unmanned electric train left a depot at Mitaka Station with its controller tied open. It accelerated through the station before crashing into buildings, killing six people and injuring many more.

Police quickly focused on railway union members and Communist Party activists. Ten suspects were indicted.

The eventual legal outcome, however, did little to settle the controversy. Most defendants were acquitted, while railway employee Keisuke Takeuchi was ultimately convicted as a lone offender. Critics argued that the prosecution's reconstruction left important questions unanswered, including how one individual could have carried out every aspect of the operation without assistance. Takeuchi maintained his innocence until his death while seeking a retrial. The broader circumstances of the sabotage remain disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitaka_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mitaka incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mitaka incident</span></span></span>

### The Matsukawa Incident

One month later, saboteurs removed rail fastenings near Matsukawa Station in Fukushima Prefecture, causing a locomotive to derail and killing three crew members.

Investigators again arrested railway and factory union members, arguing that organised labour had orchestrated the attack. Twenty defendants were prosecuted.

Unlike the Mitaka case, the legal process eventually moved decisively in the opposite direction. Serious doubts emerged regarding confessions and witness testimony, while inconsistencies in the prosecution's evidence attracted growing public criticism. After years of appeals, every defendant was acquitted, and the Supreme Court confirmed the acquittals in 1963.

The sabotage itself is not disputed—the track had clearly been tampered with—but the identity of those responsible has never been established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%9D%BE%E5%B7%9D%E4%BA%8B%E4%BB%B6-136514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kotobank.jp">[コトバンク]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kotobank.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">コトバンク松川事件(マツカワジケン)とは？ 意味や使い方</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">コトバンク松川事件(マツカワジケン)とは？ 意味や使い方</span></span></span>

## Why the Mysteries Remain Unsolved

The three railway cases survive as historical mysteries because several obstacles have never been overcome.

First, crucial forensic evidence was limited by the investigative techniques available in 1949. Modern DNA analysis, digital reconstruction and advanced forensic pathology were unavailable.

Second, the investigations unfolded amid extraordinary political pressure. Detectives, prosecutors, occupation authorities and government officials all faced intense public expectations to identify those responsible quickly. Later criticism suggested that early investigative assumptions may have narrowed attention too rapidly towards politically convenient suspects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%9D%BE%E5%B7%9D%E4%BA%8B%E4%BB%B6-136514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kotobank.jp">[コトバンク]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kotobank.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">コトバンク松川事件(マツカワジケン)とは？ 意味や使い方</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">コトバンク松川事件(マツカワジケン)とは？ 意味や使い方</span></span></span>

Third, the legal outcomes differed sharply between the cases:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Shimoyama:** no definitive conclusion regarding murder or suicide.
* **Mitaka:** one conviction, but continuing debate over whether the lone-offender explanation is convincing.
* **Matsukawa:** complete acquittal of all defendants, leaving the actual perpetrators unidentified.

</div>

These contrasting results reinforced public scepticism. Rather than resolving uncertainty, each trial seemed to generate fresh questions.

## How the Railway Cases Shaped Public Memory

The 1949 railway mysteries occupy an unusual place in Japanese historical memory. They are remembered not simply as unsolved crimes but as reflections of a turbulent occupation-era society struggling with economic hardship, ideological conflict and institutional change.

Writers, investigative journalists and historians have repeatedly returned to the cases because newly discovered documents occasionally illuminate parts of the investigations without resolving their central questions. The publication of previously unavailable occupation records has enriched historical understanding of the political climate, yet no archival discovery has definitively answered who killed Shimoyama or who carried out the acts of railway sabotage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhk-book.co.jp/detail/000000804401999.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhk-book.co.jp">[NHK Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhk-book.co.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NHK Publishing三鷹事件 １９４９年夏に何が起きたのか &#124; NHK出版June 1, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 1999</span></span></span>

The enduring fascination therefore lies less in dramatic conspiracy than in historical uncertainty itself. The evidence supports the reality of the crimes and the intense political pressures surrounding them, but it does not support a single definitive explanation. That combination has allowed the "Railway Summer" of 1949 to remain one of Japan's most enduring post-war mysteries.
