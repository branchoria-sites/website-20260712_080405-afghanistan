## Introduction

For generations, much of [Bolivia]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-bolivias/' | relative_url }})'s Amazon was described as an untouched wilderness where only small, scattered communities had lived before European contact. That picture has changed dramatically. Using lidar (Light Detection and Ranging) – a laser-scanning technology that can map the ground beneath forest vegetation – archaeologists have revealed an extensive network of large settlements, raised roads, canals, reservoirs and monumental earthworks in the Llanos de Mojos. Rather than uncovering a single "lost city", the surveys exposed an organised landscape built by the Casarabe culture between about AD 500 and 1400. The discovery solved one long-standing historical puzzle while creating new ones: not whether complex societies existed in Bolivia's Amazon, but how they functioned, how many people they supported, and why they eventually disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>

## What Lidar Revealed Beneath Bolivia's Amazon

### The Landscape People Mistook for Wilderness

The Llanos de Mojos, in Bolivia's Beni Department, is a vast seasonally flooded savannah dotted with forest islands. For centuries, travellers noticed isolated mounds, straight embankments and unusual earthworks, but many were dismissed as natural features or treated as unrelated archaeological remains.

Lidar transformed that understanding. By sending millions of laser pulses from an aircraft and digitally removing vegetation from the resulting data, researchers produced detailed three-dimensional maps of the ancient landscape. Instead of isolated sites, they found carefully planned settlements linked by an extensive engineered network that had been almost impossible to recognise from the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>

The 2022 survey examined roughly 200 square kilometres across several parts of the Casarabe cultural area. Even within this relatively small sample, archaeologists identified a remarkably complex settlement hierarchy, suggesting that much larger areas remain unexplored.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>

## The Casarabe Settlement Network

The most striking discovery was that the Casarabe people did not build one spectacular city but an interconnected regional system.

Two exceptionally large centres, Cotoca and Landívar, stood at the top of a four-level settlement hierarchy. They were surrounded by progressively smaller communities connected through straight raised causeways extending for several kilometres across the flood-prone landscape. The largest centres covered approximately 315 and 147 hectares respectively, making them substantial civic and ceremonial complexes rather than isolated villages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>

These centres contained impressive earthen architecture, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Massive stepped platforms.
* U-shaped ceremonial structures.
* Rectangular platform mounds.
* Earthen pyramids reaching around 22 metres in height.
* Defensive or symbolic concentric embankments.
* Extensive canals and reservoirs for water management.

</div>

Rather than dense stone-built cities like those of the Andes, these appear to represent what archaeologists call **low-density urbanism**: populations spread across large areas with monumental public architecture, engineered landscapes and sophisticated infrastructure linking dispersed neighbourhoods. Similar forms of urban development are known from parts of Southeast Asia and the Maya world, but this was the first convincing example identified within Amazonia itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>

The findings overturned a long-standing assumption that Amazonian societies were necessarily small, mobile groups with only limited environmental impact. Instead, the evidence shows people deliberately reshaping their surroundings on an enormous scale over many centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>

## Why the Discovery Changed Amazonian History

Before lidar, archaeologists already knew that the Llanos de Mojos contained raised agricultural fields, causeways and artificial mounds. What remained uncertain was whether these represented scattered local projects or parts of a coordinated civilisation.

The laser surveys demonstrated that the monuments formed integrated settlement systems with clear planning and regional organisation. Roads connected centres directly rather than wandering with the terrain, while canals and reservoirs indicate coordinated management of seasonal flooding.

Equally important, the settlements show that Amazonian societies developed sophisticated solutions suited to tropical conditions instead of copying urban models from the Andes. Their monuments were built largely from earth rather than stone, reflecting available materials while still creating impressive ceremonial landscapes.

This has broader consequences for understanding Indigenous history across South America. Instead of seeing the Amazon as an empty forest before European arrival, archaeologists increasingly recognise it as a region containing multiple forms of large-scale social organisation that modified forests, wetlands and waterways over generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>

## What Archaeologists Still Do Not Know

Despite the spectacular mapping results, many of the biggest historical questions remain unanswered.

One uncertainty concerns population. Lidar reveals buildings, roads and earthworks but cannot directly show how many people lived there at any one time. Estimates vary considerably, and researchers remain cautious about assigning modern-style urban populations to the settlements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>

Another unresolved question is political organisation. The hierarchy of settlements suggests regional leadership, but it remains unclear whether the Casarabe world functioned as a unified state, a network of allied communities or something between those models. Excavation can identify architecture and artefacts more easily than political authority.

The purpose of some monumental constructions is also debated. U-shaped buildings and pyramids probably had ceremonial functions, yet their exact religious or administrative roles remain uncertain because the Casarabe left no written records.

Perhaps the greatest mystery concerns decline. Radiocarbon evidence indicates that the Casarabe cultural system ended around AD 1400, before Europeans reached the region. Archaeologists have not identified a single catastrophic event that explains its disappearance.

Possible explanations include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Environmental change affecting water management.
* Shifts in regional climate.
* Political fragmentation.
* Social reorganisation.
* Disease or demographic decline unrelated to European contact.

</div>

At present, none of these explanations has enough evidence to become the accepted answer. Unlike popular stories about vanished civilisations, the mystery lies not in the settlements disappearing without trace but in understanding the complex historical processes that transformed them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>

## Why the Mystery Still Matters

The lidar discoveries replaced an old misconception with a richer historical puzzle. The mystery is no longer whether Bolivia's Amazon supported organised societies—it clearly did—but how those societies achieved large-scale engineering in a seasonally flooded landscape and why that system ultimately came to an end.

The findings also serve as a reminder that archaeological visibility is not the same as historical importance. Dense vegetation concealed these landscapes from modern observers, yet their builders permanently reshaped thousands of square kilometres through roads, canals, reservoirs and monumental earthworks.

As additional lidar surveys and excavations continue, archaeologists expect more settlements to emerge beneath the forest canopy. Each new discovery has the potential to refine our understanding of the Casarabe culture and the wider history of Amazonian civilisation, while challenging older assumptions about what complex societies looked like outside the world's better-known stone-built cities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04780-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; Nature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lidar reveals pre-Hispanic low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon &#124; NatureMay 25, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 25, 2022</span></span></span>
