## Introduction

The Byblos Script is one of [Lebanon]({{ 'which-mysteries-still-haunt-lebanons/' | relative_url }})'s oldest and most persistent historical mysteries. Discovered during excavations at the ancient port city of Byblos between 1928 and 1932, the inscriptions belong to the Bronze Age yet remain unread despite nearly a century of study. Unlike famous undeciphered texts that survive in large numbers, the Byblos corpus consists of only a handful of short inscriptions engraved on bronze and stone. Every major decipherment has promised a breakthrough, but none has persuaded the wider scholarly community because no proposal explains all of the surviving texts consistently. Rather than a mystery of hidden knowledge, the Byblos Script is a reminder of how difficult it is to recover a lost language when the surviving evidence is extremely limited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

## What the surviving inscriptions contain

The known corpus is remarkably small. Most scholars recognise around fourteen inscriptions, although some fragmentary pieces have complicated the exact count. They were found on bronze tablets, bronze spatulae and stone monuments during the excavations led by the French archaeologist Maurice Dunand, who published the principal corpus in *Byblia Grammata* in 1945 and later added several fragmentary examples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

The inscriptions vary greatly in length. Two bronze tablets contain the longest continuous texts, while many of the remaining objects preserve only a few signs. This matters because decipherment normally depends on repeated words, predictable formulae and long passages that allow hypotheses to be tested. The Byblos material provides very little of this.

The script itself appears carefully organised rather than decorative. Researchers generally identify roughly one hundred distinct signs, although the exact number depends on whether some symbols represent separate characters or simply stylistic variants. The direction of writing is usually right to left, but a few inscriptions show different orientations, suggesting that scribes followed more than one layout convention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

## Why proposed decipherments remain unproven

The mystery is not a shortage of theories. It is the inability of any theory to survive detailed testing.

Successful decipherments usually rest on several independent lines of evidence, such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a bilingual inscription like the Rosetta Stone;
* recognisable royal names or place names;
* consistent grammatical patterns;
* predictions that also explain newly discovered texts.

</div>

The Byblos Script has none of these advantages. No bilingual inscription has been found, the underlying language is unknown, and the corpus is too small to generate statistically reliable patterns. As a result, almost any proposed sound values can be made to fit one inscription while failing on another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

This lack of independent verification explains why scholars remain cautious. A translation may appear convincing in isolation, but unless it also predicts the readings of every other inscription, it remains a hypothesis rather than a decipherment.

## The major decipherment attempts

Several ambitious efforts have tried to unlock the script, each starting from different assumptions about both the writing system and the language behind it.

### Early proposals

The earliest attempts came from Bedřich Hrozný and Édouard Dhorme during the 1940s. Both sought phonetic values for the signs, but neither interpretation gained lasting acceptance because the readings could not be demonstrated consistently across the corpus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

### George Mendenhall's syllabic model

The best-known modern proposal was published by George E. Mendenhall in 1985. He argued that the script was primarily syllabic rather than alphabetic, reducing the apparent inventory of signs by treating many as variants. He proposed that the underlying language was an early North-West Semitic language related to the ancestors of Phoenician and Hebrew.

Mendenhall went beyond assigning sound values by translating the longer inscriptions. One bronze tablet became, in his interpretation, a marriage contract. His work attracted considerable attention because it offered a complete system rather than isolated guesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

However, specialists criticised several aspects of the proposal, including its treatment of sign variants, its assumptions about pronunciation and the absence of independent confirmation. Although a few researchers refined elements of his work, it never achieved broad acceptance.

### Alternative readings

Other scholars reached dramatically different conclusions from the same inscriptions.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://bop.unibe.ch/baf/article/view/7186" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bop.unibe.ch">[bop.unibe.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bop.unibe.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">word boundaries in an undeciphered script: The Byblos syllabary &#124; BAF-Online: Proceedings of the Berner Altorientalisches ForumDecember 2...</span></span></span>

Jan Best connected the script with Egyptian writing and Aegean traditions, interpreting one of the main texts as a dedication. Francesco Woudhuizen later modified this approach, introducing greater use of logographic signs, while Giovanni Garbini offered yet another interpretation, reading one inscription as a medical remedy against vomiting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

The striking fact is not which translation is correct, but that the same inscription has been interpreted as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* a marriage contract;
* a religious dedication;
* a medical prescription.

</div>

Such radically different outcomes demonstrate why none of the decipherments has convinced the wider academic community. A reliable decipherment should narrow possibilities rather than multiply them.

## Could the script link Egypt and Phoenicia?

One reason the script attracts so much attention is its position in the history of writing.

Byblos maintained exceptionally close commercial and diplomatic relations with Egypt throughout much of the Bronze Age. Timber from Lebanon was exported to the Nile Valley, while Egyptian influence is visible in local art, religion and administration. Several signs in the script resemble simplified Egyptian hieroglyphs, leading Maurice Dunand to describe it as "pseudo-hieroglyphic".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

Most specialists agree, however, that visual resemblance alone proves very little. Writing systems often borrow shapes while assigning entirely different sounds or meanings. Even if Egyptian writing influenced the appearance of the signs, that does not reveal how the script was read.

Another possibility is that the system represents an experimental stage between Egyptian-inspired writing and the later alphabetic traditions that emerged in the Levant. The number of signs—too many for an alphabet but too few for a fully logographic system—has led many researchers to favour a mainly syllabic script. Even so, the available evidence is insufficient to establish how, or even whether, it contributed directly to the later Phoenician alphabet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

## Why modern technology has not solved the mystery

Advances in digital imaging, statistical linguistics and artificial intelligence have transformed the study of many ancient scripts. They can identify repeated sign sequences, distinguish subtle variations between characters and compare writing systems more efficiently than earlier generations could.

Yet these methods cannot compensate for missing evidence.

Machine learning performs best when trained on large datasets. The Byblos Script offers only a tiny collection of short inscriptions, leaving too little material to establish reliable linguistic patterns. Computers may improve sign classification or reveal previously unnoticed relationships, but they cannot infer an entire language from such limited data without external confirmation. The principal obstacle remains archaeological rather than computational: too few texts survive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery still matters

The enduring importance of the Byblos Script lies less in the hope of a spectacular hidden message than in what it represents for the history of writing in Lebanon and the eastern Mediterranean.

If the script were securely deciphered, it could illuminate literacy, administration and cultural exchange in Bronze Age Byblos at a time when Egypt, the Levant and neighbouring societies were closely connected. It might clarify which language was spoken by the scribes, how writing evolved before the emergence of the Phoenician alphabet, and whether multiple writing traditions coexisted in the city.

For now, the script remains a genuine historical puzzle because the evidence is incomplete rather than because scholars lack ideas. Every major decipherment has failed the same test: it cannot explain the entire corpus in a way that other specialists can independently verify. Until new inscriptions are discovered or an unexpected bilingual text emerges, the Byblos Script is likely to remain one of Lebanon's most significant unresolved archaeological and linguistic mysteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=3&amp;lang=en&amp;page=Scrittura" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mnamon.sns.it">[Mnamon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mnamon.sns.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mnamon Byblos (Pseudo-hieroglyphic</span></span></span>
