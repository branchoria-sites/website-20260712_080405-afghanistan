## Introduction

The bones discovered at the ruined Augustinian complex in Old Goa are among the strongest archaeological candidates ever found for the remains of Queen Ketevan of [Georgia]({{ 'georgia/' | relative_url }}), the seventeenth-century royal martyr executed in Safavid Persia in 1624. Yet the discovery has never become an unquestioned historical fact. Scientific testing, historical documents and archaeological evidence all point in the same direction, but none can identify the bones with absolute certainty. The result is an unusual historical mystery: not whether the relics are genuine in a religious sense, but whether modern science can prove beyond reasonable doubt that they belonged to one of Georgia's best-known queens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24355295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan - PubMedDecember 16, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 16, 2013</span></span></span>

## How the relic trail led from Persia to Goa

Queen Ketevan was imprisoned for years after the Persian ruler Shah Abbas I invaded eastern Georgia. According to contemporary and near-contemporary Christian accounts, she refused to renounce her faith and was tortured to death in September 1624. Her execution quickly became central to Georgian religious memory.

The mystery begins immediately after her death. Historical records written by the Augustinian missionaries working in Persia describe how two friars secretly recovered part of her remains after the execution. They reportedly concealed the bones for several years before transporting at least one major relic—traditionally identified as her right arm—to the Augustinian convent in Old Goa, then one of the Portuguese Empire's most important religious centres. Other relics were dispersed to churches elsewhere, including Rome, making it unlikely that her skeleton would ever be found intact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1567724913002791" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

For centuries, however, the Goa relic disappeared from view. The Augustinian monastery gradually fell into ruin after the Portuguese religious orders were dissolved, and the exact location of the relic became uncertain. Georgian delegations began searching seriously during the late twentieth century, working alongside the Archaeological Survey of India. Excavations at the former convent uncovered stone reliquary remains and several human bone fragments from the area where historical records indicated Ketevan's relic had once been kept.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1567724913002791" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

This convergence of written evidence and archaeological location made the bones unusually promising candidates, but archaeology alone could not establish whose remains they were.

## What the mitochondrial DNA result showed

The strongest scientific evidence arrived with an ancient DNA study published in 2014. Researchers extracted degraded mitochondrial DNA—genetic material inherited through the maternal line—from one of the excavated bone samples.

The analysis identified mitochondrial haplogroup U1b. This lineage is rare globally and was not known from Indian populations, but it does occur in Georgia and neighbouring parts of the Caucasus and western Asia. Because the bones were recovered exactly where historical sources placed Ketevan's relic, the researchers concluded that the genetic findings supported the archaeological and documentary evidence rather than contradicting it. They therefore judged that the remains were probably those of Queen Ketevan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24355295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan - PubMedDecember 16, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 16, 2013</span></span></span>

The study was significant for another reason: it represented one of the earliest successful ancient DNA analyses conducted on archaeological human remains recovered in India, demonstrating that genetic evidence could contribute to solving historical identification problems despite severe DNA degradation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1567724913002791" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## Why a strong attribution is not final proof

The mystery survives because mitochondrial DNA has important limitations.

Unlike a complete genome, mitochondrial DNA cannot identify a unique individual. Instead, it places someone within a maternal ancestry shared by many people. A person carrying haplogroup U1b could have originated from the Caucasus without necessarily being Queen Ketevan herself.

Several factors therefore prevent an absolute identification:

* **No confirmed comparison sample exists.** There is no authenticated DNA from Queen Ketevan or an unquestionably identified maternal relative against which the Goa DNA can be matched.
* **Historical transport cannot be observed directly.** The written accounts describe the movement of her relics, but historians cannot independently verify every stage of that journey.
* **Only fragmentary remains survive.** The recovered bones represent part of a dispersed relic collection rather than an intact burial, limiting forensic reconstruction.
* **Ancient DNA is highly degraded.** Although the researchers followed authentication procedures appropriate for ancient DNA, degraded samples inevitably provide less information than modern genomes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24355295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan - PubMedDecember 16, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 16, 2013</span></span></span>

For these reasons, scholars generally describe the identification as highly probable rather than conclusively proven.

## Why most historians accept the identification

Although the DNA evidence alone is insufficient for certainty, historians rarely evaluate archaeological discoveries using genetics in isolation.

Instead, several independent strands reinforce one another:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* contemporary missionary accounts describing the recovery of Ketevan's remains;
* records stating that relics were transferred to the Augustinian convent in Goa;
* excavation of the correct location within the ruined convent;
* discovery of bones associated with the reported reliquary site; and
* mitochondrial DNA consistent with Caucasian rather than local Indian ancestry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1567724913002791" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

</div>

No single element proves the case. Together, however, they form a coherent chain that many researchers regard as persuasive.

## Why the question still matters

The Goa relics illustrate how historical mysteries often persist even after major scientific advances. DNA testing narrowed the range of possibilities dramatically, but it could not eliminate every uncertainty because the necessary comparison samples simply do not exist.

For Georgia, the case has importance beyond archaeology. Queen Ketevan is remembered both as a historical ruler and as a Christian martyr. Identifying relics associated with her strengthens the documentary record linking Georgia, Safavid Persia and Portuguese Goa during the early seventeenth century, while also showing how relics could travel across continents through missionary networks.

The enduring question is therefore no longer, "Were any relics of Queen Ketevan taken to Goa?" The historical evidence strongly suggests they were. The remaining mystery is whether modern science can ever move from "probably" to "proved". Given the fragmentary remains, dispersed relics and absence of authenticated family DNA, that final level of certainty may remain permanently beyond reach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24355295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relic excavated in western India is probably of Georgian Queen Ketevan - PubMedDecember 16, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 16, 2013</span></span></span>
