## Introduction

When did [Brunei]({{ 'what-bruneis-fragmentary-past-still/' | relative_url }})'s sultanate truly begin? The simple answer is that historians can identify a tradition placing Sultan Muhammad Shah at the beginning of the Islamic monarchy in the late fourteenth century, but they cannot date the earliest reigns with complete confidence. The mystery is not whether Brunei possessed an ancient royal line, but how that line entered written history. The surviving royal genealogies were compiled centuries after the rulers they describe, while contemporary evidence comes mainly from Chinese diplomatic records, archaeology and a handful of external sources. Together they provide a recognisable outline, yet they do not fit together perfectly. That gap between royal tradition and surviving documentation explains why the origins of Brunei's first sultans remain one of the country's most enduring historical debates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bruneitourism.com/places/batu-tersilah/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bruneitourism.com">[bruneitourism.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bruneitourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batu Tersilah</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abode of Peace - Brunei Tourism Official Site...</span></span></span>

## Muhammad Shah and the founding tradition

According to Brunei's royal tradition, the first Muslim ruler was Sultan Muhammad Shah, remembered before his conversion as Awang Alak Betatar. He is generally placed in the second half of the fourteenth century and is credited with transforming an older river kingdom into an Islamic sultanate. Later Bruneian histories present this conversion as the defining moment from which the succession of sultans begins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/sites/default/files/knowledge-bank-article/a%20brunei%20sultan%20of%20the%20early%20fourteenth%20century.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chapter 8A BRUNEI SULTAN OF THE...</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that no contemporary Bruneian chronicle survives from Muhammad Shah's reign. Instead, historians reconstruct his life by comparing later Bruneian genealogies with Chinese records of rulers from a kingdom known as Boni or Po-ni. Chinese sources record a ruler whose name has been interpreted as Muhammad Shah or Mahmud Shah during the early Ming period, but scholars differ over exactly how these Chinese transcriptions should be read. Even where the identification is accepted, the records were created for diplomatic purposes rather than as biographies, leaving many details of the ruler's life uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/sites/default/files/knowledge-bank-article/a%20brunei%20sultan%20of%20the%20early%20fourteenth%20century.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chapter 8A BRUNEI SULTAN OF THE...</span></span></span>

This means that Muhammad Shah occupies an unusual place in Southeast Asian history. He is neither purely legendary nor fully documented. Instead, he stands at the point where oral tradition, royal memory and scattered foreign records begin to overlap.

## What the Batu Tarsilah records

The single most important local source for Brunei's early rulers is the Batu Tarsilah, a carved genealogical stone recording the succession of Brunei's sultans. It traces the royal line from Sultan Muhammad Shah through later rulers and remains one of the monarchy's central historical monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bruneitourism.com/places/batu-tersilah/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bruneitourism.com">[bruneitourism.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bruneitourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batu Tersilah</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abode of Peace - Brunei Tourism Official Site...</span></span></span>

Its value, however, must be understood carefully. The stone itself was commissioned many centuries after the reigns of the earliest sultans. Rather than being a contemporary inscription from the fourteenth century, it preserves a genealogy that had already been transmitted through earlier written traditions and court memory before being carved into stone. Historians therefore treat it as an invaluable witness to how Brunei understood its own royal past, but not as direct proof that every detail of the earliest succession is historically exact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bruneitourism.com/places/batu-tersilah/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bruneitourism.com">[bruneitourism.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bruneitourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batu Tersilah</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abode of Peace - Brunei Tourism Official Site...</span></span></span>

* It establishes the official sequence of Brunei's early rulers.
* It links later sultans to Sultan Muhammad Shah as founder of the Islamic monarchy.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Shah_of_Brunei" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Muhammad Shah of Brunei</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Muhammad Shah of Brunei</span></span></span>
* It preserves dynastic continuity across periods where other local records have disappeared.
* It demonstrates how later Bruneian rulers wished the history of the monarchy to be remembered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bruneitourism.com/places/batu-tersilah/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bruneitourism.com">[bruneitourism.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bruneitourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batu Tersilah</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abode of Peace - Brunei Tourism Official Site...</span></span></span>

For historians, its greatest importance lies not simply in the names it contains but in revealing how historical memory was organised within the Bruneian court.

## Where genealogy and external evidence diverge

The principal historical puzzle appears when the genealogy is compared with contemporary foreign records.

Chinese imperial documents record tribute missions from Boni during the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries, including rulers who travelled to the Ming court or sent diplomatic envoys. These records confirm that a significant kingdom existed in north-west Borneo and maintained relations with China. Yet the names, dates and succession do not always align neatly with the later Bruneian genealogy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/sites/default/files/knowledge-bank-article/a%20brunei%20sultan%20of%20the%20early%20fourteenth%20century.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chapter 8A BRUNEI SULTAN OF THE...</span></span></span>

One example concerns the ruler who died while visiting the Ming court in 1408 and was buried near Nanjing. Chinese sources identify this king with a name usually associated with Sultan Abdul Majid Hassan, Brunei's second sultan in the traditional succession. The existence of his tomb provides unusually strong external confirmation that an early Bruneian ruler maintained direct diplomatic relations with Ming China. At the same time, it illustrates how much historians still depend on foreign records to anchor Brunei's earliest chronology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sultans_of_Brunei" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">List of sultans of Brunei</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">List of sultans of Brunei</span></span></span>

Differences also appear in regnal dates. Modern published lists often assign precise accession and death years to the first sultans, but these dates are largely reconstructions derived from later traditions combined with Chinese evidence rather than dates recorded in contemporary Bruneian documents. Consequently, historians generally regard them as plausible estimates rather than fixed historical facts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sultans_of_Brunei" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">List of sultans of Brunei</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">List of sultans of Brunei</span></span></span>

## Why dating the first reigns remains difficult

Several factors explain why the earliest history of Brunei's monarchy remains uncertain.

First, very few indigenous written records survive from the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. Tropical climate, warfare and the natural fragility of manuscripts all contributed to the loss of early documents.

Second, royal genealogies serve purposes beyond simple chronology. Throughout Southeast Asia they legitimised dynasties, explained succession and reinforced political continuity. That does not make them fictional, but it means they often compress events, simplify disputed successions or emphasise legitimacy over exact dating.

Third, external observers were recording only those events relevant to them. Chinese officials cared primarily about tribute missions and diplomatic protocol rather than documenting Brunei's internal politics. As a result, long intervals between embassy records leave many questions unanswered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/sites/default/files/knowledge-bank-article/a%20brunei%20sultan%20of%20the%20early%20fourteenth%20century.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chapter 8A BRUNEI SULTAN OF THE...</span></span></span>

Finally, archaeology can confirm that the Brunei River supported a prosperous trading settlement before and during the emergence of the sultanate, but archaeological finds rarely identify individual rulers. Material evidence therefore strengthens the general historical picture without resolving debates over specific reigns.

## What historians consider well established—and what remains uncertain

Several conclusions command broad acceptance.

Well supported by the available evidence are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* Brunei had an organised ruling dynasty by the late fourteenth or early fifteenth century.
* Sultan Muhammad Shah is consistently remembered as the founder of the Islamic sultanate in Bruneian tradition.
* The early rulers maintained diplomatic relations with Ming China.
* The Batu Tarsilah preserves an important dynastic tradition that became central to Brunei's historical identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bruneitourism.com/places/batu-tersilah/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bruneitourism.com">[bruneitourism.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bruneitourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batu Tersilah</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abode of Peace - Brunei Tourism Official Site...</span></span></span>

</div>

The principal uncertainties concern:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the precise year the sultanate was founded;
* the exact identities behind several Chinese royal names;
* whether every ruler in the later genealogy can be securely matched to contemporary foreign records; and
* the extent to which later genealogical traditions reshaped earlier political history.

</div>

These questions are matters of historical method rather than evidence of deception. Most scholars see the surviving genealogy as preserving a genuine historical core while recognising that its earliest sections cannot always be verified independently.

## Why the mystery still matters

The story of Brunei's first sultans illustrates how kingdoms often entered recorded history gradually rather than suddenly. Instead of a single founding document, Brunei's beginnings survive through the combination of court genealogy, foreign diplomacy and archaeology. Each source answers different questions while leaving others unresolved.

That makes the origins of the sultanate less a mystery of disappearance than one of historical visibility. The first rulers almost certainly existed, but they emerged into written history through records created for different purposes, at different times and by different cultures. Reconstructing their story therefore requires balancing royal tradition with external evidence rather than accepting either source on its own.
