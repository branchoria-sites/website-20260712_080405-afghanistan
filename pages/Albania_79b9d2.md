## Who laid the Corfu Channel mines?

On 22 October 1946, the British destroyers HMS *Saumarez* and HMS *Volage* struck mines while sailing through Albanian territorial waters in the North Corfu Channel. The explosions killed 44 men, injured many more and badly damaged both ships. The incident became one of the first major confrontations of the early Cold War and produced the first contentious case decided by the newly established International Court of Justice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj-cij.org/case/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj-cij.org">[International Court of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj-cij.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Court of JusticeCorfu Channel (United Kingdom of Great Britain and...A second Judgment, rendered on 9 April 1949, related...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1949</span></span></span>

The court reached a firm legal conclusion but did not solve the central historical puzzle. In 1949 it held Albania internationally responsible because the minefield was so close to its coast that the Albanian authorities must have known of the danger and should have warned passing ships. Yet it rejected the British claim that Albania itself had laid the mines, and it did not find sufficient proof that Albania had conspired with Yugoslavia to place them. Albania was ordered to compensate Britain, but the identity of the mine-layers remained legally unproven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icj-cij.org/case/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icj-cij.org">[International Court of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icj-cij.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Court of JusticeCorfu Channel (United Kingdom of Great Britain and...A second Judgment, rendered on 9 April 1949, related...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1949</span></span></span>

The leading explanation is that Yugoslav naval vessels laid newly prepared German-made mines with Albanian knowledge or approval. ## Did Mehmet Shehu take his own life?

Mehmet Shehu was Albania’s prime minister from 1954 until 1981 and, for much of that period, the most powerful figure beneath Enver Hoxha. On 18 December 1981, Albanians were told that Shehu had killed himself after suffering a nervous crisis. Soon afterwards, the regime recast him as a foreign agent who had supposedly served a succession of hostile intelligence services.

The transformation was characteristic of Hoxha’s political system. Senior officials who fell from favour were not merely dismissed; their entire biographies could be rewritten. Shehu disappeared from official memory, members of his family were persecuted, and the government presented his alleged treachery as the final explanation for both his fall and his death. Contemporary foreign observers recognised that a serious leadership struggle had occurred, but they lacked reliable information capable of disproving the official account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1981-88v10/d427" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[state.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on state.gov.</span></span></span>

Three broad theories have persisted.

**Suicide after political defeat.** According to this interpretation, Hoxha confronted Shehu over a politically unsuitable engagement arranged for Shehu’s son. The dispute exposed deeper distrust inside the leadership. Facing humiliation, removal and likely punishment, Shehu shot himself. Some contemporary diplomatic reporting regarded suicide as plausible and noted that claims of a violent confrontation between Hoxha and Shehu were unsupported rumours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1981-88v10/d427" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on state.gov.</span></span></span>

**Murder ordered by Hoxha.** Shehu’s relatives and several later commentators have argued that he was killed because Hoxha feared him as a rival or needed a prominent enemy to justify another purge. Supporters of this theory point to alleged irregularities in the forensic record, inconsistent descriptions of the scene and the regime’s immediate control over the investigation. Later forensic interpretations have claimed that aspects of the wound and body position are more compatible with homicide, although such arguments depend heavily on records produced under dictatorship rather than a preserved, independently examined crime scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://memorie.al/en/scientific-evidence-proving-that-mehmet-shehus-death-was-murder-and-not-suicide-as-the-executioner-stayed-reflections-of-the-well-known-professor-of-forensic-medicine/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: memorie.al">[Memorie.al]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">memorie.al</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“Scientific evidence proving that Mehmet Shehu&#x27;s death</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“Scientific evidence proving that Mehmet Shehu&#x27;s death</span></span></span>

**A coerced or politically engineered suicide.** This middle explanation accepts that Shehu may have fired the fatal shot but treats his death as the result of a calculated political trap. In a system where arrest, execution and punishment of relatives were credible possibilities, the distinction between an entirely voluntary suicide and a death engineered by the leadership becomes morally and historically blurred.

No surviving evidence establishes murder beyond reasonable doubt. The official suicide version remains possible, but it cannot be treated as fully trustworthy because the same state that announced the death controlled the scene, the medical evidence, the witnesses and the archives. Shehu’s case endures because it condenses the central problem of communist Albanian history: the documentary record is extensive, yet much of it was produced to enforce a verdict rather than discover the truth.

## Where are the dictatorship’s missing dead?

The largest unresolved mystery in Albania is not the fate of one famous person but the whereabouts of thousands. The International Commission on Missing Persons estimates that roughly 6,000 people disappeared during communist rule between 1944 and 1991. They included executed political prisoners, people who died in detention and victims buried in unmarked or poorly recorded graves. Even the exact total remains uncertain because official registers are incomplete and different institutions counted victims in different ways.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/albania/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icmp.int.</span></span></span>

In many cases, families were never told where their relatives had been buried. Executions could take place in restricted areas, prison grounds, riverbanks or remote fields. Graves were left unmarked, while relatives were threatened or denied access. Later construction, agricultural work and the passage of time altered suspected sites. Records sometimes identify a sentence and date of execution without providing a dependable burial location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icmp.int/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/icmp-gr-wb-152-6-W-doc-albania-missing-persons-from-the-communist-era-a-needs-assesment.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icmp.int.</span></span></span>

This is not a conventional mystery in which investigators simply need to identify a clever culprit. The perpetrators and institutions are often broadly known. The uncertainty concerns the location and identification of the dead, the precise circumstances of individual deaths and the responsibility of particular officials. Solving it requires archival comparison, witness interviews, archaeological survey, exhumation and DNA matching with living relatives.

Progress has been slow. Albania ratified a cooperation agreement with the International Commission on Missing Persons to support identification through modern forensic methods, but assessments have continued to identify fragmented authority, weak coordination, inadequate investigations and limited implementation of existing legal duties. A 2024 policy review reported that a number of prosecution offices had recorded no proceedings under the relevant criminal provisions concerning enforced disappearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/press-releases/albania-ratifies-agreement-with-icmp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[icmp.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">albania ratifies agreement with icmp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">albania ratifies agreement with icmp</span></span></span>

The search nevertheless continues. In 2026, investigators reported evidence relating to several locations suspected of containing communist-era burials. Such discoveries may eventually resolve individual cases, but they also demonstrate how much work remains: locating a possible grave is only the beginning, because remains must be excavated properly, separated, scientifically examined and matched to families.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://balkaninsight.com/2026/04/03/suspected-burial-sites-of-communist-era-disappeared-found-in-albania/btj/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: balkaninsight.com">[Balkan Insight]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">balkaninsight.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on balkaninsight.com.</span></span></span>

The missing dead matter beyond private grief. Their absence affects how Albania understands state violence. A dictatorship can continue shaping public memory long after it falls when its victims remain unnamed, its burial sites are unknown and its archives cannot be fully trusted.

## What happened to Remzi Hoxha?

Remzi Hoxha, an ethnic Albanian businessman and political activist from what is now North Macedonia, was taken from his workplace in Tirana in October 1995. Witnesses said that men in civilian clothes drove him away. He was never seen by his family again, and his body has not been recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/ar/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/eur110042004en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[Amnesty International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Albania: What happened to Remzi Hoxha?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Albania: What happened to Remzi Hoxha?</span></span></span>

Unlike the disappearances of the communist period, Hoxha vanished after Albania had formally become a democracy. The case therefore became a test of whether the new state had truly broken with the secret-police practices of the past.

Investigations eventually concluded that Hoxha and two other men had been unlawfully detained and abused by officers of Albania’s post-communist intelligence service. The other detainees survived. Prosecutors alleged that Hoxha died as a result of torture and that his body was concealed. Ilir Kumbaro, a former intelligence official, was convicted in absentia in 2012 and sentenced to 15 years’ imprisonment, while other defendants were acquitted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amnesty.org/ru/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/eur110102006en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amnesty.org">[amnesty.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amnesty.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Albania: Remzi Hoxha</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Albania: Remzi Hoxha</span></span></span>

The court process answered part of the mystery: it placed responsibility for Hoxha’s detention and death within the security service. It did not establish where his remains were taken or provide his family with a burial place. Questions about motive have also persisted. Allegations have included suspicion of involvement in a plot against the Macedonian president and claims that Hoxha possessed politically damaging information, but no single motive has been proved as convincingly as the fact of official involvement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.balkanweb.com/en/i-biri-i-remzi-hoxhes-mora-informacion-per-nje-dokument-te-udb-me-178-emra-klodiana-lala-ne-dosjen-e-gjykates-thuhet-se-shish-ishte/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: balkanweb.com">[Balkanweb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">balkanweb.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on balkanweb.com.</span></span></span>

Hoxha’s case is especially significant because it bridges two eras. The methods associated with dictatorship—unrecorded detention, coercive interrogation and concealment—appeared in a formally democratic system. The mystery is therefore not merely where one man was buried. It is how much of the old security culture survived the change of regime, and which people or institutions prevented a full accounting.

## Did King Zog steal Albania’s gold?

When Italy invaded Albania in April 1939, King Zog fled across the border into Greece with his family and entourage. Communist-era accounts later depicted his departure as a straightforward theft: the king had supposedly abandoned the country while carrying away its national treasure.

The historical dispute is more complicated because several different categories of wealth became mixed together in public memory: Zog’s private property, funds legally assigned to the royal household, money released for government expenses during the emergency, and assets belonging to the Albanian state.

Documents publicised by Albania’s national archives in 2023 reported that border officials recorded 250,000 gold napoleons attributed to Zog, alongside additional sums associated with members of his entourage. Other archival interpretations calculate the funds in gold francs or by weight, producing figures that do not always align neatly because the records use different monetary units and may describe different holdings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://euronews.al/en/head-of-archive-why-we-published-the-secret-document-about-king-zogs-gold/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: euronews.al">[Euronews Albania]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">euronews.al</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Albania Head of archives: Why we published the secret document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Albania Head of archives: Why we published the secret document</span></span></span>

The unresolved issue is not simply whether Zog took gold. There is strong evidence that substantial wealth accompanied him into exile. The argument concerns ownership and authority: was it personal royal wealth, money legally drawn from the treasury, emergency state funds intended to maintain an Albanian government abroad, or outright appropriation?

Defenders of Zog note that Albania’s banking arrangements involved Italian control and that he could not simply have emptied every national reserve at will. They also argue that communist propaganda deliberately collapsed legal distinctions in order to present the monarchy as uniquely corrupt. Critics reply that legal paperwork does not settle the moral question of a monarch carrying scarce public resources abroad while the country was being occupied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://balkaninsight.com/2023/04/05/golden-touch-eight-decades-on-kings-theft-still-divides-albania/bi/all-balkan-countries/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: balkaninsight.com">[Balkan Insight]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">balkaninsight.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">all balkan countries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">all balkan countries</span></span></span>

A separate but connected gold dispute later reached the International Court of Justice. Nazi Germany had seized more than two tonnes of Albanian monetary gold from Rome in 1943. After the war, competing claims involving Albania, Italy and Britain became entangled with the unpaid Corfu Channel compensation. The resulting litigation and decades of negotiation helped turn “Albanian gold” into a cluster of overlapping stories rather than one easily traceable treasure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law%3Aepil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e175" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: opil.ouplaw.com">[OUP Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">opil.ouplaw.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">law 9780199231690 e175</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">law 9780199231690 e175</span></span></span>

The claim that Zog stole “all Albania’s gold” is therefore misleading. He carried a large quantity of wealth out of the country, but the precise legal status of every portion remains disputed. The story survived because it served opposing political memories: for communists, proof of royal betrayal; for monarchists, proof that propaganda had distorted a legitimate emergency transfer.

## How Berat’s lost codices were found

Not every Albanian mystery remains unresolved. The survival of the Berat codices shows how a disappearance can be explained without diminishing its drama.

The two celebrated Gospel manuscripts known as Beratinus 1 and Beratinus 2 date from approximately the sixth and ninth centuries. Beratinus 1 is written on purple-dyed parchment and belongs to a very small group of surviving “purple codices”. Both manuscripts are now recognised by UNESCO’s Memory of the World programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/codex-purpureus-beratinus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Codex Purpureus Beratinus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Codex Purpureus Beratinus</span></span></span>

For centuries, the manuscripts faced repeated danger from armies and would-be looters. During the Second World War, local clergy concealed them to prevent German forces from taking them. After liberation, Albanian scholars knew the codices were missing but could not discover their hiding place. Searches of the church initially failed.

The manuscripts were eventually recovered from a concealed space beneath the floor after those involved in hiding them revealed the secret. The episode became part of the codices’ modern history: priceless objects had apparently vanished, but their disappearance was an organised act of protection rather than theft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/albania-beratinus.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">albania beratinus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">albania beratinus</span></span></span>

The case is instructive because later retellings can make the recovery sound miraculous. The documented explanation is more convincing and more human. People facing occupation decided that secrecy was the only effective form of conservation. Their success created a temporary mystery and saved objects that might otherwise have been destroyed or removed permanently.

## Why Albania’s mysteries are difficult to solve

Several recurring conditions connect these cases.

**States controlled the first version of events.** Communist Albania decided what could be reported about Mehmet Shehu and political prisoners. Post-communist security officials were implicated in the disappearance of Remzi Hoxha. In such cases, official records are evidence, but they may also be instruments of concealment.

**Archives are uneven rather than simply closed or open.** A newly released document can clarify one detail while creating another dispute. The records concerning King Zog’s gold show that publication does not automatically produce agreement, particularly when documents use different measurements or were written by politically interested authorities.

**Foreign powers shaped Albanian mysteries.** The Corfu Channel case involved Britain, Yugoslavia and the emerging Cold War. Albania’s monetary gold passed through Italian, German and British control. International archives may therefore contain evidence absent from Albanian collections, but those governments also pursued their own strategic interests.

**Forensic evidence arrived late.** Graves were disturbed, bodies were hidden, crime scenes were controlled and witnesses died. DNA analysis and archaeological survey can now answer questions that paper records cannot, but only when burial sites can first be located and families are able to provide reference samples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icmp.int/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/icmp-gr-wb-152-6-W-doc-albania-missing-persons-from-the-communist-era-a-needs-assesment.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icmp.int.</span></span></span>

**Political myths simplify mixed evidence.** One side may insist that Zog stole everything; another that he took nothing improper. One account treats Shehu’s death as an obvious suicide; another as a proven assassination. The most credible historical judgement often lies between these absolutes: identifying what is documented, what is probable and what remains unsupported.

## What the evidence supports

The best-supported assessment of Albania’s major historical enigmas is cautious but not indecisive.

The [Corfu Channel mines]({{ 'corfu-mines/' | relative_url }}) were deliberately placed, and Albania almost certainly knew of the danger, but the surviving public evidence does not conclusively identify the full chain of command. Mehmet Shehu may have committed suicide under extreme political pressure, although the compromised investigation means murder cannot be ruled out. Thousands of communist-era victims remain missing because the regime concealed burial places and later institutions failed to conduct a sufficiently systematic search.

Remzi Hoxha was unlawfully detained by intelligence officers and is believed to have died in custody, yet the location of his body and the complete motive remain unknown. King Zog unquestionably carried substantial wealth into exile, but the familiar claim that he simply stole the entire national reserve confuses private, royal and state assets. The Berat codices, by contrast, present a largely resolved mystery: their disappearance was a successful wartime rescue.

Together, these cases reveal that Albania’s deepest enigmas concern custody of truth. Mines, bodies, gold and manuscripts all disappeared into systems of secrecy. Recovering them requires more than finding a hidden object or opening a file. It requires deciding who created the record, who benefited from its story, what evidence survived and which silences were deliberate.
