## Introduction

The mystery of Tarawa's missing war graves is not that the dead vanished without trace, but that many known battlefield burials disappeared from the historical record for decades. After the Battle of Tarawa on Betio Island in November 1943, hundreds of American Marines and sailors were buried in hastily dug cemeteries marked with simple wooden crosses. When recovery teams returned after the war, many of those graves could no longer be found, while others had been moved, altered or incorrectly recorded. As a result, hundreds of servicemen were officially listed as unrecoverable despite witnesses having buried them. Over the past two decades, archaeology, improved wartime record analysis and modern DNA testing have transformed this long-standing mystery into one of the most successful wartime identification efforts in the Pacific.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/Projects_WWII_Tarawa_InfoSheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span></span></span>

## Battlefield burials and post-war confusion

The Battle of Tarawa lasted only four days, from 20 to 23 November 1943, but it was among the bloodiest amphibious assaults of the Pacific War. More than 1,000 American servicemen were killed or went missing on the tiny island of Betio. Immediate battlefield conditions left little opportunity for carefully planned burials. Units interred their dead wherever fighting allowed, creating numerous temporary cemeteries across the island.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/Projects_WWII_Tarawa_InfoSheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span></span></span>

The mystery emerged after the war rather than during it. In 1946 the 604th Quartermaster Graves Registration Company returned to recover the dead. They expected to locate clearly marked cemeteries recorded during the battle. Instead, they encountered several problems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Some grave markers had disappeared.
* Road construction and military engineering had altered the landscape.
* Cemeteries had been relocated or landscaped by construction units after the battle.
* Original burial maps were incomplete, contradictory or inaccurate.
* Many bodies no longer had identifiable personal effects or readable identification tags.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/dpaaFamWebInTarawa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military Battle of Tarawa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military Battle of Tarawa</span></span></span>

</div>

The U.S. Navy's Seabees had improved and reorganised burial grounds during the occupation of Betio in 1944. Although done respectfully and for practical reasons, these changes unintentionally obscured the original wartime burial locations. Some grave markers no longer stood above the individuals they were intended to identify, while other marked graves proved to contain no remains at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/dpaaFamWebInTarawa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military Battle of Tarawa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military Battle of Tarawa</span></span></span>

Recovery teams nevertheless recovered hundreds of bodies. Between 1946 and the early 1950s, more than 400 servicemen were successfully identified. However, many others remained unidentified or were never located, and review boards eventually declared numerous casualties "non-recoverable".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/Projects_WWII_Tarawa_InfoSheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span></span></span>

## How lost cemeteries were rediscovered

For decades, many historians believed the missing graves had simply been destroyed or permanently lost beneath modern Betio. The breakthrough came when researchers began re-examining wartime records instead of assuming the original recovery missions had been complete.

Military historians compared:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* battlefield sketches;
* aerial photographs;
* engineering maps;
* eyewitness accounts;
* graves registration paperwork; and
* post-war recovery reports.

</div>

Rather than pointing to a single missing cemetery, these documents suggested that several burial grounds had either never been fully excavated or had been searched in the wrong locations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/Projects_WWII_Tarawa_InfoSheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span></span></span>

A major turning point occurred in 2015, when the non-profit organisation History Flight excavated the site of Cemetery 27 on Betio. Archaeologists recovered the remains of dozens of American servicemen from graves that had effectively disappeared from official records. Their discoveries demonstrated that at least some supposedly unrecoverable casualties had never actually been removed from Tarawa at all. The recovered remains were transferred to the U.S. Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency (DPAA) for scientific identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dpaa.mil/News-Stories/Our-Stories/Article/1043285/marine-missing-from-world-war-ii-identified-roberts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa.mil">[Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense POW/MIA Accounting AgencyMarine Missing From World War II Identified (Roberts) &gt; Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency &gt; Our StoriesJ...</span></span></span>

Subsequent work expanded to other lost burial areas, including Cemetery 26, Cemetery 33 and an area known as Row D, together with smaller undocumented burial sites identified through historical research and archaeological surveys.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/Projects_WWII_Tarawa_InfoSheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span></span></span>

## DNA, records and restored identities

Rediscovering graves solved only part of the mystery. Many recovered remains still lacked names because decades underground had destroyed identification tags and other personal belongings.

Modern forensic science has therefore become as important as archaeology. DPAA specialists combine several independent forms of evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* archaeological context;
* skeletal analysis;
* dental comparisons where records survive;
* wartime unit records;
* burial documentation;
* mitochondrial DNA inherited through the maternal line; and
* where possible, additional nuclear DNA comparisons with living relatives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/Projects_WWII_Tarawa_InfoSheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span></span></span>

</div>

Instead of relying on a single test, investigators build a cumulative case. A probable burial location, matching age and physical characteristics, surviving military records and DNA evidence together can produce a confident identification even after more than eighty years.

This multidisciplinary approach has steadily reduced the number of unknown casualties. Since the Tarawa Project formally began in 2016, DPAA has identified well over 150 servicemen connected with the battle, with additional identifications continuing as new evidence is analysed. The agency also exhumed all 94 unidentified Tarawa burials from the National Memorial Cemetery of the Pacific in Hawaii to allow modern forensic examination using techniques unavailable during the 1940s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/Projects_WWII_Tarawa_InfoSheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span></span></span>

Individual announcements illustrate how the process works. Marines such as Larry R. Roberts, James O. Whitehurst and Albert Tye had all been declared unrecoverable after the war. Following archaeological recoveries and laboratory analysis, including DNA testing where appropriate, each was eventually identified and returned to their families for burial under their own names.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dpaa.mil/News-Stories/Our-Stories/Article/1043285/marine-missing-from-world-war-ii-identified-roberts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa.mil">[Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense POW/MIA Accounting AgencyMarine Missing From World War II Identified (Roberts) &gt; Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency &gt; Our StoriesJ...</span></span></span>

## What remains uncertain?

Although the overall mystery has been greatly reduced, not every question has been answered.

Some servicemen were lost at sea during the assault and were never buried on Betio. Others may remain beneath areas that have since been heavily developed or altered. In some cases, surviving records are too fragmentary to associate recovered remains with a particular individual. DNA comparisons also depend upon locating suitable living relatives willing to provide reference samples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil/Projects_WWII_Tarawa_InfoSheet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil">[DPAA Military]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpaa-mil.sites.crmforce.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DPAA Military DPAA Tarawa Info Sheet</span></span></span>

The exact number of recoverable remains still on or around Tarawa therefore remains uncertain. Archaeological work continues whenever historical evidence suggests additional burial locations.

## Why the mystery still matters in Kiribati

Tarawa's missing war graves represent a different kind of historical mystery from many famous unsolved cases. There is little evidence of conspiracy or deliberate concealment. Instead, the puzzle arose through the chaos of combat, hurried wartime burials, changing landscapes and imperfect record-keeping.

For [Kiribati]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-kiribatis/' | relative_url }}), the story illustrates how a small Pacific island became the scene of one of the Second World War's most significant forensic investigations. Betio is both a modern community and a battlefield whose landscape still preserves traces of 1943 beneath roads, buildings and vegetation.

The continuing recoveries also demonstrate how historical mysteries can gradually be resolved. Improvements in archival research, archaeological methods and forensic genetics have allowed names to be restored to many servicemen once thought permanently lost.
