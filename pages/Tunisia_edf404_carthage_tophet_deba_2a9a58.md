## Introduction

Few questions in ancient Mediterranean history are as contentious as whether the people of Carthage, in present-day [Tunisia]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-tunisias/' | relative_url }}), deliberately sacrificed their children. The debate centres on the Carthage Tophet, an open-air sanctuary and burial ground containing thousands of urns filled with cremated remains of infants, young children and, in some cases, animals. Ancient Greek and Roman writers described Carthaginian child sacrifice in vivid and often shocking terms, but those authors were also political and military rivals of Carthage. Modern archaeology has therefore had to answer a difficult question: do the physical remains confirm the ancient accusations, or do they instead represent a specialised cemetery for infants who died naturally? More than a century of excavation has narrowed the range of plausible explanations, but it has not produced universal agreement. The mystery endures because the evidence is genuine yet unusually difficult to interpret.

## What archaeologists found at the Tophet

The Carthage Tophet lies just outside the ancient city's main residential area. Excavations revealed layer upon layer of urn burials accumulated over several centuries, from the Punic period until shortly before the Roman destruction of Carthage in 146 BC. Many urns contain cremated bones of infants or very young children, while others include the remains of lambs, kids or birds. Stone stelae erected above some burials bear religious symbols and inscriptions dedicated to deities, especially Baal Hammon and Tanit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 17, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 17, 2010</span></span></span>

Several features distinguish the site from an ordinary cemetery:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The burials were placed in urns after cremation rather than as intact bodies.
* Animal remains sometimes accompany human remains, suggesting ritual activity.
* The cemetery appears to have been reserved almost exclusively for very young individuals.
* Dedicatory inscriptions imply that at least some burials were connected with vows or offerings to the gods rather than simply marking a death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 17, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 17, 2010</span></span></span>

</div>

These characteristics explain why the Tophet became central to the debate. Few archaeologists dispute that it was a sacred place. The disagreement concerns what kind of sacred place it was.

## Why ancient accusations remain contested

The literary evidence comes mainly from Greek and Roman authors, including Diodorus Siculus, who described children being sacrificed during periods of crisis. Some accounts portray large bronze statues associated with ritual burning, while others claim that noble families offered their own children to secure divine favour.

These texts cannot simply be accepted at face value. Most surviving descriptions were written generations after the events they describe, often by writers whose societies had fought Carthage or regarded Punic religion as alien and barbaric. Carthage's own historical literature was largely destroyed when the city fell to Rome, leaving historians with an unusually one-sided written record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 17, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 17, 2010</span></span></span>

That imbalance creates a central historical problem. The accusations may preserve genuine information about Punic religious practice, but they also fit a familiar pattern in which enemies depict one another as exceptionally cruel. Historians therefore treat the literary evidence as important but insufficient on its own.

## What the bones can and cannot prove

The fiercest modern disagreement concerns the cremated skeletal remains themselves.

One influential study led by Jeffrey Schwartz argued that the age distribution of the children is broadly compatible with natural infant mortality. Because many appear to have died around birth or shortly afterwards, the researchers proposed that the Tophet may have served as a cemetery for infants who died naturally before they became full members of society. They also stressed that cremation shrinks and distorts bones, making precise age estimates extremely difficult. In their view, the skeletal evidence does not demonstrate systematic sacrifice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 17, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 17, 2010</span></span></span>

Other researchers, including Patricia Smith and Lawrence Stager, re-examined many of the same remains using different methods, particularly detailed analysis of developing teeth. They concluded that many children were older than the natural-death model predicted, with a concentration around one to one-and-a-half months of age. Combined with the ritual setting, inscriptions and associated animal offerings, they argued that infant sacrifice was the most convincing explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/aging-cremated-infants-the-problem-of-sacrifice-at-the-tophet-of-carthage/7F572DE957BE72A634867AC52E6BD2E8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAging cremated infants: the problem of sacrifice at the Tophet of Carthage &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge...</span></span></span>

The disagreement is not simply philosophical. It rests on technical questions including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* how much cremation alters infant bones and teeth;
* which anatomical measurements remain reliable after burning;
* how accurately newborn age can be estimated from fragmented remains;
* whether individual urns represent one child or multiple individuals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/aging-cremated-infants-the-problem-of-sacrifice-at-the-tophet-of-carthage/7F572DE957BE72A634867AC52E6BD2E8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAging cremated infants: the problem of sacrifice at the Tophet of Carthage &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge...</span></span></span>

</div>

Small differences in estimated age can produce very different historical interpretations. If most individuals died naturally around birth, the Tophet could represent a specialised infant cemetery. If significant numbers lived for weeks or months before death, deliberate ritual selection becomes more plausible.

## Why animal remains matter

One of the strongest archaeological arguments supporting ritual sacrifice is the repeated association of animal and human cremations.

In several urns, archaeologists identified the cremated remains of young sheep or goats alongside those of children. Animal sacrifice is well documented in many ancient Mediterranean religions, so supporters of the sacrifice interpretation argue that these mixed burials fit a broader sacrificial tradition rather than an ordinary cemetery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/cemetery-or-sacrifice-infant-burials-at-the-carthage-tophet/EA2F96A8FD7229800391B766C95ECBE1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentCemetery or sacrifice? Infant burials at the Carthage Tophet &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Core...</span></span></span>

Critics respond that the animal offerings may have been symbolic accompaniments to children who died naturally. They argue that ritual treatment after death does not necessarily reveal the cause of death itself. The presence of offerings therefore strengthens the religious interpretation of the site without conclusively demonstrating that every child was intentionally killed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 17, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 17, 2010</span></span></span>

## Why the inscriptions are difficult to interpret

Hundreds of stone stelae recovered from the Tophet bear short religious inscriptions recording dedications to Punic deities. These texts are among the few surviving voices from Carthaginian religion itself.

The inscriptions clearly show that worshippers believed they had fulfilled vows or received divine favour. What they do not explicitly state is whether the children buried beneath them were sacrificial victims, children who died naturally and were ceremonially dedicated, or individuals whose deaths became occasions for religious offerings.

Because the wording is brief and formulaic, scholars disagree over how much should be inferred from terms referring to offerings or fulfilled vows. The inscriptions therefore reinforce the site's sacred character while stopping short of resolving the central question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 17, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 17, 2010</span></span></span>

## Where the scholarly debate stands today

The modern debate has gradually shifted away from two extreme positions.

Few specialists now argue that every ancient accusation was simply invented Roman propaganda. The archaeological evidence clearly demonstrates that the Tophet was a distinctive ritual landscape unlike ordinary cemeteries, and the combination of cremation, dedications and animal offerings cannot be dismissed as routine burial practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/cemetery-or-sacrifice-infant-burials-at-the-carthage-tophet/EA2F96A8FD7229800391B766C95ECBE1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentCemetery or sacrifice? Infant burials at the Carthage Tophet &#124; Antiquity &#124; Cambridge Core...</span></span></span>

Equally, many researchers caution against assuming that every child buried there was sacrificed. Burned infant remains rarely preserve evidence capable of identifying an exact cause of death. Archaeologists can often estimate age, but they cannot usually determine whether a child died naturally before cremation or was intentionally killed.

As a result, current scholarship tends to focus on narrower questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* How frequently, if at all, were children sacrificed?
* Did the practice occur only during periods of crisis?
* Were some Tophet burials natural deaths while others reflected ritual killing?
* How representative is the Carthage Tophet of Punic practice across the wider Mediterranean?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/bones-teeth-and-estimating-age-of-perinates-carthaginian-infant-sacrifice-revisited/CA46F625ED28CFD23EFB5A71D06BCF48" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentBones, teeth, and estimating age of perinates: Carthaginian infant sacrifice revisited &#124; Antiquity...</span></span></span>

</div>

Rather than debating a simple yes-or-no question, researchers increasingly investigate the scale, circumstances and variability of any sacrificial practice.

## Why the mystery still matters

The Tophet debate remains one of Tunisia's most important archaeological controversies because it illustrates how history is reconstructed when evidence is incomplete and deeply contested. The destruction of Carthage removed most of the civilisation's own written explanations, forcing historians to weigh hostile literary accounts against difficult archaeological evidence.

The result is an enduring historical mystery built not on missing artefacts but on competing interpretations of the same physical remains. Scientific methods have greatly improved understanding of the bones, teeth and burial practices, yet they have not eliminated uncertainty. The Carthage Tophet therefore stands as a reminder that archaeology often answers broad questions while leaving the most human one unresolved: whether the children whose ashes filled those urns died by tragedy, by disease, or by ritual.
