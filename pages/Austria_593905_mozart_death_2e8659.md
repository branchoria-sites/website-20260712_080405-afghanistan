## Introduction

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart died in Vienna on 5 December 1791 at the age of just 35. His sudden death quickly became one of [Austria]({{ 'austria/' | relative_url }})'s most enduring historical mysteries, not because there is strong evidence of murder, but because the surviving medical evidence is too limited to identify his illness with confidence. The famous claim that he was poisoned—especially by the Italian composer Antonio Salieri—has become one of the best-known stories in classical music. Yet historians and medical researchers overwhelmingly regard the poisoning theory as unsupported. What remains uncertain is not whether Mozart was murdered, but precisely which natural disease caused his rapid decline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1960696/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med The medical history and death of Mozart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The medical history and death of Mozart - PubMed...</span></span></span>

## Mozart's final illness and recorded symptoms

Mozart remained professionally active until shortly before becoming ill. In November 1791 he was conducting performances, supervising work on *The Magic Flute* and composing the unfinished *Requiem*. Contemporary accounts suggest that his fatal illness lasted around two weeks.

Descriptions from relatives and acquaintances mention symptoms including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* high fever;
* vomiting;
* weakness and exhaustion;
* pain;
* swelling of the hands and feet, and possibly more extensive swelling;
* an inability to move comfortably in bed during his final days.

</div>

The official recorded cause of death was "acute miliary fever" (or "heated miliary fever"), a broad eighteenth-century medical label rather than a diagnosis in the modern sense. It referred to a fever accompanied by a rash resembling millet seeds and was commonly used for several different illnesses. It therefore tells historians almost nothing about the precise disease that killed him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1472719/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span></span></span>

One major obstacle is the lack of detailed clinical records. Mozart's physicians left no comprehensive medical notes, and most surviving descriptions were written years later by family members whose memories were imperfect. The composer was also buried in accordance with Viennese practice of the period, meaning there is no authenticated body available for modern forensic examination. Claims that an alleged skull belonged to Mozart have not been accepted as reliable evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1472719/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span></span></span>

## Why did people think Mozart had been poisoned?

Rumours of poisoning appeared surprisingly soon after Mozart's death. Several factors encouraged suspicion.

First, Mozart was young and exceptionally famous. His death seemed difficult for admirers to accept as the result of an ordinary infectious illness.

Second, according to later recollections, Mozart himself became convinced during his final illness that he had been poisoned. Historians treat these statements cautiously because they come through later testimony rather than contemporary medical documentation, and they may reflect anxiety or delirium during severe illness rather than evidence of foul play.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1472719/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span></span></span>

Finally, Vienna's competitive musical world made it easy for rumours to attach themselves to a rival. Antonio Salieri, a successful court composer, became the principal target despite the absence of credible evidence connecting him to Mozart's death.

## Why the Salieri theory became famous

The idea that Salieri murdered Mozart owes far more to literature and theatre than to historical evidence.

Rumours circulated during Salieri's lifetime and intensified after reports that the elderly composer, suffering from severe illness and mental decline, had allegedly confessed to poisoning Mozart before later denying it. Contemporary witnesses considered these stories unreliable, yet they proved impossible to suppress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bmj.com/rapid-response/2011/11/03/mozarts-140-causes-death-and-27-mental-disorders" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bmj.com">[BMJ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bmj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mozarts 140 causes death and 27 mental disorders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mozart&#x27;s 140 causes of death and 27 mental disorders &#124; The BMJJanuary 2, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 2, 2011</span></span></span>

The legend gained lasting influence through a series of artistic works:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Alexander Pushkin's 1830 dramatic poem *Mozart and Salieri* transformed jealousy into a moral tragedy.
* Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov later adapted Pushkin's work into an opera.
* Peter Shaffer's 1979 play *Amadeus* and the acclaimed 1984 film adaptation brought the fictional rivalry to an international audience.

</div>

These works never claimed to be literal history. Shaffer's drama deliberately used Salieri as a symbolic narrator exploring genius, envy and faith. Nevertheless, many viewers came away believing the central accusation reflected historical fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ft.com/content/3f0d78a9-0d80-409a-9774-f9aba372cf77" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ft.com">[Financial Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mozart died at 35 in 1791, likely from mundane causes like infection or exhaustion. Ironically, surviving evidence suggests that Mozart and Salieri's relationship was considerably more complex than the myth allows. They competed professionally, as many composers did, but Salieri also conducted Mozart's music and later taught Mozart's son Franz Xaver. Nothing in the surviving documentary record demonstrates a murder plot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1472719/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span></span></span>

## What modern medical studies can and cannot prove

Medical historians have proposed well over a hundred different explanations for Mozart's death, ranging from infectious diseases to kidney disorders, rheumatic fever, trichinosis and other rare conditions. The sheer number of diagnoses illustrates how little reliable clinical information survives rather than indicating genuine support for dozens of competing theories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bmj.com/content/341/bmj.c6789" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bmj.com">[BMJ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bmj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mozart’s 140 causes of death and 27 mental disorders &#124; The BMJ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mozart’s 140 causes of death and 27 mental disorders &#124; The BMJ...</span></span></span>

Several broad conclusions have emerged.

First, most researchers agree that Mozart died from an acute natural illness rather than a long-term chronic disease. Evidence from his correspondence and professional activity indicates that he remained remarkably productive until shortly before his collapse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1960696/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med The medical history and death of Mozart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The medical history and death of Mozart - PubMed...</span></span></span>

Second, some modern researchers have argued that an epidemic streptococcal infection circulating in Vienna could explain both his symptoms and the timing of his death, possibly leading to kidney complications. This remains a plausible hypothesis rather than a proven diagnosis because the evidence is indirect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bmj.com/content/341/bmj.c6789" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bmj.com">[BMJ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bmj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mozart’s 140 causes of death and 27 mental disorders &#124; The BMJ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mozart’s 140 causes of death and 27 mental disorders &#124; The BMJ...</span></span></span>

Third, there is no convincing medical evidence for poisoning. Proposed poisons such as arsenic or mercury fail to match the surviving descriptions consistently, and no physical remains exist to test scientifically. The absence of forensic evidence means poisoning cannot be disproved with absolute certainty, but neither can it be supported by credible historical or medical data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1960696/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med The medical history and death of Mozart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The medical history and death of Mozart - PubMed...</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery remains unsolved

Mozart's death remains a genuine historical mystery because the available evidence has permanent limitations.

The principal uncertainties include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the absence of detailed clinical records;
* contradictory or incomplete eyewitness recollections;
* eighteenth-century medical terminology that cannot be translated into a precise modern diagnosis;
* the lack of authenticated remains for forensic analysis.

</div>

These gaps mean historians cannot confidently identify a single disease, even though many proposed illnesses fit parts of the surviving evidence.

The mystery therefore differs from popular conspiracy theories. The unresolved question is medical rather than criminal: historians continue to debate *what illness* killed Mozart, not *whether someone murdered him*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1472719/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span></span></span>

## How the poisoning myth became part of Austria's cultural memory

The poisoning story has endured because it offers a dramatic explanation for an event that was probably much more ordinary. A healthy-looking musical genius dying at 35 appears emotionally unsatisfying without a villain, while the image of jealous mediocrity destroying extraordinary talent has proved irresistible to generations of writers, dramatists and filmmakers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ft.com/content/3f0d78a9-0d80-409a-9774-f9aba372cf77" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ft.com">[Financial Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mozart died at 35 in 1791, likely from mundane causes like infection or exhaustion. However, cultural narratives have long resisted accep...</span></span></span>

For Austria's history, the case illustrates an important distinction between documented uncertainty and enduring myth. The exact medical cause of Mozart's death remains unknowable with present evidence, making it a legitimate historical puzzle. The accusation that Salieri poisoned him, however, belongs largely to the history of literature, theatre and popular imagination rather than to the historical record. That contrast—between an unresolved diagnosis and a largely discredited murder legend—is what keeps Mozart's death one of Austria's most fascinating historical mysteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1472719/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCMozart—portrait and myth</span></span></span>
