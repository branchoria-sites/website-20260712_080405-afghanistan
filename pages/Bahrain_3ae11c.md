## Introduction

These are genuine archaeological puzzles rather than evidence of a lost super-civilisation or hidden conspiracy. Written records about Dilmun usually come from foreign societies, especially Mesopotamia, while Bahrain’s inhabitants left no substantial surviving historical narrative of their own. Archaeologists must therefore reconstruct their world from buildings, graves, pottery, seals, imported goods and fragmentary inscriptions. That evidence has solved some old riddles while making others more precise.

## Was Bahrain really the land of Dilmun?

Dilmun appears in Mesopotamian records as both a real trading region and a distant place of abundance in religious literature. Early references connect it with merchants, imported commodities and maritime exchange. Later literary compositions describe an idealised Dilmun associated with purity, freshwater and divine life. For generations, scholars debated whether the name referred to Bahrain, part of eastern Arabia, an area farther north in the Gulf or a wider network rather than one island.

Excavation has largely settled the central question. Qal’at al-Bahrain contains successive occupation layers beginning around 2300 BC and includes houses, public buildings, warehouses, religious structures and fortifications. UNESCO identifies the site as an ancient harbour and capital of Dilmun, although only about a quarter of the large archaeological mound has been excavated. Bahrain was probably not the whole of Dilmun at every point in its history. Archaeological and textual evidence indicates that Dilmun’s political and commercial influence extended into neighbouring parts of the Gulf, including areas of eastern Arabia and, during some periods, islands farther north. It is therefore safer to describe Bahrain as Dilmun’s principal centre during its Early Bronze Age height than to treat the ancient name as an exact equivalent of the modern state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/archaeology-of-prehistoric-arabia/eastern-arabia-from-3000-to-2000-bc/54EA7C3327C9DD64E0BE5899A82FF990" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">As early as 1880 it was argued that the toponym &#x27;Dilmun&#x27;, which occurs in Late Uruk (Uruk IV: 3400–3100 BC)...Read more...</span></span></span>

The strongest evidence comes from trade. Objects and seals link Bahrain with southern Mesopotamia, the copper-producing regions of what is now Oman, Iran and the Indus civilisation of South Asia. “Gulf-type” seals found across these regions suggest the movement of merchants as well as goods. Some carry signs related to the still-undeciphered Indus script; later examples appear to imitate writing without preserving clearly recognisable Indus forms. Whether these marks represented a local language, a foreign commercial script or prestigious pseudo-writing remains unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumstamp-sealSuch seals are found largely in Mesopotamia and Bahrain, and more sporadically in the Indus Valley and Iran, demo...</span></span></span>

The surviving picture is therefore clear in outline but incomplete in detail. Bahrain was not an imaginary island invented by Mesopotamian poets. It was a wealthy Gulf entrepôt whose rulers and merchants connected distant economies. What remains uncertain is the changing extent of Dilmun, the identity and language of its people, and the exact relationship between the historical trading state and the idealised land described in religious literature.

## Why did Bahrain have so many burial mounds?

The burial fields are Bahrain’s most visible archaeological enigma. During the Early Dilmun period, particularly between about 2050 and 1750 BC, communities constructed vast numbers of stone-and-earth tombs across the western part of the main island. The UNESCO World Heritage property preserves 21 archaeological components containing 11,774 mounds, including extensive cemeteries and monumental graves associated with an elite.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1542/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDilmun Burial Mounds6 Jul 2019 — The Dilmun Burial Mounds is a serial property formed by 21 archaeological si...</span></span></span>

Older estimates for the original number of mounds vary dramatically, sometimes reaching well above 100,000. Those figures should not be treated as a precise ancient census. Many mounds were damaged or removed before systematic mapping, while early travellers sometimes counted natural features, overlapping remains or poorly defined groups. Modern documentation is more dependable for surviving and archaeologically verified sites than broad reconstructions of the original total.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iraq/article/bronze-age-burial-mounds-on-bahrain/12B590C1524C837C59B1F5B8A30DE603" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Even the securely identified cemeteries are extraordinary for such a small island. Several explanations have been proposed:

* **A large local population.** Dilmun’s commercial success may have supported densely settled farming, craft and trading communities whose size is underestimated because many residential areas remain unexcavated or lie beneath later development.
* **Burial over several generations.** The mounds were not created at once. Their accumulation over roughly three centuries greatly reduces the population required to produce them.
* **A regional cemetery centre.** Some scholars have considered whether people from elsewhere in the Gulf were brought to Bahrain for burial. Direct proof for mass transport of the dead is limited, but Bahrain’s religious and commercial importance makes some non-local burial plausible.
* **Migration into a trading hub.** Recent isotope research, which compares chemical signatures in human teeth and bones with local geology, has identified a meaningful minority of probable non-local individuals in archaeological populations from Bahrain. The findings support mobility and immigration, although they do not show that most of the dead came from overseas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s12520-024-01935-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on doi.org.</span></span></span>

The shape and contents of the mounds also reveal a society becoming more unequal. Most were modest individual tombs, but a smaller number had larger chambers, ring walls or monumental forms. The exceptional “royal” mounds at A’ali were built on a far grander scale and are interpreted as burials for rulers or the highest elite. Their architecture suggests that Dilmun’s commercial growth was accompanied by the consolidation of political authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227719765_Early_Dilmun_and_its_rulers_New_evidence_of_the_burial_mounds_of_the_elite_and_the_development_of_social_complexity_c_2200-1750_BC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Grave goods, body positions and chamber plans vary, making it difficult to reconstruct a single Dilmun belief about death. Robbery, ancient disturbance and later destruction have removed much evidence. The enduring puzzle is therefore not whether the mounds were tombs—they clearly were—but how Bahrain sustained the population, labour organisation and political system required to create one of the densest surviving prehistoric funerary landscapes in the world.

## What happened at the temples and sacred springs?

Freshwater was central to ancient Bahrain. Underground aquifers once fed natural springs on land and, in some places, beneath the shallow sea. In an arid region, reliable water made agriculture and permanent settlement possible. It also appears to have shaped Dilmun’s religious identity.

The clearest example is the temple complex at Barbar. Archaeologists uncovered three major temples constructed successively on the same site during the third and early second millennia BC. At the heart of the complex was a sunken chamber built around a freshwater spring. Carefully made stairs, channels, platforms and offering areas indicate that access to the water formed part of organised ceremony rather than ordinary domestic use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://culture.gov.bh/en/visitingbahrain/CulturalTourism/Destinations/Name%2C10329%2Cen.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: culture.gov.bh">[Culture Bahrain]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">culture.gov.bh</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Culture Bahrain Destinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Culture Bahrain Destinations</span></span></span>

The spring chamber is often interpreted in relation to Mesopotamian beliefs about a subterranean freshwater realm associated with a god of water and wisdom. This is plausible because Dilmun traded intensively with Mesopotamia and because water plays a prominent role in literary descriptions of Dilmun. It is not, however, proved by a local inscription naming the deity worshipped at Barbar. The identification rests on architecture, comparative religion and imported cultural connections.

Animal bones, ash, altars and deposited objects indicate sacrifice or ritual feasting. A copper bull’s head discovered at the site is one of Bahrain’s best-known ancient objects, but its exact role remains unknown. It may have decorated ritual furniture, a ceremonial vessel or another object whose perishable parts have disappeared. Scientific analysis can establish aspects of the metal and manufacturing process, yet it cannot by itself recover the object’s meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X20301814" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

Other water installations deepen the mystery. Wells and constructed pools at sites such as Ain Umm Sujoor have been interpreted as places where water use, craft activity and worship overlapped. The difficulty is distinguishing a sanctuary from an elaborate communal water facility when both could contain similar stonework, rooms and channels.

The most responsible conclusion is that water was both practical and sacred in Dilmun. Temples may have controlled access to springs, organised offerings and linked political authority to the source of agricultural fertility. Precisely who performed the ceremonies, which gods were addressed and what participants believed about the underground water remain beyond the surviving evidence.

## Why did Dilmun’s golden age end?

Dilmun did not disappear in a single catastrophe. The archaeological record instead shows changes in settlement, trade and political control over several centuries. Its greatest expansion followed the growth of long-distance exchange around the turn of the second millennium BC, when Bahrain occupied a valuable position between Mesopotamia, Oman and the Indus region.

That system was vulnerable because it depended on distant partners. The Indus civilisation underwent major urban and economic transformation after about 1900 BC. Political conditions changed in Mesopotamia, while production and trade networks elsewhere in the Gulf were reorganised. As routes, suppliers and commercial institutions shifted, Bahrain’s role as the preferred intermediary became less profitable. Archaeological scholarship generally treats the decline as a restructuring of interconnected economies rather than the result of one invasion, drought or natural disaster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/archaeology-of-prehistoric-arabia/eastern-arabia-from-2000-to-1300-bc/4BE7F3FD2DBC3AB771875EFADF3BF9F6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

The burial record appears to reflect this transformation. Monumental construction and the clearest signs of concentrated elite power diminished. Some settlements were abandoned or reduced, and the highly distinctive material culture of Early Dilmun changed. Yet Bahrain remained inhabited, and the name Dilmun continued to appear in later records. Letters from the later second millennium BC indicate political connections with Babylonian power, showing that the island had not simply become empty or forgotten.

Several explanations probably worked together:

**Commercial displacement.** Bahrain’s wealth depended heavily on controlling or servicing trade. Direct routes and new political arrangements could weaken its intermediary role.

**Instability among trading partners.** Disruption in one region affected demand and supply across the whole Gulf network.

**Changing political control.** Dilmun’s rulers may have lost independence or become subordinate to larger Mesopotamian states.

**Environmental pressure.** Changes in water availability or agricultural conditions may have contributed, but there is no secure evidence for a single environmental event that destroyed the civilisation.

The mystery persists partly because “collapse” is too simple a word. Dilmun’s institutions and international prominence declined, but communities continued living in Bahrain. Later powers reused the same strategic harbour at Qal’at al-Bahrain, adding new settlements and fortifications over ancient remains. The site’s occupation lasted into the sixteenth century, turning the former Dilmun capital into a layered record of repeated political reinvention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1192/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The strata of the 300 × 600 m tell testify...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Did Bahrain inspire the Garden of Eden?

The claim that Bahrain was the biblical Garden of Eden is one of the country’s most persistent historical legends. It arises from a genuine connection, but the popular conclusion goes well beyond the evidence.

In Mesopotamian literature, Dilmun can appear as a clean, bright and life-giving land associated with divine figures and freshwater. Because Bahrain is strongly identified with historical Dilmun, writers have compared these descriptions with the garden in the Book of Genesis. Bahrain’s former abundance of springs and date gardens makes the comparison especially attractive.

Similarity is not identity. The Mesopotamian compositions and Genesis belong to different textual traditions, and descriptions of ideal lands were not geographical surveys. Even when myths borrowed from or responded to earlier traditions, that does not mean their settings corresponded to one identifiable archaeological location. No inscription from Bahrain calls the island Eden, and no excavated structure can verify the events of a creation story.

The sensible distinction is between three propositions:

1. **Historical Dilmun was centred on Bahrain.** This is well supported by archaeology.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.archaeology.wiki/blog/2013/05/23/discovering-dilmun/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeology.wiki">[archaeology.wiki]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeology.wiki</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discovering Dilmun</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discovering Dilmun</span></span></span>
2. **Mesopotamian writers sometimes portrayed Dilmun as an ideal or sacred land.** This is supported by ancient literature.
3. **Bahrain was the literal Garden of Eden.** This is speculative and cannot be demonstrated from the available evidence.

The Eden association nevertheless matters because it has shaped Bahrain’s modern public image. It turns archaeological discoveries—springs, temples, gardens and a wealthy island civilisation—into elements of a familiar origin story. The result is memorable, but it can obscure the more interesting reality: Dilmun was remarkable not because it was a supernatural paradise, but because its inhabitants built a sophisticated maritime trading society in a strategically placed and unusually well-watered part of the Gulf.

## The Tree of Life: mystery or resilient biology?

Bahrain’s celebrated Tree of Life is often presented as an unexplained survival: a mature tree standing in an exposed desert area with no obvious stream or irrigation channel. Its isolation has encouraged stories connecting it with ancient sacred landscapes and even the Garden of Eden.

The tree is genuinely old, although the frequently repeated claim that it dates precisely to 1583 is less secure than tourist retellings suggest. An investigation into the supposed tree-ring study behind that date found physical evidence that the trunk had been cored, but no readily traceable published scientific report establishing the exact year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nautil.us/you-need-to-know-about-bahrains-loneliest-tree-235825" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nautil.us">[Nautilus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nautil.us</span><span class="citation-popover-title">us You Need to Know About Bahrain&#x27;s Loneliest Tree</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">us You Need to Know About Bahrain&#x27;s Loneliest Tree</span></span></span>

Its survival does not require a supernatural explanation. Deep-rooted desert trees can exploit groundwater unavailable at the surface, while humidity, occasional rainfall and extensive root systems may provide additional moisture. The surrounding terrain is not completely devoid of subsurface water or other vegetation. The real scientific questions concern the precise depth and route of its water supply, its verified age and how it has endured changing local conditions.

The Tree of Life is therefore best understood as a partly documented natural puzzle amplified by modern folklore. It is mysterious in the ordinary sense that its exact water source and age are not fully published or popularly understood. It is not evidence of a magical spring, an ancient secret technology or the physical remains of Eden.

## What remains genuinely unresolved?

Bahrain’s archaeology has answered the largest nineteenth- and twentieth-century question: the island was a major centre of Dilmun. The remaining mysteries are narrower but historically richer.

Researchers still lack a locally written political history naming Dilmun’s rulers in sequence. The spoken language of much of the population remains uncertain. Signs on some seals cannot be confidently read, while the relationship between local identity and foreign merchant communities is still being reconstructed. Large parts of Qal’at al-Bahrain and other settlements remain unexcavated, so the organisation of government, trade and everyday neighbourhood life is only partly visible.

The burial mounds preserve evidence of hierarchy, migration and family life, but their original number and the full range of people interred within them cannot be recovered with certainty. Temple architecture shows that water and ritual were closely connected, yet the ceremonies themselves were never recorded in a surviving local explanation. Dilmun’s decline can be placed within wider changes in Gulf trade, but no document describes the decision-making, conflict or human experience behind that transformation.

These gaps are not invitations to invent a vanished advanced civilisation. They are the normal result of studying a society through damaged monuments and records written mainly by outsiders. Bahrain’s enduring historical enigma is more grounded and more revealing: a small island became a central meeting point of the Bronze Age world, accumulated extraordinary wealth and funerary monuments, and then changed so thoroughly that later generations remembered its name more clearly than the lives of the people who built it.
