## Introduction

The central mystery surrounding the death of Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III is not whether colonial forces were responsible for the violence on Black Saturday, 28 December 1929. That much is well established. The enduring question is narrower and more troubling: which officer fired the rifle shot that fatally wounded [Samoa]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-samoas-greatest/' | relative_url }})'s leading independence figure, and why was no individual ever held legally accountable?

The shooting became one of the defining moments in Samoa's struggle for self-government under New Zealand's League of Nations mandate. Contemporary investigations accepted that police gunfire killed Tamasese, yet they stopped short of identifying the officer responsible. The absence of criminal prosecutions, combined with conflicting eyewitness accounts and an official response focused on restoring colonial authority rather than assigning personal responsibility, has left a lasting historical grievance that remains inseparable from the story of Black Saturday.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Black Saturday &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Saturday &#124; NZ HistoryNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

## The Mau movement and the events of Black Saturday

By late 1929, the Mau movement had become the principal expression of Samoan opposition to New Zealand's colonial administration. Its strategy relied largely on organised non-violent resistance, including boycotts, refusals to cooperate with colonial authorities and public demonstrations. The administration, however, increasingly viewed the movement as a challenge to its legitimacy.

On the morning of 28 December 1929, Mau supporters gathered in Apia to welcome members returning from exile. Colonial officials had already warned that they intended to arrest wanted Mau leaders if they appeared in the procession. When police attempted to seize the Mau secretary, Mataʻūtia Karaunu, marchers resisted and a struggle developed into wider violence. Police fell back towards the station while firing revolvers, and during the retreat Constable Abraham was separated from his colleagues and beaten to death by members of the crowd.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Black Saturday &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Saturday &#124; NZ HistoryNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

As the confrontation intensified, a police sergeant used a Lewis machine gun from the police station balcony. According to the official account, the machine-gun fire was deliberately directed above the crowd as a warning. At the same time, several policemen fired rifles into the advancing Samoans. It was this rifle fire—not the machine gun—that mortally wounded Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III, who witnesses consistently described as attempting to calm the crowd rather than encourage violence. He later died from his wounds after reportedly urging his followers to maintain peace rather than seek revenge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Black Saturday &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Saturday &#124; NZ HistoryNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

## Police responsibility and the disputed fatal gunfire

The broad responsibility for the killings has never been seriously disputed by historians. Police opened fire, and Tamasese was struck by police bullets. The unresolved issue is individual responsibility.

Several factors contributed to the uncertainty:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Multiple officers fired rifles during a rapidly changing confrontation.
* Contemporary forensic techniques were incapable of matching bullets to particular weapons.
* Witnesses were scattered, frightened and often focused on escaping rather than identifying individual shooters.
* Official investigations concentrated on whether police actions were justified rather than establishing which officer fired which shot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Black Saturday &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Saturday &#124; NZ HistoryNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

</div>

The machine gun has attracted particular attention because of its dramatic symbolism. Many Samoan witnesses believed it had been fired directly into the crowd. The official coroner rejected this interpretation, concluding that the machine gun had been aimed over people's heads and that the fatal wounds were caused by rifle fire instead. This disagreement has persisted in public memory, although the best-supported historical evidence continues to indicate that Tamasese was killed by rifle bullets fired by police rather than by the Lewis gun itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Black Saturday &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Saturday &#124; NZ HistoryNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

The identity of the rifleman, however, was never conclusively established. Official records refer only to several policemen firing. No definitive witness testimony, ballistic evidence or admission identified the officer whose shot proved fatal.

## Why no officer was prosecuted

The absence of prosecutions reflected more than evidential uncertainty. It also reflected the priorities of the colonial administration.

The coroner's inquiry accepted that police had fired the fatal shots. It concluded that although the rifle fire was understandable from the officers' perspective, it had not actually been necessary. This finding criticised the decision to use lethal force without translating that criticism into criminal responsibility for any individual officer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Black Saturday &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Saturday &#124; NZ HistoryNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

Several factors made accountability unlikely:

* **Collective rather than individual responsibility:** Police acted as a unit during a public-order operation, making it easier institutionally to defend the force as a whole.
* **Colonial legal structures:** The investigation was conducted within the same governing system that had authorised the crackdown on the Mau.
* **Political considerations:** The administration's immediate objective became suppressing the Mau movement rather than examining possible criminal liability among police.
* **Practical evidential limits:** Even if prosecutors had wished to proceed, proving beyond reasonable doubt which rifleman fired the fatal shot would have been difficult under the investigative standards of the period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Black Saturday &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Saturday &#124; NZ HistoryNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

Rather than pursuing prosecutions, Administrator Stephen Allen intensified the campaign against the Mau. The organisation was declared seditious, its symbols were outlawed, and hundreds of supporters fled into the bush while New Zealand forces—including naval personnel—conducted armed operations to break the movement. For many Samoans, these actions reinforced the perception that the government sought to punish victims rather than investigate the killings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Black Saturday &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Saturday &#124; NZ HistoryNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why the unanswered question still matters

The historical mystery is therefore not one of complete ignorance. Historians are confident that colonial police gunfire killed Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III during Black Saturday. The uncertainty lies at the level of personal accountability.

This distinction matters because it shapes how the event is remembered. It is not a mystery comparable to an unexplained disappearance or an unsolved archaeological puzzle. Instead, it is an example of institutional failure: responsibility was acknowledged in general terms, while individual responsibility remained unresolved.

The case has also become a symbol of wider colonial injustice. In 2002, New Zealand Prime Minister Helen Clark formally apologised to Samoa for injustices committed during New Zealand's administration, explicitly including the shooting of peaceful demonstrators in 1929. The apology recognised governmental responsibility but, naturally, could not answer the narrower historical question of exactly who fired the fatal rifle shot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://teara.govt.nz/en/samoans/page-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: teara.govt.nz">[Te Ara Encyclopedia of New Zealand]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">teara.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Te Ara Encyclopedia of New ZealandHistory and migration &#124; Te Ara Encyclopedia of New ZealandMarch 4, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2009</span></span></span>

For modern historians, the most persuasive conclusion is therefore twofold. The evidence clearly establishes that Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III was killed by police rifle fire during Black Saturday. What remains permanently uncertain is which officer fired the fatal bullet and why the colonial justice system never pursued personal accountability despite accepting that lethal force had been used unnecessarily. That unresolved gap continues to make Black Saturday one of Samoa's most significant historical controversies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/samoa/black-saturday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nzhistory.govt.nz">[NZHistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nzhistory.govt.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NZHistory Black Saturday &#124; NZ History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Saturday &#124; NZ HistoryNovember 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2025</span></span></span>
