## Introduction

These cases differ sharply in evidential quality. Bishop’s missing remains are an archival and forensic puzzle supported by official documents. The *Island Queen* is a genuine maritime disappearance, but several popular explanations remain unproved. Fédon’s end is obscured by war, pursuit and legend. The petroglyphs are real archaeological monuments whose makers and purpose can be studied, although not identified with certainty. Together, they show how Grenadian mysteries often arise not from a total absence of evidence, but from evidence that is damaged, scattered or open to competing interpretations.

## Where are Maurice Bishop’s remains?

On 19 October 1983, a crisis inside Grenada’s People’s Revolutionary Government ended with the execution of Maurice Bishop and seven other people at Fort Rupert, now Fort George. Those killed with him included government ministers and Bishop’s partner, Jacqueline Creft. Testimony examined after the event indicated that their bodies were taken to the military camp at Calivigny, burned and buried in an unmarked pit. Six days later, United States and Caribbean forces invaded Grenada.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/documents/ba00caeb-8b0e-43cd-8d9b-7e554643e1b5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington Post</span></span></span>

The central mystery is not whether Bishop died. His execution is firmly established. The puzzle is what happened after American military personnel exhumed badly burned and mixed human remains at Calivigny on 8 November 1983. Contemporary officials suspected that the pit contained Bishop and his companions, and a United States Armed Forces Institute of Pathology team was sent to examine the material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/documents/ba00caeb-8b0e-43cd-8d9b-7e554643e1b5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington Post</span></span></span>

The forensic examination, conducted at a St George’s University medical facility on 10 and 11 November, found the remains of at least five people. Their condition was so poor that the examiners could not identify Bishop conclusively. However, personal effects linked to cabinet officials killed alongside him were reportedly found among the remains. This makes it highly likely that at least some victims from the Fort Rupert executions were in the Calivigny pit, even though the surviving evidence did not permit individual identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/documents/ba00caeb-8b0e-43cd-8d9b-7e554643e1b5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington Post</span></span></span>

A 2024 letter from the United States Secretary of the Army offered the clearest official reconstruction yet. It stated that the remains were transferred to the forensic team, collected afterwards by Grenadian undertaker Leslie Bailey and buried by cemetery workers in St George’s Cemetery. The Army said it had found no evidence that American forces secretly removed Bishop’s body from Grenada. It also admitted, however, that detailed military records concerning the remains could not be located and that the recovery operation was poorly staffed and difficult to document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/documents/ba00caeb-8b0e-43cd-8d9b-7e554643e1b5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington Post</span></span></span>

That account narrows the search but does not solve the case. It points towards an unidentified burial in St George’s Cemetery, yet no grave has been definitively located, exhumed and linked through modern forensic testing to Bishop or the other victims. Grenadian campaigners have argued that documents prove the United States retained or removed at least some remains, while the Army’s review maintains that the material was returned to a Grenadian undertaker for burial. The contradiction partly reflects a difference between what the documents demonstrate and what later interpretations claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nowgrenada.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Booklet.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nowgrenada.com">[NOW Grenada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nowgrenada.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort George in October 1983...Read more...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1983</span></span></span>

The case endures because it combines personal grief with unresolved national history. Families were denied identifiable bodies and funerals. Grenada’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission later investigated the revolutionary period, but it did not produce a final recovery of the remains. A Washington Post investigation prompted renewed attention, and the United States Army undertook a further records review, yet the physical question remains unanswered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://truthcommissions.humanities.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Grenada-TRC-2001-Full-Report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: truthcommissions.humanities.mcmaster.ca">[mcmaster.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">truthcommissions.humanities.mcmaster.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grenada TRC 2001 Full Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ll UlBISHOP TRIAL REPORT- December 1986-By … and examined by the Truth and Reconcmatlon Commission. occurred in Grenada during the period...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1986</span></span></span>

The most plausible present conclusion is therefore restrained: burned and commingled remains from Calivigny were examined by American specialists and were probably buried in Grenada, but failures in identification, record-keeping and grave registration broke the chain of evidence. The mystery does not require a grand conspiracy. Administrative disorder during an invasion, inadequate forensic procedures and later institutional opacity are sufficient to explain why certainty was lost. What would resolve it is not another theory, but cemetery research, surviving burial records, carefully authorised excavation and DNA comparison with relatives.

## What sank the *Island Queen*?

The auxiliary schooner *Island Queen* left Grenada for St Vincent on 5 August 1944 with 67 passengers and crew. It never arrived. Searches found no confirmed wreckage, and no accepted account explains its disappearance. The loss became one of Grenada’s worst maritime tragedies and remains especially haunting because whole families lost relatives without a body or known place of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.familysearch.org/search/catalog/4369240" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: familysearch.org">[familysearch.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">familysearch.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on familysearch.org.</span></span></span>

The vessel vanished during the Second World War, which encouraged immediate suspicions of enemy action. A German submarine was blamed in some retellings; another theory suggested that an Allied submarine or patrol vessel mistook the schooner for an enemy craft. Stories about its German-built engine later became part of this speculation. Yet no publicly established wartime record has been produced that confirms a torpedo attack on the *Island Queen*. The submarine theory is therefore possible only in a broad sense, not demonstrated by documentary evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nowgrenada.com/2019/08/island-queen-disappeared-without-a-trace-75-years-ago/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nowgrenada.com">[NOW Grenada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nowgrenada.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">island queen disappeared without a trace 75 years ago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">island queen disappeared without a trace 75 years ago</span></span></span>

Fire, structural failure and severe weather are more ordinary explanations. Wooden inter-island vessels could be vulnerable to overloading, mechanical problems and sudden squalls. An onboard fire might also account for a rapid loss. The difficulty is the reported absence of verified debris, bodies or cargo. That absence does not make an ordinary sinking impossible: currents can disperse wreckage, reports may be incomplete, and much of a wooden vessel can sink or break apart. It does, however, prevent any one conventional explanation from becoming decisive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nowgrenada.com/2019/08/island-queen-disappeared-without-a-trace-75-years-ago/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nowgrenada.com">[NOW Grenada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nowgrenada.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">island queen disappeared without a trace 75 years ago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">island queen disappeared without a trace 75 years ago</span></span></span>

The most distinctive theory involves Kick-’em-Jenny, the active submarine volcano north of Grenada. The University of the West Indies Seismic Research Centre has suggested that volcanic gas released into the sea might have reduced the water’s density, depriving the vessel of buoyancy and causing a very rapid sinking. Its hazard material notes that such a mechanism could help explain the lack of floating evidence and that the volcano may have been actively releasing gas in 1944 without obvious disturbance at the surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uwiseismic.com/volcanoes/kick-em-jenny/kej-hazards/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwiseismic.com">[UWI Seismic Research Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwiseismic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">kej hazards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">kej hazards</span></span></span>

This hypothesis is scientifically interesting but still circumstantial. There is no direct observation placing the *Island Queen* over an active gas release, no recovered hull showing a sudden intact descent, and no confirmed eruption exactly tied to the vessel’s final position. The sinking-by-gas explanation should therefore be treated as a plausible scenario rather than a solution.

The most honest verdict is that the *Island Queen* was probably lost through a sudden maritime disaster somewhere between Grenada and St Vincent, but the available evidence cannot distinguish confidently among fire, foundering, collision, military action or a volcanic effect. Modern seabed mapping might eventually locate the wreck. Until then, the case remains a documented disappearance surrounded by theories of very unequal evidential weight.

## Did Julien Fédon escape Grenada?

Julien Fédon led the most formidable challenge to British rule in eighteenth-century Grenada. Beginning in 1795, his forces controlled much of the island and operated from the mountain stronghold of Belvidere. The uprising drew energy from the French Revolution, conflict between British Protestant authority and French-speaking Catholic inhabitants, and the participation of enslaved people seeking freedom or improved conditions. British forces finally overran the rebel positions in June 1796.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.openedition.org/lrf/2017" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.openedition.org">[OpenEdition Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.openedition.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on openedition.org.</span></span></span>

Fédon was never conclusively captured, identified among the dead or placed in a known grave. Contemporary and later accounts suggested that he attempted to flee towards Trinidad. An overturned boat and a compass reportedly found after the collapse of the rebellion encouraged the belief that he drowned. Yet no body was recovered, and later rumours placed him alive elsewhere in the Caribbean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9don%27s_rebellion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fédon&#x27;s rebellion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fédon&#x27;s rebellion</span></span></span>

Three broad possibilities remain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **He died during the final fighting.** Rebels fled through steep, forested terrain as British troops seized their positions. Fédon could have been killed or fatally injured without being recognised.
* **He drowned while escaping.** This became the most persistent practical explanation, supported by the story of the abandoned or overturned boat.
* **He reached another island and lived under another identity.** Later sightings and traditions preserve this possibility, but none supplies a secure documentary trail.

</div>

Escape was not impossible. Other rebel leaders remained hidden for years after the uprising. One of Fédon’s deputies, Joachim Philip, evaded capture until 1803, while another fugitive survived until 1808. Their cases show that Grenada’s terrain and regional maritime networks could conceal wanted men for long periods. They do not, however, prove that Fédon survived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9don%27s_rebellion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fédon&#x27;s rebellion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fédon&#x27;s rebellion</span></span></span>

The absence of a body gave Fédon a symbolic afterlife. Instead of ending as a captured rebel displayed by the colonial state, he became a figure who might have escaped its reach. That ambiguity helped transform him from a defeated commander into an enduring emblem of resistance. Later Grenadian political memory connected his rebellion with broader struggles against colonial authority, even though historians continue to debate the rebels’ exact social programme and whether emancipation was formally declared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137030818_1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on springer.com.</span></span></span>

The drowning theory remains the simplest explanation, but “simplest” is not the same as proved. Without a body, authenticated correspondence or a reliable post-1796 sighting, Fédon’s fate remains a genuine historical disappearance rather than a solved death embellished by folklore.

## Who carved Grenada’s “Carib Stones”?

Grenada contains some of the most notable Indigenous rock art in the Lesser Antilles. At Mt Rich in St Patrick parish, more than 60 carved designs appear on boulders beside the river. Faces, geometric forms and other figures have long been called “Carib Stones”, but that familiar name can mislead. It does not establish that the carvings were made by the people encountered by Europeans in the final period before colonisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mycedo.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mycedo.org">[mycedo.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mycedo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mycedo.org.</span></span></span>

The stones were recorded in writing by the nineteenth century and studied more systematically by Thomas Huckerby, whose illustrated 1921 publication is preserved by the Smithsonian Libraries. Later archaeological work compared the carvings with pottery, settlement evidence and rock art elsewhere in the Caribbean. This research generally places much of Grenada’s rock art within the pre-Columbian Ceramic Age rather than treating it as the work of an unknown vanished civilisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.si.edu/digital-library/book/petroglyphsofgre13huck" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.si.edu">[Smithsonian Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

Dating the carvings is difficult because rock cannot usually be radiocarbon-dated directly. Archaeologists instead rely on nearby settlements, associated artefacts, weathering, style and regional comparison. Proposed dates vary, but research has linked the principal phase of Grenada’s rock art to communities living on the island roughly between the eighth and tenth centuries, while allowing that some images may be earlier or may have been recut over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.grenadaarchaeology.com/Papers/Hanna_2018.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: grenadaarchaeology.com">[grenadaarchaeology.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">grenadaarchaeology.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Pennsylvania State University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Pennsylvania State University</span></span></span>

Their meaning is even harder to establish. The faces may represent ancestors, spirits, mythical beings or participants in ritual. Their repeated placement near rivers and pools suggests that water, boundaries or spiritually important landscapes mattered. Nearby cup-like depressions may have been used for grinding, food preparation or ritual substances, but function cannot be read confidently from shape alone.

The strongest interpretation is that the Mt Rich carvings formed part of an Indigenous sacred or social landscape used over generations. The weakest interpretations are those that assign each image a precise story, astronomical message or secret code without supporting evidence. Archaeology can identify patterns and cultural context; it cannot recover an oral explanation lost before the carvings were adequately documented.

There is also a modern conservation mystery: how much of the original surface has already been lost? A geological stability assessment found serious erosion risks at Grenadian rock-art sites, particularly where water, moisture and weathering affect exposed panels. Damage can erase fine lines and make later interpretation less reliable, meaning that the historical record is still deteriorating.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269393311_Evaluation_of_Grenada%27s_Carib_Stones_via_the_Rock_Art_Stability_Index" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) Evaluation of Grenada&#x27;s &quot;Carib Stones&quot; via the Rock</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) Evaluation of Grenada&#x27;s &quot;Carib Stones&quot; via the Rock</span></span></span>

## What the evidence really supports

Grenada’s major enigmas do not all deserve the same level of confidence.

**Maurice Bishop’s missing remains** form the best-documented unresolved case. Official records establish recovery, forensic examination and a claimed cemetery burial, but not a securely identified grave. The likely cause of the mystery is a broken chain of custody compounded by political mistrust.

**The *Island Queen*** is a confirmed disappearance with no known wreck. Volcanic gas, military attack and onboard disaster are all proposed explanations, but none has direct physical proof. Claims that one theory has “solved” the case go beyond the evidence.

**Julien Fédon’s fate** is genuinely unknown. Drowning is plausible, survival is possible and later sightings are unverified. His disappearance matters because it shaped his transition from historical insurgent to national symbol.

**The Mt Rich petroglyphs** are not mysterious in origin in the sensational sense: they belong to Grenada’s Indigenous past. What remains unresolved is their exact date, sequence and meaning. Archaeological caution is more useful here than imaginative decoding.

The common thread is the loss of reliable context. Bodies were moved without secure documentation. A ship disappeared beyond observation. A hunted leader vanished during military defeat. Rock art survived after the traditions that explained it had been disrupted. Grenada’s historical mysteries persist where evidence was destroyed, never recorded or separated from the people who could interpret it. Their value lies not in encouraging conspiracy, but in showing why archives, forensic care, archaeology and the preservation of community memory matter.
