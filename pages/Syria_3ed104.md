## Who destroyed Ugarit?

Ugarit, on Syria’s Mediterranean coast at Ras Shamra, was one of the great international cities of the Late Bronze Age. Its merchants traded across the eastern Mediterranean, while its scribes worked in several languages and writing systems. Thousands of surviving tablets document diplomacy, commerce, law, religion and ordinary disputes. Among the finds was a compact cuneiform alphabet of 30 signs, one of the most important writing systems recovered from the ancient Near East.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://virtual-museum-syria.org/damascus/the-ugaritic-alphabet-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: virtual-museum-syria.org">[Virtual Museum of Syria]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">virtual-museum-syria.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Virtual Museum of Syria Ugaritic Alphabet Stone &#124; National Museum of Damascus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Virtual Museum of SyriaUgaritic Alphabet Stone &#124; National Museum of DamascusDecember 15, 2024 — 20 Sept 2025 — Clay tablet bearing the th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2024</span></span></span>

The city was destroyed at the beginning of the 12th century BCE and never recovered. For decades, the familiar explanation blamed the “Sea Peoples”, mobile groups mentioned in Egyptian records during the wider collapse of Bronze Age states. Letters found at Ugarit appear to describe enemy ships, absent troops and a kingdom struggling to defend itself. That combination created an irresistibly dramatic picture: warnings arrive, the fleet is away, invaders land and the city burns.

The evidence is less tidy. Recent reassessments of destruction layers across Syria and the eastern Mediterranean show that many sites traditionally grouped into one invasion were actually destroyed at different times. Several Syrian cities once attributed to the Sea Peoples fell decades earlier, while some settlements show disruption without a clear foreign attack. Even at Ugarit, the archaeological record does not securely identify the people responsible for the final destruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/AUTHORS/SeaPeoples/SeaPeoples-Millek2023-2021.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ancientportsantiques.com">[ancientportsantiques.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ancientportsantiques.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">That being said, there is no standout candidate for the destruction...</span></span></span>

Environmental evidence adds another possibility. Research based partly on pollen and climate records from the region indicates prolonged aridity around the end of the Bronze Age. Drought would not, by itself, explain burning buildings or hostile ships, but it could have weakened harvests, increased migration, disrupted trade and made palace economies less able to survive war or rebellion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3743901/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCEnvironmental Roots of the Late Bronze Age Crisis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by D Kaniewski · 2013 · Cited by 347 — The Late Bronze Age world of the Eastern Mediterranean, a rich linkage of Aegean, Egyptian, Syr...</span></span></span>

The most plausible explanation is therefore a compound crisis rather than a single cinematic assault. Ugarit faced military danger, regional economic breakdown and environmental stress at roughly the same time. Raiders or an organised enemy may have delivered the final blow, but archaeology has not established their identity. The mystery survives because the city’s archives describe an emergency without preserving the last chapter.

## What did the Ebla tablets really reveal?

When Italian archaeologists uncovered the royal archives of Ebla at Tell Mardikh in the 1970s, the discovery transformed knowledge of third-millennium BCE Syria. Thousands of clay tablets had fallen from shelves when the palace was destroyed, preserving administrative accounts, diplomatic records, literary compositions, vocabulary lists and evidence for a powerful Syrian kingdom around 2400 BCE. The collection is among the earliest large archives found in its original storage setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5702/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMari &amp; Europos-Dura sites of Euphrates ValleyThe excavated ruins of the ancient cities Ebla and Mari bear wit...</span></span></span>

The mystery began when preliminary readings appeared to connect the tablets with biblical names and places. Epigrapher Giovanni Pettinato announced possible references to Sodom, Gomorrah and other locations associated with the Hebrew Bible. He also proposed that certain personal names might contain an early form of the divine name later rendered as Yahweh. Newspapers and religious writers quickly presented Ebla as archaeological confirmation of biblical traditions.

Those claims were challenged by other members of the excavation team, especially Alfonso Archi. Ancient place names are difficult to identify because similar sound sequences can occur in different languages and locations. The relevant geographical list also had parallels in Mesopotamian texts, making an eastern rather than Palestinian setting more likely. The supposed divine-name evidence depended on how a particular cuneiform sign was read, while no offering list or divine catalogue at Ebla clearly names Yahweh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebla%E2%80%93biblical_controversy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ebla–biblical controversy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ebla–biblical controversy</span></span></span>

The dispute was intensified by restricted access to unpublished tablets, changing readings and personal conflict among scholars. That delay allowed bold claims to circulate before specialists could inspect photographs, copies and linguistic context. It is a useful example of how an authentic archaeological sensation can produce a secondary mystery: not “Were the tablets real?” but “How much of the public story arose from evidence, and how much from premature interpretation?”

The broad scholarly position today is that Ebla illuminates the languages, politics and religious practices of early Syria, but does not prove the historical existence of Sodom, Gomorrah, the biblical patriarchs or early Yahweh worship. Similar names and rituals demonstrate that biblical traditions developed within a wider ancient Near Eastern world; they do not establish direct identity. The genuine wonder of Ebla is larger than the discarded claims: an entire Syrian state, barely known before excavation, emerged through its own paperwork.

## Why were the northern villages abandoned?

Across the limestone hills between Aleppo and the Turkish border stand the remains of hundreds of late Roman and Byzantine settlements. Their stone houses, churches, baths, tombs, workshops and olive presses are so well preserved that travellers once called them the “Dead Cities”. UNESCO more carefully describes them as the [Ancient Villages]({{ 'ancient-villages/' | relative_url }}) of Northern Syria. Many flourished between the first and seventh centuries and were abandoned gradually between roughly the eighth and tenth centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1348/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

The misleading popular image is of prosperous Christian communities suddenly emptied by invasion. There is no convincing evidence for one simultaneous catastrophe. The buildings do not generally form a single destruction horizon, and settlement decline varied from place to place. Their abandonment appears to have been a long economic and demographic process.

The villages prospered by producing agricultural goods, particularly olives and wine, for regional and Mediterranean markets. Their success depended on trade routes, urban consumers and stable political connections. After the Byzantine Empire lost Syria in the seventh century, commercial networks and administrative centres changed. Warfare between Byzantine and later Islamic powers made the northern frontier less secure, while economic activity shifted towards other towns and routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/103578519/World_Heritage_at_risks_The_Ancient_Villages_of_Northern_Syria_before_and_during_the_Crisis_of" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage at risks The Ancient Villages of Northern</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Heritage at risks The Ancient Villages of Northern</span></span></span>

Climate and land use may also have contributed, but claims of a simple ecological collapse go beyond the evidence. Some communities persisted for generations after the political transition, and the region was not emptied altogether. Residents could have moved towards larger settlements, more productive land or new commercial centres without leaving a written account of their decisions.

The real mystery is therefore not why every village vanished on one terrible day, but why a once-profitable rural system ceased to be worthwhile. The surviving stone architecture exaggerates the sense of sudden disappearance: timber, cultivated fields, livestock and human movement are gone, while durable walls remain. What looks like a frozen catastrophe was probably a prolonged adjustment to altered markets, security and government.

## Where is Eli Cohen’s grave?

Eli Cohen, an Egyptian-born Israeli intelligence officer, entered Syria under a false identity and established relationships within Damascus’s political and military elite. Syrian counter-intelligence arrested him in January 1965 after detecting his radio transmissions. Following a trial, he was publicly hanged in Damascus on 18 May 1965.

The execution is documented; the burial is not. Syrian authorities concealed the grave, and Cohen’s remains were reportedly moved to prevent recovery. Over subsequent decades, intermediaries, intelligence services and Cohen’s family pursued rumours about cemeteries, military zones and later reburials. None produced publicly verified remains.

The case changed significantly in May 2025, when Israel announced that it had recovered approximately 2,500 items from a Syrian intelligence archive concerning Cohen. They included photographs, recordings, handwritten material, false documents and personal possessions seized after his arrest. Syrian and regional sources subsequently described the handover as an indirect gesture by Syria’s post-Assad leadership, although Israel characterised it as an intelligence operation conducted with a foreign partner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/syrian-leadership-approved-return-dead-spy-archive-israel-sources-say-2025-05-20/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Syrian leadership approved return of dead spy archive to Israel, sources say</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sources suggest the archive&#x27;s return was a calculated move following the fall of Bashar al-Assad’s regime, with Sharaa using the material...</span></span></span>

The recovered archive proves that Syrian security agencies preserved an extensive documentary record for six decades. It does not reveal—or at least has not publicly revealed—the grave. Reports in October 2025 suggested that negotiations over the remains might be advancing, but Syrian sources denied that an immediate transfer had been agreed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://themedialine.org/headlines/senior-syrian-official-denies-return-of-eli-cohens-remains-is-imminent-but-confirms-negotiations-are-in-progress/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themedialine.org">[The Media Line]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themedialine.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themedialine.org.</span></span></span>

Cohen’s disappearance after death occupies an unusual place between espionage history and national memory. In Israel, he is commemorated as a legendary agent; in Syria, he was an enemy spy who penetrated the state at a period of extreme political instability. The missing grave has allowed both narratives to continue. Until identifiable remains are located through reliable records or forensic testing, specific burial stories remain claims rather than solutions.

## What happened to Austin Tice?

American freelance journalist and former US Marine Austin Tice disappeared near Daraya, outside Damascus, in August 2012 while reporting on the Syrian war. A video released weeks later showed him blindfolded and held by armed men. No organisation made a credible public claim of responsibility, and no independently verified image or message from him has appeared since. The US Federal Bureau of Investigation continues to list him as kidnapped and offers a reward for information leading to his safe recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/wanted/kidnap/austin-bennett-tice" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">austin bennett tice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">austin bennett tice</span></span></span>

For years, the Syrian government denied holding Tice. US officials nevertheless stated that they believed he had been detained by forces aligned with Bashar al-Assad’s state. Later reporting based on former officials and Syrian intelligence material indicated that he was taken by a pro-government force and held in Damascus. Accounts that he briefly escaped and was recaptured have also emerged, but they do not establish where he was subsequently taken.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/us/state-dept-says-no-us-government-organization-ground-search-austin-tice-2024-12-16/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">No US government group in Syria for search of Austin Tice, State Department says</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">No US government group in Syria for search of Austin Tice, State Department says</span></span></span>

The collapse of Assad’s government in December 2024 opened prisons and archives but did not resolve the case. Searches produced competing testimony rather than decisive evidence. One former official reportedly claimed that Tice had been killed on Assad’s orders in 2013; US and Syrian officials said the allegation remained unsubstantiated. In 2026, Tice’s family said it had information suggesting that he might still be alive and possibly outside Syria, but that claim has not been independently confirmed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2025/08/13/austin-tice-syria-assad-search/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Despite numerous backchannel efforts, intelligence assessments, and even direct negotiations with top Syrian officials across different a...</span></span></span>

Three possibilities remain credible: Tice died in Syrian custody; he survived for an extended period and was moved between detention sites; or he was transferred to another state or armed organisation. Public evidence cannot yet distinguish among them. The absence of a body, detention record or verified later sighting makes confident declarations—whether of death or survival—premature.

## Syria’s greater mystery of the missing

Tice is internationally prominent, but his case belongs to a much larger Syrian history of disappearance. The International Commission on Missing Persons cites an estimate of at least 130,000 people missing from the conflict beginning in 2011, in addition to approximately 17,000 linked to the earlier rule of Hafez al-Assad. Victims include government opponents, civilians detained at checkpoints, members of armed groups, foreign nationals, abductees held by Islamic State and people lost during migration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/syria/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icmp.int.</span></span></span>

The scale is uncertain partly because names appear in several databases, spellings vary, whole families were displaced and many arrests were never officially acknowledged. Enforced disappearance also differs from an ordinary missing-person case: state officials or armed organisations may know what happened while deliberately refusing to disclose it.

Evidence recovered since 2024 has confirmed that many disappearances ended in detention deaths. Photographic archives, intelligence files, prison records and mass graves point to an organised system in which bodies were numbered, transported and concealed. Investigators have warned that grave sites can lose evidential value when relatives or untrained teams dig without forensic controls, however understandable their urgency may be.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icij.org/investigations/damascus-dossier/syria-assad-mass-murder-photos-evidence/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icij.org">[ICIJ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icij.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Leaked photos expose an archive of death in Assad&#x27;s Syria</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Leaked photos expose an archive of death in Assad&#x27;s Syria</span></span></span>

The United Nations created the Independent Institution on Missing Persons in Syria in 2023 to determine the fate and whereabouts of the missing and support their families. Syria established its own national commission in May 2025. The International Commission on Missing Persons reported in October 2025 that about 30,000 missing people had been reported through its data system and that 70 suspected mass-grave sites had been submitted through its online locator. Those figures represent documented entries, not a final national total.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iimp.un.org/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iimp.un.org">[un.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iimp.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on un.org.</span></span></span>

Solving these cases requires more than opening former prisons. Investigators must preserve archives, compare detention registers, interview survivors, map transport routes, protect graves, collect family DNA and maintain a transparent system for identifying remains. A single identification can answer one family’s question, but establishing responsibility requires linking that person’s arrest, detention, death and burial to institutions and named officials.

This is why Syria’s missing cannot be treated merely as an eerie national enigma. It is an unfinished legal and humanitarian emergency. The central questions—who was taken, by whom, where they were held and where their bodies went—are in principle answerable. The obstacle is not lack of plausible theories but the fragmented, concealed and endangered state of the evidence.

## How myth and evidence become separated

Syria’s mysteries range across four thousand years, yet the most reliable way to assess them is remarkably consistent.

**Contemporary records carry weight, but not automatic truth.** Ugarit’s final letters describe danger without identifying the destroyers. Syrian security files may record arrests while concealing torture or falsifying causes of death.

**Archaeological context matters more than an attractive object or inscription.** A tablet found on a palace shelf can be dated and compared with neighbouring texts. A looted artefact sold without provenance may be genuine, forged or assigned to the wrong site. Conflict-era looting has removed thousands of Syrian objects from their original settings, destroying clues even when the objects themselves survive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark%3A/48223/pf0000387932" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesdoc.unesco.org">[UNESCO Document Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesdoc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span>

**Similar names are not necessarily identical people or places.** The Ebla controversy demonstrates how uncertain readings can become public “discoveries” before other specialists test them.

**Absence of evidence has different meanings.** A missing Bronze Age text may simply have decayed. A missing intelligence file may have been removed deliberately. A concealed grave is evidence of an act of concealment even before the victim is identified.

**Theories should be ranked, not merely collected.** Multiple pressures provide a stronger explanation for Ugarit than an unsupported claim about one invading people. Gradual economic change explains the northern villages better than legends of instantaneous flight. In modern disappearance cases, documentary and forensic evidence should take precedence over anonymous rumours.

Syria’s enduring mysteries matter because they expose the limits of what ruins and archives can tell us—and the damage caused when evidence is monopolised, sensationalised or destroyed. The best investigations do not remove uncertainty by force. They define it carefully, eliminate what the evidence cannot support and preserve the possibility that future excavation, declassification or identification may finally supply the missing chapter.
