## Introduction

The Nomoli stone figures are among [Sierra Leone]({{ 'which-sierra-leone-mysteries-still/' | relative_url }})'s greatest archaeological mysteries because nobody can identify their makers, date every example with confidence, or explain exactly why they were carved. Hundreds of these distinctive soapstone sculptures have been uncovered by farmers, miners and builders, often buried beneath the ground with no surviving settlements or written records to explain them. That missing context has left historians and archaeologists piecing together their story from artistic style, oral traditions, early European accounts and a small number of archaeological investigations.

Modern research has narrowed the possibilities considerably. Most specialists now believe the figures were created by peoples known to early Portuguese visitors as the Sapi, ancestors or close relatives of present-day Temne, Bullom and Baga communities, rather than by a vanished civilisation or visitors from elsewhere. Even so, important questions remain unresolved. The exact age of the carving tradition, the purpose of individual sculptures and the reasons why the tradition disappeared continue to be debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/nomoli-pomdo-stone-figures" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Nomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtNomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2025</span></span></span>

## Where the figures were found

Nomoli figures have been discovered mainly across southeastern Sierra Leone, with related stone sculptures also appearing in neighbouring Guinea and parts of Liberia. Most were not recovered through systematic archaeological excavations. Instead, they emerged unexpectedly during farming, road construction, mining or other earth-moving activities, sometimes several metres below the surface. Because they were separated from their original surroundings, archaeologists usually cannot rely on soil layers, associated pottery or organic material to establish precise dates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/nomoli-pomdo-stone-figures" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Nomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtNomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2025</span></span></span>

The figures are carved primarily from steatite, also known as soapstone, a relatively soft rock that allows fine detail. Their appearance varies considerably:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Human heads with exaggerated facial features.
* Seated or squatting figures.
* Rulers wearing elaborate headdresses and jewellery.
* Human-animal combinations.
* Groups showing several figures interacting.

</div>

Many display careful attention to hairstyles, scarification, necklaces, bracelets and ceremonial clothing, suggesting they represented people of importance rather than ordinary members of society.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africa.si.edu/collection/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africa.si.edu">[National Museum of African Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africa.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of African Art Male figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of African Art Male figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span></span></span>

One reason the mystery has endured is that almost every new discovery repeats the same pattern: an isolated sculpture appears without the buildings, burials or ritual spaces that might explain its original function.

## Who probably made the Nomoli figures?

For much of the twentieth century, scholars proposed widely different origins. Some suggested links with unknown migrants or entirely lost peoples. Others believed the sculptures might have been imported.

Those ideas have gradually lost support. Comparative research now points towards the Sapi peoples who occupied much of the Upper Guinea coast before major political and demographic changes during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Portuguese traders described these communities, while surviving ivory carvings made for European customers display remarkably similar artistic features.

The strongest evidence includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Similar facial proportions and enlarged heads.
* Comparable hairstyles and royal insignia.
* Matching seated poses and formal frontal composition.
* Shared emphasis on elite status and ceremonial dress.

</div>

These similarities suggest that stone and ivory carvings belonged to the same broad artistic tradition rather than being unrelated works separated by centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/nomoli-pomdo-stone-figures" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Nomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtNomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2025</span></span></span>

Some museum catalogues now identify the figures as products of Sapi artistic traditions, while recognising that exact ethnic identities remain uncertain because political boundaries and cultural identities changed substantially after the sixteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africa.si.edu/collection/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africa.si.edu">[National Museum of African Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africa.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of African Art Figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of African Art Figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span></span></span>

## Why is dating them still so difficult?

Dating the Nomoli figures is far harder than dating pottery or buildings found in controlled excavations.

Stone itself cannot usually be dated directly. Instead, archaeologists must date surrounding material such as charcoal, wood or soil. Since most Nomoli figures were removed long before modern archaeological recording, that evidence was often lost.

Researchers therefore rely on several indirect methods:

* **Artistic comparison.** Similarities with Sapi ivory carvings securely dated to roughly 1490–1530 suggest many stone sculptures belong to the late medieval or early modern period.
* **Historical documents.** Early European travellers described stone idols in the region during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.
* **Archaeological associations.** Pottery and charcoal from sites such as Kamabai Rock Shelter indicate that related artistic traditions may extend back several centuries before European contact.
* **Comparative wooden sculpture.** Radiocarbon dating of stylistically related wooden figures hints that the broader carving tradition could have begun much earlier, perhaps between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, although this does not directly date every surviving stone figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/nomoli-pomdo-stone-figures" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Nomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtNomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2025</span></span></span>

As a result, scholars increasingly favour a tradition that developed over several centuries rather than a single moment of production.

## Competing theories about their original purpose

Although agreement has grown about who probably carved the figures, their purpose remains less certain.

### Ancestor memorials

Many art historians argue that the sculptures commemorated important ancestors, rulers or chiefs. The elaborate regalia, commanding poses and repeated symbols of authority support this interpretation. Some scholars believe the larger heads represented wisdom, status or spiritual power rather than literal anatomy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/nomoli-pomdo-stone-figures" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Nomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtNomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2025</span></span></span>

### Images of political authority

Another closely related interpretation sees the sculptures as representations of living or recently deceased elites. Objects such as staffs, elaborate necklaces, ceremonial hats and symbolic animals suggest that at least some figures expressed political leadership rather than general religious belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africa.si.edu/collection/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africa.si.edu">[National Museum of African Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africa.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of African Art Male figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of African Art Male figure &#124; National Museum of African Art</span></span></span>

### Ritual and sacred objects

Some researchers suggest the sculptures formed part of shrines or ceremonial spaces. Unfortunately, because so few were recovered in their original locations, there is little archaeological evidence to reconstruct how they were arranged or used in ritual.

Rather than choosing a single explanation, many specialists believe different figures probably served different purposes across several centuries and communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/nomoli/FA25A3EAE9D80C1C3A7DD93B043A4DAC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentNomoli &#124; The Journal of African History &#124; Cambridge Core...</span></span></span>

## From sacred objects to museum collections

An important part of the mystery is that the figures acquired new meanings long after they were carved.

Communities who later discovered them often had no direct memory of their creators. Instead, they interpreted the sculptures according to their own religious traditions. Among some Mende communities, Nomoli figures became associated with agricultural fertility and were placed in rice fields to encourage successful harvests. Oral traditions describe offerings made to them and, in some cases, ritual punishment if crops failed. Earlier observers also recorded their use as objects connected with oaths and disputes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/africa/article/abs/nomoli-of-mende-country/425D3B75F3E17DDF9FADC01458E84406" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Nomoli of Mende Country &#124; Africa &#124; Cambridge CoreAugust 21, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 21, 2012</span></span></span>

Anthropologists describe this process as the "ritual recycling" of ancient objects: artefacts whose original purpose was forgotten but whose physical presence inspired entirely new religious meanings. Rather than preserving an unchanged tradition, later communities incorporated the sculptures into their own spiritual lives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/nomoli-pomdo-stone-figures" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[The Metropolitan Museum of Art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Nomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtNomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2025</span></span></span>

During the colonial period and afterwards, many figures entered museums and private collections in Sierra Leone, Europe and North America. While this helped preserve numerous examples, it also meant that many lost whatever archaeological information survived about where they had been found. Today, important collections are held by the Sierra Leone National Museum, the Smithsonian's National Museum of African Art, the British Museum and other institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sierraleoneheritage.org/item/SLNM.1963.36.08/nomoli" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sierraleoneheritage.org">[sierraleoneheritage.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sierraleoneheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sierra Leone Heritage Nomoli</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sierra Leone Heritage Nomoli</span></span></span>

## Separating evidence from popular myths

Because the origins of the Nomoli figures remain incomplete, they have attracted extraordinary claims.

Stories circulating online have described the sculptures as evidence of lost global civilisations, prehistoric advanced technology, extraterrestrial visitors or astonishing ages stretching back 17,000 years or more. Other claims refer to mysterious materials supposedly found inside certain figures.

None of these ideas is supported by credible archaeological evidence. There are no verified excavation reports, laboratory analyses or documented finds demonstrating that Nomoli sculptures contain unknown materials or originate from the Ice Age. By contrast, the mainstream interpretation rests on identifiable objects, historical records, stylistic comparisons and archaeological research that collectively place the tradition within the cultural history of the Upper Guinea coast.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/nomoli-pomdo-stone-figures" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metmuseum.org">[metmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Metropolitan Museum of Art Nomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Metropolitan Museum of ArtNomoli and Pomdo Stone Figures of Upper Guinea - The Metropolitan Museum of ArtOctober 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2, 2025</span></span></span>

The genuine mystery is therefore not whether the figures came from an impossible civilisation, but how an important artistic tradition flourished, evolved and eventually disappeared with so few written records.

## Why the mystery still matters

The Nomoli stone figures remain central to understanding Sierra Leone's early history because they preserve evidence of complex societies that existed before extensive written documentation of the region. They demonstrate sophisticated stone-working skills, rich artistic conventions and political symbolism, even though the identities of many individual carvers and patrons have been lost.

Research continues to refine the story. Improved archaeological excavations, scientific dating of associated materials and comparative study of stone, ivory and wooden sculptures have steadily replaced speculation with evidence. Yet the absence of secure excavation contexts means that some questions may never receive complete answers.

For that reason, the Nomoli figures remain one of Sierra Leone's most enduring historical mysteries: not because they defy explanation altogether, but because they preserve the achievements of a forgotten artistic tradition whose creators left remarkable sculptures but almost no written account of themselves.
