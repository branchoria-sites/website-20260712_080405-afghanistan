## Introduction

The mystery of La Buse's supposed treasure is not whether the pirate existed, but whether the famous treasure cipher ever did. Olivier Levasseur, known as La Buse ("The Buzzard"), was a real eighteenth-century pirate who shared in one of the richest captures of the Indian Ocean's age of piracy. Yet the dramatic story that he threw a coded message into the crowd before his execution, challenging spectators to find his hidden fortune, rests on surprisingly weak historical foundations. Modern research increasingly suggests that the celebrated cryptogram entered the story only in the twentieth century, long after Levasseur's death, making the cipher itself one of the mystery's greatest puzzles. Rather than being a proven map to buried riches, the La Buse legend illustrates how authentic history, literary invention and treasure hunting became intertwined across [Mauritius]({{ 'mauritius/' | relative_url }}), Réunion, Madagascar and the wider western Indian Ocean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogram_of_Olivier_Levasseur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span></span></span>

## The documented pirate raid and captured wealth

The historical foundation of the legend is solid. In April 1721, Levasseur and the English pirate John Taylor captured the Portuguese treasure ship *Nossa Senhora do Cabo* after it had been damaged by storms near Réunion. The vessel was carrying the former Viceroy of Portuguese India, the Archbishop of Goa, valuable religious objects, precious stones, gold, silver and other luxury cargo accumulated during years of imperial administration. Contemporary records leave little doubt that this was among the richest pirate prizes of the era.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogram_of_Olivier_Levasseur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span></span></span>

The enormous value of the captured cargo helps explain why stories of a hidden fortune emerged. Even if much of the loot was divided among the pirates, historians acknowledge that it is impossible to reconstruct exactly what became of every item. Incomplete eighteenth-century records leave room for speculation that some wealth disappeared without documentation. That uncertainty, however, is very different from evidence that Levasseur buried a vast treasure awaiting discovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogram_of_Olivier_Levasseur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span></span></span>

For Mauritius, the legend gained particular significance because pirates regularly used the Mascarene Islands as temporary bases and anchorages. As a result, caves, coastal rock formations and remote bays around Mauritius have repeatedly been proposed as possible hiding places despite the absence of documentary proof linking Levasseur's personal treasure to any site on the island.

## How the cryptogram entered the story

The famous cryptogram is where documented history begins to give way to uncertainty.

According to the popular version of the story, Levasseur stood on the scaffold before his execution in 1730, threw a coded document into the crowd and declared that whoever solved it would inherit his treasure. The cipher consists of seventeen lines written largely in symbols resembling a pigpen cipher, a substitution system known in Europe long before the eighteenth century. The image has circulated widely in books, documentaries and treasure-hunting websites for decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cipherfoundation.org/older-ciphers/la-buse-cryptogram/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cipherfoundation.org">[The Cipher Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cipherfoundation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Cipher Foundation La Buse Cryptogram</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cipher FoundationLa Buse Cryptogram - The Cipher Foundation...</span></span></span>

The problem is that no known eighteenth-century source records this dramatic scene.

Instead, the earliest known appearance of the cryptogram is in 1934, when the respected French naval historian Charles de La Roncière published *Le Flibustier Mystérieux*. His book introduced both the cipher and the treasure narrative to a broad audience. Modern researchers have searched for an independent manuscript of the cryptogram dating from before this publication but have found none. Every later version ultimately traces back to La Roncière's work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogram_of_Olivier_Levasseur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span></span></span>

This chronology has led many historians to question whether the cryptogram was ever an eighteenth-century artefact at all.

## Why historians doubt the cipher's authenticity

Recent scholarship has become increasingly sceptical for several connected reasons.

First, no authenticated original manuscript has ever surfaced. Researchers have identified numerous reproductions and copies, but no verifiable document with an eighteenth-century chain of custody has been found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogram_of_Olivier_Levasseur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span></span></span>

Second, contemporary reports of Levasseur's execution do not mention a cryptogram or a theatrical challenge to the crowd. Considering how memorable such an event would have been, its absence from surviving accounts is significant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogram_of_Olivier_Levasseur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span></span></span>

Third, the publishing history raises questions. La Roncière introduced the story in a publication that blurred historical narrative with adventure storytelling. Later writers frequently repeated the account without independently verifying the existence of the original document, allowing the story to acquire an appearance of historical certainty through repetition rather than new evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogram_of_Olivier_Levasseur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span></span></span>

Researchers have also pointed out that the supposed decrypted text itself is problematic. Various proposed translations differ considerably, often producing awkward or incoherent French. Treasure hunters have repeatedly supplied alternative readings, but none has gained scholarly acceptance or led to any verifiable discovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cipherfoundation.org/older-ciphers/la-buse-cryptogram/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cipherfoundation.org">[The Cipher Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cipherfoundation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Cipher Foundation La Buse Cryptogram</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cipher FoundationLa Buse Cryptogram - The Cipher Foundation...</span></span></span>

## The disputed "Fiery Cross of Goa"

The cipher is closely connected to another famous element of the legend: the so-called "Fiery" or "Flaming Cross of Goa."

Popular retellings describe an enormous jewel-encrusted golden cross supposedly captured aboard *Nossa Senhora do Cabo* and hidden with Levasseur's treasure. The object is often presented as the greatest prize of the raid.

Here again, modern historical analysis paints a more cautious picture. Contemporary documentation confirms that valuable religious artefacts and a golden cross associated with the former Viceroy existed aboard the ship. However, the spectacular giant cross described in many treasure books appears to be a later embellishment rather than something clearly documented in eighteenth-century records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.news.com.au/travel/destinations/africa/pirate-shipwreck-may-hold-key-to-fate-of-210-million-in-treasure/news-story/8936596a6f00499784f13e3da30a04e6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.com.au">[News.com.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.com.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Seized by pirates Olivier Levasseur (La Buse) and John Taylor in 1721, the ship carried a vast cargo of gold, jewels, and religious artef...</span></span></span>

The gradual enlargement of this single artefact into an almost mythical object mirrors the evolution of the cryptogram itself: a small historical kernel surrounded by increasingly dramatic storytelling.

## Why treasure theories survive without proof

The remarkable durability of the La Buse legend comes from the combination of genuine historical mystery and psychological appeal.

The pirate unquestionably acquired immense wealth, yet historians cannot reconstruct every stage of its dispersal. That incomplete record creates an opening for speculation. Once a supposedly authentic cipher is added, every cave, shoreline or carved rock in the western Indian Ocean can be interpreted as another clue rather than simply a natural feature.

The legend also possesses an unusual self-reinforcing quality. Every failed expedition can be explained by arguing that the code has not yet been correctly deciphered instead of questioning whether the cipher itself is authentic. Because the alleged solution is never independently testable, the theory becomes almost impossible to disprove through treasure hunting alone.

This dynamic has encouraged generations of amateur cryptographers, explorers and commercial expeditions to search Mauritius, Réunion, Rodrigues, Madagascar and the Seychelles for evidence of the hidden cache, despite the absence of archaeological discoveries that conclusively support the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogram_of_Olivier_Levasseur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptogram of Olivier Levasseur</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery still matters in Mauritius

The enduring importance of the La Buse story lies less in the possibility of buried treasure than in what it reveals about historical memory.

Mauritius occupies a landscape genuinely connected with the Indian Ocean's pirate era, making the island a natural setting for legends that blend documented events with folklore. The La Buse narrative demonstrates how a real episode of maritime history evolved into a twentieth-century treasure myth through repeated retelling, selective use of evidence and the irresistible appeal of an unsolved code.

In that sense, the greatest mystery is no longer the location of Levasseur's fortune.
