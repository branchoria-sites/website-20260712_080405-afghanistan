## Introduction

The disappearance of the French explorer Jean-François de Galaup, comte de La Pérouse, became one of the eighteenth century's greatest maritime mysteries. Today, the central puzzle is no longer where his expedition was lost. Archaeology and oral tradition have established that his two ships were wrecked on the reefs of Vanikoro in the eastern [Solomon Islands]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-the-solomon/' | relative_url }}) around 1788. The enduring mystery concerns the survivors. Evidence indicates that a number of expedition members lived ashore for months, built a camp, constructed a small replacement vessel from the wreckage and eventually sailed away. None are known to have returned to Europe, and no definitive record has ever revealed where they ended their journey. The question of what became of those final survivors remains one of the Pacific's most compelling historical enigmas, because the evidence is unusually strong that they escaped Vanikoro, yet almost completely silent about what happened afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collection-laperouse.fr/en/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collection-laperouse.fr">[collection-laperouse.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collection-laperouse.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span></span></span>

## How the expedition was lost at Vanikoro

After leaving Botany Bay in March 1788, La Pérouse's expedition vanished from European knowledge. For decades its fate was unknown until the Irish-born trader Peter Dillon visited Vanikoro in 1826–1827 after local people showed him European objects, including sword fittings and silverware. His investigations convinced him that the missing French expedition had been wrecked there. A later expedition led by Jules Dumont d'Urville confirmed the identification and recovered further remains from the reefs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collection-laperouse.fr/en/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collection-laperouse.fr">[collection-laperouse.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collection-laperouse.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span></span></span>

Modern underwater archaeology has strengthened these nineteenth-century discoveries. Excavations have identified the wreck sites of both *La Boussole* and *L'Astrolabe*, recovering anchors, guns, navigational instruments, personal possessions and structural remains that leave little doubt about where the disaster occurred. The mystery therefore shifted from locating the ships to reconstructing the experiences of those who survived the wrecks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collection-laperouse.fr/en/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collection-laperouse.fr">[collection-laperouse.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collection-laperouse.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span></span></span>

## What happened after the wrecks?

The strongest evidence comes from a combination of Vanikoro oral traditions, early nineteenth-century interviews and archaeological discoveries made on land.

According to accounts recorded by Dillon and later by Dumont d'Urville, one ship was driven directly onto the reef and destroyed with heavy loss of life, while the crew of the other vessel managed to reach shore in greater numbers. The survivors salvaged timber, iron fittings, tools and supplies from the wrecks before establishing a camp inland. Local traditions consistently describe these Europeans living on the island for several months while constructing a smaller sailing vessel from recovered materials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collection-laperouse.fr/en/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collection-laperouse.fr">[collection-laperouse.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collection-laperouse.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span></span></span>

The oral histories also contain several recurring elements:

* they dismantled the wrecks to obtain usable timber and metal;
* after completing their vessel, most survivors departed by sea;

These stories were once viewed cautiously because they depended largely on local testimony collected decades after the events. Archaeology has since provided important support.

## The survivor camp and improvised vessel

One of the most significant developments came from excavations undertaken by the Association Salomon and later supported by the French Navy and research institutions. Beginning in the 1980s and especially after 1999, archaeologists identified what is now known as the "French Camp".

Objects recovered from the site included precision measuring instruments, a copper candlestick, navigational equipment and other unmistakably French artefacts dating to the expedition. Their location inland matches the long-standing descriptions that survivors established a semi-permanent settlement while preparing their escape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collection-laperouse.fr/en/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collection-laperouse.fr">[collection-laperouse.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collection-laperouse.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span></span></span>

The archaeological evidence does not reveal how many men occupied the camp or exactly how long they remained there. It does, however, make it much harder to dismiss the traditional accounts as legend. The physical remains support the idea that survivors organised themselves, continued using specialised equipment and maintained enough discipline and technical skill to undertake major construction work after the wrecks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collection-laperouse.fr/en/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collection-laperouse.fr">[collection-laperouse.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collection-laperouse.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span></span></span>

The vessel itself has never been found. Contemporary accounts suggest it was built from salvaged ship timbers combined with locally available materials. If completed successfully, it represented an extraordinary feat of maritime engineering under desperate conditions.

## Where did the survivors go?

This is where the historical trail largely disappears.

The traditional narrative ends with the improvised vessel sailing away from Vanikoro roughly six months after the wrecks. No reliable European record describes its arrival at another port, and no confirmed wreck attributable to the survivors has been identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00223344.2017.1335370" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineManuscript XXXIIThe Final Fate of the La Pérouse Expedition?The 1818 Account of Shaik Jumaul, A Lascar Castaway in...</span></span></span>

Several possibilities have been proposed.

**The vessel was lost at sea.** This remains the simplest explanation. Even an expertly built emergency craft faced thousands of kilometres of open Pacific, uncertain charts, storms and limited provisions. A second shipwreck could easily have left no recoverable evidence.

**The survivors reached another island but left no lasting record.** The Pacific contained numerous islands with little or no written documentation during the late eighteenth century. Small groups of Europeans might have died, been absorbed into local communities or disappeared before any subsequent European contact.

**The expedition reached Torres Strait or northern Australia.** One of the more intriguing modern suggestions arises from an 1818 newspaper account describing Shaik Jumaul, a lascar sailor rescued after years on Murray Island in Torres Strait. His story includes references that some researchers argue may preserve distant memories of La Pérouse's survivors. While fascinating, the evidence is indirect and cannot establish that the French actually reached the region. Most historians regard it as an interesting possibility rather than proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00223344.2017.1335370" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineManuscript XXXIIThe Final Fate of the La Pérouse Expedition?The 1818 Account of Shaik Jumaul, A Lascar Castaway in...</span></span></span>

## Searches, artefacts and unresolved reports

Searches have continued for nearly two centuries, but they have produced far more information about life on Vanikoro than about the final voyage.

Underwater excavations have recovered thousands of objects from the wrecks, including navigational instruments, weapons, ballast, ceramics and ship fittings. On land, repeated archaeological campaigns have searched for graves associated with the survivors, but no securely identified burial place for those who left aboard the improvised vessel has been discovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collection-laperouse.fr/en/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collection-laperouse.fr">[collection-laperouse.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collection-laperouse.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span></span></span>

Reports that one or two Europeans remained on Vanikoro after the departure also remain difficult to evaluate. Early accounts describe an officer and his servant staying behind, either by choice or necessity, and eventually dying on the island. These reports appear consistently enough in nineteenth-century testimony that historians generally take them seriously, yet no archaeological discovery has conclusively identified their graves or confirmed their identities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collection-laperouse.fr/en/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collection-laperouse.fr">[collection-laperouse.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collection-laperouse.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span></span></span>

The absence of definitive evidence has encouraged occasional speculation that descendants of survivors might exist elsewhere in the Pacific. No documentary or genetic evidence has yet demonstrated such a connection.

## Why the mystery still matters

The fate of La Pérouse's survivors illustrates how a historical mystery can become narrower rather than broader as evidence accumulates. The great question is no longer whether the expedition reached Vanikoro or whether people survived the wrecks. Those points are supported by archaeology, artefacts and remarkably consistent local traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collection-laperouse.fr/en/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collection-laperouse.fr">[collection-laperouse.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collection-laperouse.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Pérouse, le voyage et les recherches</span></span></span>

Instead, the remaining mystery concerns a single missing chapter: the final voyage of the improvised vessel. It is one of the rare maritime enigmas in which historians can identify the departure point with considerable confidence but not the destination.

For the Solomon Islands, Vanikoro represents more than the location of a famous shipwreck. It demonstrates the importance of combining archaeology with Indigenous oral history. Local accounts preserved crucial elements of the story—including the survivor camp and the escape vessel—for generations before excavations uncovered physical evidence that supported them. Even so, the ocean beyond Vanikoro has so far yielded no answer to the question that has lingered for more than two centuries: where did La Pérouse's last survivors finally meet their fate?
