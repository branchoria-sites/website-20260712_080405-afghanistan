## Introduction

The Yonaguni Monument, an underwater rock formation off [Japan]({{ 'japan/' | relative_url }})'s westernmost inhabited island, is one of the world's most famous archaeological controversies. Its broad terraces, sharp edges and step-like platforms look uncannily like the remains of an ancient monument. This striking appearance has inspired claims that it represents a lost city, a prehistoric temple or even evidence of a forgotten civilisation swallowed by rising seas after the last Ice Age.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Japan_fcf29f_yonaguni_formation_d_cd1ff7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Yonaguni illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Japan_fcf29f_yonaguni_formation_d_cd1ff7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Japan_fcf29f_yonaguni_formation_d_cd1ff7-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The mystery is compelling because the formation genuinely looks artificial in photographs and while diving. Yet after decades of geological study, the strongest evidence still favours a natural explanation. The debate is therefore less about whether the rocks are unusual—they undoubtedly are—and more about how easily the human brain interprets natural patterns as architecture. Yonaguni has become an important case study in distinguishing geological processes from archaeological evidence, and in understanding how modern myths can develop around spectacular landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/yonaguni-jima-japan-underwater-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicJapan&#x27;s Ancient Underwater &quot;Pyramid&quot; Mystifies Scholars &#124; National GeographicSeptember 19, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2007</span></span></span>

## Why the formation looks artificial

The monument lies around 25 metres below the surface on the southern side of Yonaguni Island in the Ryukyu chain. Divers encounter a massive sandstone outcrop with remarkably straight edges, broad flat platforms, apparent staircases and deep channels separating rectangular blocks. Some surfaces appear almost perfectly level, while corners sometimes approach right angles.

From many viewpoints the formation resembles a stepped pyramid or ceremonial platform. Individual terraces stretch for tens of metres, giving the impression of deliberate planning on a monumental scale. Because underwater visibility can be excellent around Yonaguni, photographs often reinforce the impression that viewers are looking at ruined architecture rather than exposed bedrock.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/yonaguni-jima-japan-underwater-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicJapan&#x27;s Ancient Underwater &quot;Pyramid&quot; Mystifies Scholars &#124; National GeographicSeptember 19, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2007</span></span></span>

Marine geologist Masaaki Kimura became the site's best-known advocate for an archaeological interpretation. After years of surveying the formation, he argued that some features—including terraces, possible roadways, holes and what he interpreted as carved elements—were too regular to be entirely natural. He suggested that people may have constructed or extensively modified the site before post-glacial sea-level rise submerged it thousands of years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/yonaguni-jima-japan-underwater-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicJapan&#x27;s Ancient Underwater &quot;Pyramid&quot; Mystifies Scholars &#124; National GeographicSeptember 19, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2007</span></span></span>

These ideas attracted widespread public attention because they appeared to offer evidence of a sophisticated prehistoric culture in East Asia far earlier than generally accepted archaeological timelines.

## How geology explains the terraces and steps

Most geologists who have examined the site argue that the same characteristics which make Yonaguni appear artificial can be explained through ordinary geological processes.

The monument consists mainly of layered sandstone and mudstone belonging to the Yaeyama Group. Sedimentary rocks of this type naturally split along flat bedding planes. At the same time, tectonic stresses create vertical fractures known as joints. Where horizontal bedding intersects vertical joint systems, large rectangular blocks can break away, producing flat surfaces, straight edges and step-like forms without any human involvement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonaguni_Monument" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Yonaguni Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yonaguni Monument</span></span></span>

Yonaguni also lies in one of the world's most tectonically active regions. Earthquakes repeatedly fracture the bedrock, while waves, currents and erosion gradually widen existing cracks. Over thousands of years this combination can generate remarkably geometric landscapes.

One of the strongest arguments for a natural origin is that comparable formations occur above sea level on Yonaguni itself. These exposed sandstone cliffs display many of the same stepped profiles, sharp corners and blocky fractures without requiring any archaeological explanation. Their similarity suggests that the underwater monument represents the same geological processes acting below sea level rather than the remains of a submerged city.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonaguni_Monument" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Yonaguni Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yonaguni Monument</span></span></span>

More recent geomorphological studies using digital elevation models and detailed field observations have likewise concluded that weathering acting on fractured sandstone adequately explains the monument's overall appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonaguni_Monument" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Yonaguni Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yonaguni Monument</span></span></span>

## What archaeological proof is still missing

The central weakness of the lost-city hypothesis is not that unusual-looking rocks exist, but that convincing archaeological evidence does not.

If Yonaguni were the remains of a substantial prehistoric settlement, archaeologists would normally expect to find at least some of the following:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Stone tools or construction implements.
* Pottery or other cultural artefacts.
* Human burials.
* Occupation layers containing charcoal, food remains or other evidence of habitation.
* Clearly identifiable carved inscriptions or sculptures.
* Repeated architectural features that cannot be explained by natural fracture patterns.

</div>

None of these have been demonstrated at the monument itself to the standard normally required in archaeology. Claims of tool marks, carvings and symbolic figures remain disputed and have not gained broad acceptance among specialists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonaguni_Monument" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Yonaguni Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yonaguni Monument</span></span></span>

The absence of associated cultural material is especially significant because archaeology rarely identifies ancient settlements from architectural appearance alone. Human occupation almost always leaves multiple independent lines of evidence.

## Could people have modified a natural formation?

One possibility occasionally discussed by researchers occupies a middle ground between the competing extremes.

Geologist Robert Schoch, while rejecting the idea that the monument was primarily constructed by humans, has suggested that ancient inhabitants may have made limited use of an already-existing natural rock formation. According to this interpretation, people could have altered small sections, quarried stone or adapted naturally flat surfaces without creating the overall monument itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.robertschoch.com/yonaguni.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: robertschoch.com">[robertschoch.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">robertschoch.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Robert M. Schoch: Younaguni</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Robert M. Schoch: Younaguni</span></span></span>

Such mixed explanations are not unusual elsewhere in the world. Many cultures have incorporated striking natural cliffs, caves and rock platforms into ceremonial landscapes.

However, no conclusive evidence currently demonstrates that this happened at Yonaguni. Even this more limited hypothesis remains unproven.

## How the lost-city myth grew

Yonaguni became internationally famous because its visual impact coincided with growing public fascination with lost civilisations such as Atlantis and the hypothetical continent of Mu or Lemuria.

Television documentaries, popular books and internet discussions often highlighted photographs showing the most geometric parts of the monument while giving less attention to the surrounding fractured geology. Writers interested in alternative history presented the site as evidence that advanced societies existed before the end of the last Ice Age, linking Yonaguni with wider theories about forgotten global civilisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/yonaguni-jima-japan-underwater-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicJapan&#x27;s Ancient Underwater &quot;Pyramid&quot; Mystifies Scholars &#124; National GeographicSeptember 19, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2007</span></span></span>

The site's underwater setting also contributes to its appeal. Objects beneath the sea are inherently difficult to study, allowing speculation to flourish in the absence of easily accessible evidence.

This combination of dramatic imagery, genuine scientific disagreement over details and wider fascination with submerged worlds transformed Yonaguni from an unusual dive site into one of the best-known archaeological mysteries in popular culture.

## Why Yonaguni still matters

The enduring importance of Yonaguni lies less in whether it conceals a lost civilisation than in what it teaches about interpreting extraordinary claims.

The monument demonstrates how powerful visual impressions can be. Human perception is highly sensitive to straight lines, symmetry and patterns that resemble buildings, even when natural processes produce them. Scientists therefore rely not only on appearance but also on multiple independent forms of evidence—geology, archaeology, dating methods and material remains—before identifying ancient structures.

For Japan, Yonaguni has become an enduring historical mystery precisely because it sits at the boundary between geology and archaeology. It remains an exceptional natural formation and a remarkable diving destination, but the balance of current evidence indicates that its terraces and steps are products of fractured sandstone shaped by tectonic activity and erosion rather than the ruins of a forgotten city. Until convincing archaeological evidence emerges, the lost-city interpretation remains an intriguing hypothesis rather than an established chapter of Japanese history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonaguni_Monument" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Yonaguni Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yonaguni Monument</span></span></span>
