## Introduction

Each puzzle is different. Warner’s death survives through contradictory colonial testimony. The Maroons left a landscape whose paths and settlements have barely been excavated. LaSoye shows that commerce and cultural exchange took place long before Britain formally colonised Dominica. The Boiling Lake mystery, meanwhile, has moved from eyewitness wonder towards scientific explanation, although its sudden changes remain difficult to predict. Together, these cases reveal why Dominica’s unresolved history is less about hidden conspiracies than about whose evidence survived, what the forest concealed and what storms unexpectedly brought back to light.

## What happened at Massacre in 1674?

The village now called Massacre preserves the memory of one of Dominica’s most troubling colonial episodes. In 1674, an armed force from Antigua led by Lieutenant-Governor Philip Warner attacked Kalinago people on Dominica. Thomas Warner, commonly called Indian Warner in English documents, was among those killed. He was the son of the English coloniser Sir Thomas Warner and a Kalinago woman, and was therefore Philip Warner’s half-brother.

The broad facts are well established: an Antiguan expedition landed on Dominica, scores of Kalinago were killed, Thomas Warner died and Philip Warner was subsequently accused of serious wrongdoing. What remains contested is how the killings began, whether Thomas Warner was deliberately betrayed and how many victims there were.

One colonial version portrayed the expedition as retaliation for Kalinago attacks on Antigua. Governor William Stapleton reported that Philip Warner’s force killed about 80 people, destroyed provisions and carried away canoes. In that account, Thomas Warner simply “fell” among the opposing Kalinago. A sharply different account came from Sir Jonathan Atkins, the governor of Barbados. According to the surviving summary of his report, Thomas Warner initially joined Philip in action against another Kalinago group. Philip then invited his half-brother and his followers to a feast, supplied them with rum and ordered their killing after they had become intoxicated. Atkins condemned the episode as an act of extreme inhumanity and said that children were not spared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dn790006.ca.archive.org/0/items/1964colonialrecordsc09greauoft/1964colonialrecordsc09greauoft.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dn790006.ca.archive.org">[Archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dn790006.ca.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Calendar of State Papers...... These versions cannot be neatly reconciled. One describes a military victory; the other describes an alliance followed by calculated betrayal. Both came from colonial officials with their own political interests, and neither gives the Kalinago victims a direct voice. The documentary mystery is therefore not whether a massacre occurred, but how far the official justification concealed the circumstances.

The incident also exposes the unstable position occupied by Thomas Warner. English authorities had previously used his family connections and influence to advance English interests on Dominica. Yet his Kalinago identity and alliances made him suspect to neighbouring colonial governments. His death can be read as a family betrayal, a struggle between competing colonial jurisdictions and an attempt to destroy a powerful intermediary who could not be controlled reliably.

The place name Massacre has ensured that the event was never entirely forgotten. Dominica’s Division of Culture identifies the modern settlement with the killing of Thomas Warner and the Kalinago community associated with him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://divisionofculture.gov.dm/resource-information2/historical-information/a-short-history-of-the-village-of-warner" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: divisionofculture.gov.dm">[divisionofculture.gov.dm]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">divisionofculture.gov.dm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Short History of the Village of Warner - The Division of CultureHe was related to the Kalinago chief, Thomas &#x27;Carib&#x27; Warner, who was ki...</span></span></span> The mystery matters because the conflicting records show colonial history being manufactured almost in real time: the same violence could be recorded either as lawful punishment or as treacherous slaughter.

## How much of Maroon Dominica is still hidden?

For decades during the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, Africans who escaped slavery established communities in Dominica’s mountainous interior. These Maroons cultivated food, sheltered other fugitives, maintained communication routes and resisted colonial forces. Their best-known leader was Jacko, who survived in the forest for more than 40 years before being killed in July 1814 during the Second Maroon War. Dominica’s official cultural history records that Rangers shot him after a long campaign against his community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://divisionofculture.gov.dm/resource-information2/national-figures/jacko" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: divisionofculture.gov.dm">[divisionofculture.gov.dm]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">divisionofculture.gov.dm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Division of CultureDuring The Second Maroon War 1812-1814 he was killed, shot dead in July 1814 by Rangers after having been in the f...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1814</span></span></span>

Jacko’s death is documented more clearly than many popular accounts suggest. The deeper mystery concerns how the Maroon world actually functioned. Colonial reports concentrated on raids, military expeditions and bounties. They rarely described ordinary life inside the camps except when such details could help destroy them. The communities themselves left few conventional written records.

Dominica’s terrain made concealment possible. Deep valleys, steep slopes and dense forest separated the plantation districts from an interior that officials struggled to map or control. Modern geographical research has identified nine known Maroon camps and examined their relationship to visibility, access routes and defensive positions. The results suggest that the camps were not merely isolated hiding places. Their locations supported movement, communication and networks of mutual assistance across the interior.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of...by J Rodriguez · 2024 · Cited by 3 — This stud...</span></span></span>

This changes the central question. Rather than asking only where fugitives hid, researchers are asking how Maroon communities created a durable political landscape within a slave colony. Colonial officials themselves recognised this rival authority. Jacko was reported as calling himself the “governor of the woods”, an expression that conveyed both independence and control over territory beyond the effective reach of the plantation government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA/S0002731624000489a.pdf/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Yet much of the physical evidence remains untested. A 2024 study noted that the known Maroon encampments had not been systematically investigated through archaeological excavation. Geographic information systems can model defensibility and probable routes, but they cannot by themselves reveal how houses were built, what people ate, which imported objects reached the camps or how long particular settlements remained occupied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/an-imperium-in-imperio-a-geospatial-analysis-of-defensibility-and-accessibility-of-maroon-settlements-in-dominica/546802B275644DA0575A0A0C419C1CFA" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentAn imperium in imperio: A Geospatial Analysis of...by J Rodriguez · 2024 · Cited by 3 — This stud...</span></span></span>

Jacko Flats and the stone steps associated with the settlement have become prominent sites of public memory. Oral tradition links them to Jacko’s community and its protected access routes. Such traditions are historically valuable, but archaeology is needed to distinguish different phases of construction and occupation. Some features may have been altered, repaired or reinterpreted over time.

The Maroon mystery is therefore not a search for a vanished tribe or secret city. It is the incomplete reconstruction of an organised resistance society whose members deliberately avoided the record-keeping institutions that sought to capture them. The missing evidence is itself part of the story: concealment was essential to survival.

## The settlement that Hurricane Maria uncovered

In 2017, storm surge from Hurricane Maria stripped away sand near LaSoye Point on Dominica’s north-eastern coast. Beneath it lay evidence of a settlement that had largely disappeared from historical memory. Excavation uncovered European ceramics, glass beads, trade bells, shell ornaments, locally made pottery and objects combining Indigenous, African and European techniques.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2018/october/historical-artifacts-exposed-by-hurricane-maria-lead-to-archeological-discovery-beneath-the-sands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.northwestern.edu">[Northwestern Now]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.northwestern.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northwestern.edu.</span></span></span>

The site appears to have been a seventeenth- and eighteenth-century trading centre, probably involving Dutch merchants and Kalinago communities. Researchers have described it as the first archaeological example of a Dutch trading factory of this period identified in the Caribbean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/where-strangers-met-evidence-for-early-commerce-at-lasoye-point-dominica/5F062226C2687B369F5F4B7AC0DCF7A3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

That conclusion is important because standard political chronologies can make Dominica appear comparatively detached from European colonisation before British rule began in 1763. France and Britain formally agreed in 1660 to treat Dominica as neutral Kalinago territory, although traders, missionaries, settlers and timber cutters continued to enter the island. LaSoye provides material evidence that neutrality on paper did not mean isolation.

The harbour explains part of the site’s importance. LaSoye Point and its reef offered shelter to ships approaching from the Atlantic. Archaeologists have identified traces of a road and harbour-related structures, while later maritime work reported an abandoned warehouse, a seawall, a docking feature and shipwreck remains in the bay.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/where-strangers-met-evidence-for-early-commerce-at-lasoye-point-dominica/5F062226C2687B369F5F4B7AC0DCF7A3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Several questions remain unresolved:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* Who permanently lived at LaSoye, rather than visiting it to trade?
* How much authority did Kalinago leaders exercise over the exchange?
* Were the European residents officially sponsored merchants, private traders or people operating beyond the rules of imperial governments?
* How did African people participate in the settlement?
* Why did LaSoye disappear so thoroughly from later written histories?

</div>

The artefacts suggest more than simple barter between two separate societies. Some objects combine materials and techniques associated with different cultural traditions, indicating that people exchanged knowledge as well as goods. The site may therefore record a mixed frontier community that does not fit later colonial categories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2018/october/historical-artifacts-exposed-by-hurricane-maria-lead-to-archeological-discovery-beneath-the-sands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.northwestern.edu">[Northwestern Now]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.northwestern.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northwestern.edu.</span></span></span>

LaSoye also demonstrates the fragility of Dominica’s archaeological record. Coastal erosion and hurricanes can destroy sites, but they can also expose remains that have been buried for centuries. Maria revealed LaSoye while simultaneously placing it at greater risk. The mystery is now a race against further erosion: each season may produce new evidence or remove material before it can be studied.

## Why does the Boiling Lake sometimes vanish?

Dominica’s Boiling Lake lies in the Valley of Desolation within Morne Trois Pitons National Park, a rugged volcanic landscape that UNESCO describes as containing multiple active volcanic centres, steep canyons, fumaroles and hot springs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/814/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unesco.org.</span></span></span> The lake is essentially a flooded fumarole: groundwater collects above a source of volcanic heat, producing temperatures of roughly 80–90°C around its edge during normal conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uwiseismic.com/island-profiles/dominica/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwiseismic.com">[uwiseismic.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwiseismic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uwiseismic.com.</span></span></span>

Its unsettling feature is not simply that it boils. On several recorded occasions, the water level has dropped dramatically, the normal churning has ceased and the lake has appeared almost empty. Such episodes occurred in or around 1876, 1887, 1900, 1901, 1971, 1972, 1988 and 2004–05.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0377027318305729" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

The 1901 episode was deadly. When the lake was nearly empty, a small steam-and-gas explosion released harmful gases, probably dominated by carbon dioxide, killing two people at the water’s edge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uwiseismic.com/sp_faq/is-there-any-danger-associated-with-the-draining-of-the-boiling-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwiseismic.com">[uwiseismic.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwiseismic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">is there any danger associated with the draining of the boiling lake</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">is there any danger associated with the draining of the boiling lake</span></span></span> Because carbon dioxide is colourless and can accumulate in low ground, people may receive little warning before concentrations become dangerous.

Early observers could describe the changes but had limited means to explain them. Modern studies point to a hydrothermal system sensitive to changes in rainfall, underground water circulation, steam pressure, landslides and temporary blockages. Water may drain through fractures or underground channels. If a vent becomes obstructed and pressure builds beneath it, the system can produce a steam-driven explosion without the movement of magma associated with a larger volcanic eruption.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0377027318305729" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

This makes the lake mysterious in a precise rather than supernatural sense. Scientists understand the main mechanisms, but the underground plumbing cannot be observed directly. Different combinations of water supply, vent blockage and pressure may produce similar changes at the surface. It is therefore difficult to predict exactly when the lake will drain, refill or become hazardous.

A striking example occurred in 1988, when the level fell by about 30 feet and the water at the edge cooled to below 30°C. In 1972, the temperature reportedly dropped enough for local people to bathe in it. Such behaviour should not be taken as proof of safety: the 1901 deaths show that an apparently quiet or depleted lake may present greater gas and explosion risks than one boiling normally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uwiseismic.com/island-profiles/dominica/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwiseismic.com">[uwiseismic.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwiseismic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uwiseismic.com.</span></span></span>

The Boiling Lake has accumulated legends because its changes are sudden, visually dramatic and separated by long intervals. Yet this is also the case in which investigation has most clearly reduced the mystery. What once appeared to be a lake inexplicably dying and returning can now be understood as a fluctuating hydrothermal reservoir. The unresolved part is prediction, not basic cause.

## Buried treasure: tradition rather than verified discovery

Dominica’s caves, forested ravines and secluded bays naturally attract stories of hidden pirate wealth. Tales involving Captain William Kidd and unnamed Spanish treasure ships circulate in tourism material and oral tradition. No securely documented major pirate hoard, however, has been recovered on the island. Even promotional accounts of Dominica’s pirate legends generally concede the absence of a confirmed find.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dominicatours.org/insights/pirates-of-dominica-legends-hidden-treasures-and-maritime-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dominicatours.org">[Dominica Tours]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dominicatours.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dominicatours.org.</span></span></span>

Kidd’s genuine history also points elsewhere. His documented cache on Gardiners Island, near New York, was recovered by colonial authorities in 1699. The wreck of his vessel *Quedagh Merchant* was eventually identified off the Dominican Republic—an entirely different country whose similar name often causes confusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://underwaterscience.indiana.edu/research/dominican-republic/captain-kidd.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: underwaterscience.indiana.edu">[Center for Underwater Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">underwaterscience.indiana.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on indiana.edu.</span></span></span> There is no comparable documentary chain placing one of Kidd’s known stores on Dominica.

That does not make Dominican treasure narratives historically worthless. Research into the island’s oral traditions argues that treasure stories can encode memories of colonial violence, Indigenous resistance, social inequality and hopes of sudden reversal of fortune. In these tales, wealth is often guarded, cursed, revealed through dreams or recovered only by people who obey demanding conditions. Their significance lies as much in who is imagined to own the treasure as in whether metal lies beneath the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/20487674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Telling Treasure Tales: Commemoration</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Telling Treasure Tales: Commemoration</span></span></span>

LaSoye offers a useful contrast. It produced no legendary chest of gold, but it yielded the kind of “treasure” historians can verify: trade objects, ceramics, harbour remains and evidence of encounters excluded from official narratives. Dominica’s most important discoveries are more likely to come from careful archaeology than from maps marked with an X.

## What these mysteries reveal about Dominica

Dominica’s unresolved past is shaped by uneven survival. Colonial governments preserved reports about military operations but rarely the testimony of the people attacked. Maroons deliberately built beyond official sight. Coastal settlements vanished beneath vegetation and sand. Volcanic systems left spectacular effects while concealing their workings underground.

The best-supported conclusions are therefore cautious but meaningful. The 1674 massacre happened, although the precise sequence remains disputed. Jacko and other Maroon leaders controlled enduring communities, but their domestic and political life is only beginning to be reconstructed. LaSoye proves that substantial international trade and cultural exchange preceded formal British colonisation, although the identities and relationships of its inhabitants remain incomplete. The Boiling Lake’s draining is a natural hydrothermal process, but its timing and exact subterranean pathways cannot yet be predicted reliably.

These are not mysteries because nothing is known. They endure because each contains a boundary between evidence and silence. That boundary—between a colonial accusation and a victim’s absent voice, between a mapped camp and an unexcavated settlement, or between a scientific mechanism and an unpredictable event—is where Dominica’s history remains most open to discovery.
