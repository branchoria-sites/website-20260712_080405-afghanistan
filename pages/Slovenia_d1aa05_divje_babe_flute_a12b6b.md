## Introduction

The Divje Babe flute debate centres on one of the most controversial prehistoric objects ever discovered. In 1995, archaeologists excavating Divje Babe I cave in western [Slovenia]({{ 'slovenia/' | relative_url }}) uncovered part of the thigh bone of a juvenile cave bear containing a series of aligned holes. If those holes were deliberately made by Neanderthals around 50,000–60,000 years ago, the object would represent the oldest known musical instrument and powerful evidence that Neanderthals possessed symbolic behaviour previously associated mainly with modern humans. If, however, the holes resulted from carnivores gnawing the bone, the artefact loses its extraordinary significance and becomes an unusual but natural fossil. More than two decades later, the object remains one of archaeology's most enduring debates because the evidence can be interpreted in sharply different ways and no single test has settled the question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

## What was found in Divje Babe cave?

The object was recovered during excavations led by Slovenian archaeologist Ivan Turk at Divje Babe I, a cave near Cerkno. It comes from a Middle Palaeolithic layer associated with Mousterian stone tools, a technology widely linked to Neanderthals in Europe. Radiometric dating and the cave's stratigraphy place the layer roughly between 50,000 and 60,000 years old, long before modern humans are thought to have spread widely across this part of Europe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.si/en/collections/highlights/343-Neanderthal-flute" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.si">[nms.si]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.si</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Neanderthal flute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neanderthal flute</span></span></span>

The surviving specimen is only part of the original bone. It preserves two complete holes and fragments of two more, while both ends are broken away. This incomplete state is one reason the debate has proved so difficult to resolve. Researchers must infer the original shape of the object, the total number of holes, and whether damage occurred before, during or after any possible human modification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

The National Museum of Slovenia displays the object as a Neanderthal flute and argues that it demonstrates musical ability among Neanderthals. Many researchers accept the importance of the discovery but remain unconvinced that the evidence proves deliberate manufacture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.si/en/collections/highlights/343-Neanderthal-flute" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.si">[nms.si]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.si</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Neanderthal flute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neanderthal flute</span></span></span>

## Why some researchers believe it was deliberately made

Supporters of the flute interpretation argue that several observations fit intentional manufacture better than accidental damage.

The first concerns the arrangement of the holes. The best-preserved perforations occur in a line along the shaft of the bone in positions that could allow finger placement on a wind instrument. Advocates argue that reproducing such spacing through random carnivore bites would be improbable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

A second argument comes from experimental archaeology. Researchers have attempted to recreate similar perforations using stone tools available to Neanderthals. These experiments suggest that carefully punching or piercing the bone can produce openings without necessarily leaving the obvious scraping marks expected from later drilling techniques. According to supporters, this helps explain why conventional manufacturing traces may be absent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

Computed tomography (CT) scanning and microscopic studies have also been used to reassess earlier claims that certain marks around the holes must have been produced by carnivore teeth. Some investigators argue that the internal structure of the perforations and associated damage is not fully consistent with known bite patterns and may instead preserve traces compatible with human modification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

Finally, musicians have constructed playable replicas based on proposed reconstructions of the missing portions. These replicas can produce recognisable scales and melodies, demonstrating that the surviving hole pattern is acoustically viable. Advocates stress that this does not prove the object was a flute, but they argue it shows the design is compatible with an intentionally made musical instrument rather than merely resembling one by coincidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

## Why the carnivore theory survives

Despite these arguments, many archaeologists continue to regard the carnivore explanation as plausible or even more convincing.

The strongest objection is taphonomic—the study of what happens to remains after death. Carnivores such as hyenas and other large predators regularly puncture and chew long bones while extracting marrow. Similar punctured cave-bear bones have been documented at other Palaeolithic sites, leading critics to argue that aligned holes can arise naturally through feeding behaviour rather than deliberate craftsmanship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

Sceptics also point to the absence of unmistakable manufacturing evidence. Accepted Upper Palaeolithic bone flutes made by modern humans generally preserve clear traces of shaping, scraping or drilling. The Divje Babe specimen lacks such widely accepted indicators, making it difficult to demonstrate intentional workmanship beyond reasonable doubt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

The condition of the bone itself raises further questions. Both ends are broken, and critics interpret some fractures and damage as typical consequences of carnivore chewing. If the specimen was heavily gnawed before burial, the aligned holes may simply represent surviving punctures within a damaged shaft rather than finger holes in a musical instrument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

Another caution concerns the musical reconstructions. A reconstructed replica can only be based on assumptions about missing portions of the original object. Because different reconstructions are possible, the fact that one version produces pleasing musical notes cannot establish that prehistoric people intended the bone to function as an instrument. A naturally perforated tube may also generate sound if blown correctly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

## Why the debate matters beyond one bone

The importance of the Divje Babe find extends well beyond the object itself because it influences broader questions about Neanderthal cognition.

If the bone really is a flute, it would suggest that Neanderthals deliberately produced musical instruments tens of thousands of years before the earliest undisputed flutes associated with modern humans. That would strengthen growing evidence that Neanderthals engaged in symbolic behaviour, sophisticated planning and complex cultural practices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

If the carnivore explanation is correct, the lesson is equally significant. It demonstrates how easily natural processes can mimic human workmanship and why archaeologists require multiple independent lines of evidence before accepting extraordinary claims about prehistoric behaviour. The Divje Babe specimen has therefore become a frequently cited case study in archaeological interpretation, illustrating how context, experimental replication, imaging techniques and taphonomic analysis must all be weighed together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

## Where the evidence stands today

Few aspects of the discovery are disputed. Researchers broadly agree that the bone came from a juvenile cave bear, that it was recovered from a Middle Palaeolithic context associated with Neanderthals, and that it dates to approximately 50,000–60,000 years ago. The unresolved issue is the origin of the holes themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.si/en/collections/highlights/343-Neanderthal-flute" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.si">[nms.si]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.si</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Neanderthal flute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neanderthal flute</span></span></span>

Recent studies have continued to defend the deliberate-manufacture hypothesis using experimental archaeology, CT imaging and microscopic analysis, arguing that a purely carnivore explanation does not satisfactorily account for all observed features. At the same time, many specialists remain unconvinced, maintaining that comparable bite damage exists elsewhere and that unequivocal manufacturing traces are still lacking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003552118300633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mousterian Musical Instrument from the Divje babe I cave (Slovenia): Arguments on the Material Evidence for Neanderthal...</span></span></span>

For that reason, the Divje Babe flute remains one of Slovenia's most compelling archaeological mysteries. The object has not been disproved as a Neanderthal instrument, but neither has it achieved universal acceptance. Its enduring fascination lies in how a single damaged bone continues to shape one of the biggest questions in prehistoric archaeology: how closely Neanderthal minds resembled our own.
