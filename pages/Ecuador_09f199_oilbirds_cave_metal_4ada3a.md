## Introduction

The Cave of the Oilbirds, better known internationally as Cueva de los Tayos in [Ecuador]({{ 'ecuador/' | relative_url }})'s Morona-Santiago province, is a genuine natural cave system with a long history of Indigenous use. Its greatest mystery is not whether the cave exists—it unquestionably does—but whether it contains a hidden "metal library": shelves of engraved metal books supposedly left by an unknown ancient civilisation. Despite decades of publicity, expeditions and speculation, no independently verified evidence has ever demonstrated that such a library exists. The enduring puzzle lies in how an unsupported claim became one of the world's best-known archaeological legends and why it continues to influence popular ideas about lost civilisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_los_Tayos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cueva de los Tayos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cueva de los Tayos</span></span></span>

## How the metal library story began

The story originated with the Hungarian-born explorer Juan Móricz, who announced in the 1960s that he had discovered an extraordinary underground complex in Ecuador. According to his accounts, the cave system contained perfectly engineered passages and a vast archive of engraved metal plates preserving the history of an advanced civilisation. Móricz never produced photographs, maps, inscriptions or independently examined artefacts proving the existence of this library. Nor did he reveal a verifiable location that other researchers could investigate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_los_Tayos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cueva de los Tayos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cueva de los Tayos</span></span></span>

The claim became internationally famous after Erich von Däniken incorporated it into his 1972 book *The Gold of the Gods*. Von Däniken described artificial tunnels, immense treasures and metallic books as evidence supporting his wider theories about ancient advanced civilisations and extraterrestrial influence. The publicity reached millions of readers and transformed an obscure exploration claim into a global mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_von_D%C3%A4niken" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Erich von Däniken</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Erich von Däniken</span></span></span>

However, the foundation of the story proved remarkably fragile. Móricz never allowed independent verification of the supposed library, while von Däniken later acknowledged that he had not personally explored the cave in the manner described in his book and that parts of the narrative had been embellished. Those admissions significantly weakened the credibility of the original account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_von_D%C3%A4niken" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Erich von Däniken</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Erich von Däniken</span></span></span>

## What the 1976 expedition actually reported

The extraordinary claims prompted one of the largest cave expeditions ever organised in South America. In 1976 a British-Ecuadorian scientific expedition led by Stan Hall entered Cueva de los Tayos with more than one hundred participants, including experienced cavers, archaeologists, zoologists, geologists, Ecuadorian military personnel and former astronaut Neil Armstrong as honorary president.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_los_Tayos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cueva de los Tayos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cueva de los Tayos</span></span></span>

The expedition achieved several important scientific results:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* It produced one of the most detailed surveys of the cave system completed up to that time.
* Researchers documented the cave's geology, wildlife and archaeological remains.
* They identified evidence of prehistoric human activity, including ancient burial sites, but nothing resembling a library of engraved metal records.
* They found no convincing evidence that the principal cave passages had been artificially engineered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_los_Tayos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cueva de los Tayos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cueva de los Tayos</span></span></span>

</div>

The absence of the alleged library did not completely end the legend. Supporters argued that the expedition had searched the wrong chamber or even the wrong cave altogether. Because the supposed location had never been publicly identified, the claim became difficult to falsify: every unsuccessful search could simply be dismissed as having missed the "real" site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_los_Tayos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cueva de los Tayos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cueva de los Tayos</span></span></span>

## Why natural geology explains the cave better

One reason the legend proved persuasive is that parts of Cueva de los Tayos genuinely appear unusual to first-time visitors. The cave contains long passages, high chambers and remarkably straight sections that can look engineered under artificial light.

Modern speleology—the scientific study of caves—offers a much simpler explanation. The cave developed within limestone through natural geological processes over immense periods. Water exploited fractures in the rock, gradually enlarging them into extensive passageways. Some sections display angular walls because they follow natural fault lines rather than because they were cut by human builders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_los_Tayos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cueva de los Tayos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cueva de los Tayos</span></span></span>

Natural cave systems around the world often produce surprisingly geometric formations without requiring ancient engineering. While isolated features inside Tayos have prompted continuing discussion, no geological survey has demonstrated an extensive artificial underground complex.

## Indigenous knowledge and how it differs from the legend

Long before modern explorers arrived, the Shuar people knew the cave well and used it for cultural and practical purposes, including traditional expeditions to collect young oilbirds, from which the cave takes its common English name.

This Indigenous history is well documented and should not be confused with later stories about hidden metal archives. The cave already possessed deep cultural significance without requiring lost civilisations or extraterrestrial visitors. Modern mythmaking often merges authentic Indigenous traditions with speculative claims introduced during the twentieth century, creating the impression that local knowledge supports the metal library story when the evidence does not demonstrate this.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discoveryuk.com/mysteries/secrets-of-the-cueva-de-los-tayos-the-golden-plates-of-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoveryuk.com">[Discovery UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoveryuk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discovery UKSecrets of the Cueva de los Tayos: The Golden Plates of History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discovery UK...</span></span></span>

## Why the story refuses to disappear

Several factors have allowed the legend to survive despite the lack of supporting evidence.

First, the cave is genuinely remote, making it easy to argue that undiscovered chambers still exist.

Second, the original claim cannot be directly tested because no independently verified location for the alleged library has ever been produced.

Third, the story became intertwined with broader popular interests in lost civilisations, hidden knowledge and ancient astronaut theories during the 1970s. Once those ideas entered books, television documentaries and later internet discussions, the cave became famous for the legend rather than for the available evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_von_D%C3%A4niken" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Erich von Däniken</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Erich von Däniken</span></span></span>

Even Stan Hall, who organised the 1976 expedition, continued to express personal belief that a metal library might exist somewhere, while acknowledging that he had never seen it himself. His position illustrates the difference between conviction and documented evidence: belief persisted, but verifiable proof did not emerge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_los_Tayos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cueva de los Tayos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cueva de los Tayos</span></span></span>

## What is established and what remains uncertain

Several points can now be stated with considerable confidence.

**Well established:**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Cueva de los Tayos is a real natural cave system with archaeological, geological and ecological importance.
* The Shuar people have known and used the cave for generations.
* The 1976 international expedition conducted extensive exploration and found no verified metal library or engineered underground archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_los_Tayos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cueva de los Tayos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cueva de los Tayos</span></span></span>

</div>

**Still uncertain:**

* Whether unexplored passages remain within the wider cave system.
* Whether undiscovered archaeological material may exist in parts of the cave not yet systematically investigated.

**Unsupported by reliable evidence:**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A vast archive of engraved metal books.
* An unknown civilisation that engineered the cave.
* Claims linking the cave to extraterrestrial visitors or advanced prehistoric technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_von_D%C3%A4niken" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Erich von Däniken</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Erich von Däniken</span></span></span>

## Why the mystery still matters

The metal library story remains important not because the evidence points towards a hidden archive, but because it demonstrates how historical mysteries are created. A mixture of genuine exploration, unverifiable testimony, bestselling popular books and spectacular scenery transformed a natural cave into one of the world's most enduring archaeological legends.

For Ecuador, the episode serves as a reminder that remarkable places do not require extraordinary claims to be historically significant. Cueva de los Tayos is already exceptional as a natural landmark and a place of Indigenous heritage. The mystery lies less beneath the rock than in the remarkable journey by which an unverified story became part of global popular culture.
