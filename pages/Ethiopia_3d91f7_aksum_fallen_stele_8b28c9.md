## Introduction

The broken Great Stele of Aksum is one of the ancient world's most striking engineering mysteries. Carved from a single block of stone and standing about 33 metres high, it was larger than any other surviving Aksumite monument. Yet today it lies shattered on the ground. The central question is not whether it fell—it clearly did—but *when* and *why*. Archaeologists broadly agree that the monument collapsed in antiquity, probably during or soon after its attempted erection, but there is no contemporary written account explaining the disaster. Instead, researchers rely on the monument itself: its foundations, fractures, surrounding archaeology and comparisons with the other stelae at Aksum. The result is a case where the evidence points towards some explanations more strongly than others, while leaving important uncertainties unresolved.

## The Monument and Its Engineering Challenge

The Great Stele was created during the height of the Kingdom of Aksum, probably in the third or fourth century AD. Unlike Egypt's obelisks, Aksumite stelae were elaborately carved to resemble multi-storey buildings with false doors, windows and decorative details. They marked elite underground tombs and demonstrated the kingdom's extraordinary ability to quarry, transport and shape enormous stone monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/abs/significance-and-symbolism-of-aksumite-stelae/037FA6C1BD373BF0E69B2923EEC7359B" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentThe Significance and Symbolism of Aksumite Stelae &#124; Cambridge Archaeological Journal &#124; Cambridge C...</span></span></span>

The largest completed standing stele at Aksum is around 24 metres tall, while the famous "Obelisk of Aksum" (Stela 2), returned from Italy in 2005 and re-erected with modern engineering, stands about 24 metres high. The Great Stele exceeded both in height and weight, with estimates approaching or exceeding 500 tonnes. Every stage of the project—from quarrying and transport to raising the monument into position—would have pushed Aksumite engineering close to its practical limits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/125" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Major archaeological discoveries in Aksum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMajor archaeological discoveries in Aksum - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## Why Do Most Archaeologists Think It Fell During Erection?

The strongest current explanation is that the monument failed during its original installation rather than centuries later.

Several observations support this interpretation:

* The Great Stele lies broken into large sections close to its intended location rather than having been moved after collapse.
* Archaeological evidence suggests it never functioned as a long-standing upright monument.
* Nearby structural remains appear to have been damaged by the falling stone, implying a catastrophic collapse early in its history.
* UNESCO has noted that the giant monolith probably collapsed immediately after erection, possibly destroying the nearby Nafas Machau temple.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/125" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Major archaeological discoveries in Aksum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMajor archaeological discoveries in Aksum - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

Raising such a monument would have required earthen ramps, ropes, timber supports and carefully prepared foundations. Even a slight imbalance during lifting could have caused the enormous weight to shift uncontrollably. Once the stone began to lean beyond its centre of gravity, recovery would have been virtually impossible.

Modern engineers studying the successful reinstallation of the smaller returned Obelisk have emphasised just how demanding the process is even with steel towers, cranes, carbon-fibre reinforcement and detailed structural calculations. That comparison highlights the immense technical challenge faced by Aksumite builders working with ancient methods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/228/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre UNESCO Presents Design for Reinstallation of Aksum Obelisk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreUNESCO Presents Design for Reinstallation of Aksum Obelisk - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## Competing Explanations for the Collapse

Although early collapse is widely favoured, several mechanisms have been proposed.

### Structural weakness within the stone

One possibility is that hidden flaws already existed inside the monolith. Tiny cracks created during quarrying or transport might have weakened the stone before lifting began.

When enormous bending forces developed during erection, those flaws could have expanded suddenly, causing the monument to fracture under its own weight. The visible breaks are consistent with a catastrophic structural failure, although they cannot by themselves prove whether cracking began before or after impact with the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/405692148_Collapse_and_Continuity_in_the_Kingdom_of_Aksum_Why_the_Stelae_Fell_but_the_Ark_Endured" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Collapse and Continuity in the Kingdom of Aksum: Why the Stelae Fell but the Ark EnduredJune 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2, 2026</span></span></span>

### Foundation failure

Another explanation focuses on the ground beneath the stele.

An upright monolith weighing several hundred tonnes concentrates enormous pressure onto its base. If the foundation trench was uneven, poorly compacted or cut into unstable soil, the monument could have tilted gradually before collapsing. Researchers have also suggested that inadequate anchoring and local soil conditions may have made some Aksumite stelae especially vulnerable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.annalsofgeophysics.eu/index.php/annals/article/view/4100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: annalsofgeophysics.eu">[Annals of Geophysics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">annalsofgeophysics.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Annals of GeophysicsDid earthquakes fell Aksum obelisks? &#124; Annals of GeophysicsNovember 25, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 25, 1995</span></span></span>

### Earthquakes

Northern [Ethiopia]({{ 'what-lies-behind-ethiopias-greatest/' | relative_url }}) lies within the wider East African Rift system, where earthquakes occur.

Geologists have argued that some of Aksum's fallen stelae probably collapsed because of seismic activity, particularly where weak foundations or erosion had already reduced stability. Earthquakes therefore remain a plausible explanation for certain monuments in the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.annalsofgeophysics.eu/index.php/annals/article/view/4100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: annalsofgeophysics.eu">[Annals of Geophysics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">annalsofgeophysics.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Annals of GeophysicsDid earthquakes fell Aksum obelisks? &#124; Annals of GeophysicsNovember 25, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 25, 1995</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that the Great Stele appears to have failed unusually early. If it collapsed during or immediately after erection, an earthquake becomes less convincing than an engineering accident. A later earthquake cannot be ruled out entirely, but it currently has less archaeological support for this specific monument.

### Deliberate destruction

Older traditions sometimes attributed fallen monuments to invading rulers or iconoclasts. Similar claims have been made regarding other Aksumite stelae, with stories ranging from medieval conquerors to military action centuries later.

However, these explanations fit the Great Stele poorly. Its breakage pattern and archaeological context do not resemble a monument that had stood successfully for generations before being intentionally toppled. Most specialists therefore regard deliberate destruction as a weaker explanation for this particular case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.annalsofgeophysics.eu/index.php/annals/article/view/4100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: annalsofgeophysics.eu">[Annals of Geophysics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">annalsofgeophysics.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Annals of GeophysicsDid earthquakes fell Aksum obelisks? &#124; Annals of GeophysicsNovember 25, 1995...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 25, 1995</span></span></span>

## What the Foundations and Fractures Reveal

The monument itself provides the strongest evidence.

Its fractures indicate an immense impact rather than gradual weathering. The enormous blocks remain close to where the monument was intended to stand, suggesting collapse occurred at or near the erection site.

Equally important is what archaeologists have *not* found. There is no convincing evidence that the Great Stele stood upright for centuries before falling. Had it remained standing for a long period, researchers might expect repairs, later architectural adaptations or clearer historical references to its presence. Instead, the archaeological picture is more consistent with an ambitious project that failed very early.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/125" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Major archaeological discoveries in Aksum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreMajor archaeological discoveries in Aksum - UNESCO World Heritage Centre...</span></span></span>

## Why the Mystery Cannot Be Solved Completely

The evidence has important limits.

No surviving Aksumite inscription records the collapse. No eyewitness description has survived, and later traditions were written centuries afterwards. Archaeologists therefore have to reconstruct events indirectly from physical evidence rather than historical testimony.

Several competing scenarios can fit much of the available evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* failure while being raised;
* collapse shortly after successful erection because of unstable foundations;
* structural weakness within the stone;
* an earthquake acting on an already unstable monument; or
* some combination of these factors.

</div>

Because the event happened roughly 1,700 years ago, later erosion, rebuilding and human activity have also altered the site, making precise reconstruction more difficult.

## Why the Fallen Stele Still Matters

The Great Stele is significant not simply because it fell, but because its collapse reveals the limits of one of Africa's greatest ancient engineering traditions.

The monument demonstrates that Aksum's builders possessed remarkable technical skill, yet even they faced practical constraints when attempting projects of unprecedented scale. Significantly, no larger monolith was attempted afterwards. Whether the collapse resulted from miscalculation, hidden structural flaws or unstable ground, it appears to have marked the practical limit of Aksumite monument building.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/405692148_Collapse_and_Continuity_in_the_Kingdom_of_Aksum_Why_the_Stelae_Fell_but_the_Ark_Endured" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Collapse and Continuity in the Kingdom of Aksum: Why the Stelae Fell but the Ark EnduredJune 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2, 2026</span></span></span>

The mystery therefore remains partly unresolved. Most specialists now favour an engineering failure during or immediately after erection, supported by the archaeological setting and fracture evidence. Earthquakes, foundation instability and weaknesses within the stone remain plausible contributing factors, but no single explanation can yet be proved beyond doubt. The fallen Great Stele continues to stand—or rather lie—as one of Ethiopia's clearest reminders that even the greatest achievements of ancient engineering carried enormous risks.
