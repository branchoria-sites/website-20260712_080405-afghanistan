## Introduction

The strongest cases include the uncertain purpose of the vast Ha‘amonga ‘a Maui stone gateway; the recently mapped settlement system beneath modern Tongatapu; the disputed identity and supposed treasure of the wreck believed to be the *Port au Prince*; the reported Tongan plot to kill Captain James Cook; and the larger question of whether Tonga once ruled a true Pacific “empire”. In each case, the basic history is real, but later retellings have often turned uncertainty into certainty. The useful mystery lies in separating what the physical evidence establishes from tradition, reasonable interpretation and modern embellishment.

## Why was Ha‘amonga ‘a Maui built?

Ha‘amonga ‘a Maui stands at Heketā on eastern Tongatapu. It consists of two enormous upright slabs of coral limestone supporting a third stone lintel, fitted into cut sockets. The monument is exceptional in Tonga: other structures at Heketā are mainly low platforms, whereas the trilithon rises conspicuously above the surrounding landscape. Archaeological and historical research connects the site with the early centre of the Tu‘i Tonga ruling line, before the political and ceremonial focus shifted westwards to Lapaha.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/9480579/Stone_architecture_monumentality_and_the_rise_of_the_early_Tongan_chiefdom" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stone architecture, monumentality and the rise of the early</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ha&#x27;amonga &#x27;a Maui (&#x27;burden of Maui&#x27;), is the most famous monument in Tonga. All other structures are low rectangular to square platforms...</span></span></span>

The monument is sometimes promoted as the “Stonehenge of the Pacific”, a comparison that encourages astronomical speculation. In the 1960s, King Taufa‘ahau Tupou IV proposed that marks on the lintel indicated the rising positions of the Sun at important points in the year. The idea remains popular, but archaeological reviews report that tests have not supported the claim that Ha‘amonga functioned as a reliable solar observatory. The relevant marks are small, their significance is disputed, and the structure’s orientation does not by itself prove deliberate astronomical measurement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://go.gale.com/ps/i.do?id=GALE%7CA398627718&amp;issn=0003598X&amp;it=r&amp;linkaccess=abs&amp;p=AONE&amp;sid=googleScholar&amp;sw=w&amp;v=2.1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: go.gale.com">[Gale]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">go.gale.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stone architecture, monumentality and the rise of the early...by G Clark · 2014 · Cited by 44 — A popular theory that the trilithon...</span></span></span>

A more grounded explanation treats Ha‘amonga as part of a royal and sacred landscape. The word *ha‘amonga* refers to a carrying pole supported across the shoulders, which resembles the structure’s two uprights and lintel. Nearby platforms, a three-tiered monument and a stone seat form a complex associated in tradition with the Tu‘i Tonga dynasty, chiefly authority and the presentation of tribute. Early missionary records also indicate that Heketā retained ritual importance long after it ceased to be the main royal residence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/9480579/Stone_architecture_monumentality_and_the_rise_of_the_early_Tongan_chiefdom" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stone architecture, monumentality and the rise of the early</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ha&#x27;amonga &#x27;a Maui (&#x27;burden of Maui&#x27;), is the most famous monument in Tonga. The gateway theory therefore has the strongest archaeological setting: Ha‘amonga may have marked an entrance, processional route or boundary within a royal compound. Yet “gateway” describes its possible practical position rather than its complete meaning. Monumental architecture also communicated power. Quarrying, transporting and fitting stones weighing many tonnes would have demonstrated a ruler’s ability to mobilise labour and command specialised knowledge.

Several traditions attribute the monument to the Tu‘i Tonga Tu‘itātui, commonly placed around the thirteenth century, while legendary accounts associate its impossible-looking stones with the culture hero Maui. These stories need not be treated as failed engineering explanations. Maui traditions express the monument’s extraordinary character, while dynastic traditions preserve its connection with political authority. The remaining mystery is not whether people could build it, but precisely what ceremonies occurred there and what its form communicated to those approaching the royal centre.

## The ancient landscape hidden beneath Tongatapu

For generations, Tonga’s monumental past was represented mainly by conspicuous structures: royal tombs, defensive earthworks, raised platforms and Ha‘amonga itself. Recent aerial laser scanning has shown that these visible monuments were only fragments of a much larger constructed landscape.

Light detection and ranging, usually shortened to lidar, measures the ground from the air with laser pulses. Because it can reveal slight differences in elevation beneath vegetation, it is particularly useful for identifying old roads, platforms, ditches and mounds. Research combining lidar with fieldwork has mapped thousands of earthworks across Tongatapu, including dense clusters of residential platforms and routes connecting settlements. One widely reported estimate identified close to 10,000 mounds in the island’s eastern district.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a...by P Parton · 2024 · Cited by 3 — The high accuracy lidar data provi...</span></span></span>

Popular reports described the discovery as a “lost city”. That phrase is memorable but misleading. Archaeologists did not find an abandoned stone metropolis unknown to local people. They identified the physical organisation of an extensive, low-density settlement system whose remains had become difficult to recognise at ground level. Houses, public spaces, roads and agricultural land were spread across a broad area rather than packed within walls and streets like a medieval European city.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10816-024-09647-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-Density Urbanisation: Prestate Settlement Growth in a...by P Parton · 2024 · Cited by 3 — The high accuracy lidar data provi...</span></span></span>

The dating is especially important. Excavations at the large earth mound of Mala‘e Vakapuna indicate that durable monumental construction was occurring roughly 1,500 years ago. This challenges older models that placed major architectural development in western Polynesia mainly after AD 1000. Researchers argue that large-scale settlement growth and organised construction began considerably earlier, although the densest urban development belongs to later centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/early-architecture-in-tonga-implications-for-the-development-of-polynesian-chiefdoms/504DD08C5013AEC50DBCB1C54F8B3804" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentEarly architecture in Tonga: implications for the...by G Clark · 2024 · Cited by 5 — The authors...</span></span></span>

The findings help resolve one puzzle while creating another. Tonga’s later rulers clearly inherited a landscape already shaped by many generations of organised labour. It is no longer adequate to imagine a sudden rise of political complexity shortly before the great stone tombs and chiefly centres appeared. The open question is how authority developed during the long period between early earthwork construction and the better-documented Tu‘i Tonga state.

Lidar maps structures but cannot identify every building’s precise date or purpose. Some mounds supported homes; others had ceremonial, chiefly, defensive or sporting functions. Features visible beside one another may have been constructed centuries apart. Archaeologists must therefore combine remote sensing with excavation, radiocarbon dating and local historical knowledge before treating the mapped landscape as a single functioning “city”.

The deeper significance is that an important Pacific form of urban life was hidden by expectations about what cities ought to look like. Tongatapu appears to have supported a dispersed but highly connected population, organised through roads, ranked residences and communal structures. The mystery was partly created by the survival of the landscape itself: because later communities continued living and farming among the remains, there was no dramatic ruin to announce that a complex settlement system had once existed.

## Is the wreck of the *Port au Prince* still holding treasure?

In November 1806, the British privateering and whaling ship *Port au Prince* reached the Ha‘apai islands after a long and unsuccessful voyage. The vessel had been authorised to attack enemy shipping during Britain’s wars with France and Spain, but it had also pursued whales and carried a varied cargo. At Lifuka, forces under the chief Fīnau ‘Ulukālala seized the ship. Most of the crew were killed, while a small number survived, including the teenage clerk William Mariner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Mariner_%28writer%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">William Mariner (writer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">William Mariner (writer</span></span></span>

Mariner lived in Tonga for about four years. After returning to Britain, he told his experiences to John Martin, who published them in 1817 as *An Account of the Natives of the Tonga Islands*. The book became one of the most detailed sources for Tongan politics, language and social life before extensive Christian missionary influence. It is also the principal narrative source for the fate of the *Port au Prince*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_404570_5/component/file_404569/tonga_mariner1817_o.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.mpg.de">[MPG.PuRe]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.mpg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mpg.de.</span></span></span>

According to the account, the Tongans valued the vessel chiefly for its iron, weapons and other useful materials. The ship was stripped and then destroyed or allowed to sink. Contemporary British shipping intelligence later reported that the *Port au Prince* had been taken at Tonga and its crew killed, confirming that the vessel disappeared there even though the report lacked the detail supplied by Mariner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_au_Prince_%281790_ship%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Port au Prince (1790 ship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Port au Prince (1790 ship</span></span></span>

The treasure story developed from claims that the vessel carried money, precious metals and valuables seized during its privateering voyage. Some accounts mention silver objects and ore, while later retellings describe a great fortune lying untouched on the seabed. This is where documented history gives way to speculation. A ship might carry valuable metal without possessing a neatly stored treasure chest, and anything obviously useful or precious could have been removed before it sank.

In 2012, divers announced the discovery of an old wreck near Foa in Ha‘apai and suggested that it was the *Port au Prince*. Copper sheathing reportedly dated the remains broadly to the correct period, while the location fitted Mariner’s story. News reports and official comments nevertheless described the identification as a belief or probability, not the result of a published archaeological investigation demonstrating the ship’s identity through a unique inscription, bell, weapon or construction record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2012-08-10/an-tongan-shipwreck-may-be-pirate-treasure-ship/4189676" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News Tongan wreck may be pirate treasure ship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC News Tongan wreck may be pirate treasure ship</span></span></span>

That distinction is frequently lost. Copper sheathing can show that a wreck belongs to the late eighteenth or early nineteenth century, but other European vessels operated in the Pacific during that period. Location and date make the *Port au Prince* identification plausible; they do not make it conclusive.

The supposed treasure is even less secure. Early coverage openly acknowledged that valuables would be present only if the wreck really was the *Port au Prince*, if the cargo accounts were accurate, if valuable material had not been removed in 1806, and if it had survived two centuries of storms, corrosion and disturbance. No well-documented public recovery has established the existence of a great hoard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.news.com.au/travel/travel-updates/tonga-shipwreck-may-be-pirate-treasure-ship-portauprince/news-story/2e56102106ade81be6f979bf67287098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.com.au">[News.com.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.com.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on com.au.</span></span></span>

The wreck therefore contains two nested mysteries. The first is archaeological: has the *Port au Prince* actually been found? The second is largely a treasure legend: did the ship carry a surviving fortune? The first has credible circumstantial support. The second remains an attractive possibility built upon several unproven assumptions.

The human history matters more than the imagined gold. The seizure occurred during a period of intense political competition in Tonga, when European ships offered chiefs access to iron and firearms as well as unfamiliar dangers. Mariner was not simply the survivor of an isolated shipwreck; he became a participant in Tongan life and warfare, and his account shaped how generations of outsiders understood the kingdom. Any future investigation of the wreck would be most valuable for what it could reveal about this violent encounter, not merely for what might be sold as treasure.

## Did Tongans plan to kill Captain Cook?

Captain James Cook visited Tonga during his second and third Pacific voyages and applied the name “Friendly Islands” after receiving elaborate hospitality. The name became one of the most enduring ironies in Pacific exploration history because later Tongan testimony claimed that chiefs had considered killing Cook and seizing his ships during the 1777 visit.

The alleged plan was not recorded by Cook as a plot he discovered. His journals instead describe large gatherings, ceremonies, exchanges and a generally warm reception, though relations were not free of theft, punishment and mutual suspicion. The murder story entered written history through later accounts, especially information preserved in Mariner’s book and subsequently discussed through Tongan oral traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.captaincooksociety.com/cooks-life/places/a-cook-s-tour-of-tonga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: captaincooksociety.com">[captaincooksociety.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">captaincooksociety.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on captaincooksociety.com.</span></span></span>

In the best-known version, Fīnau and other leaders debated how to separate Cook’s men from their weapons and attack them during a gathering. The proposal failed because the chiefs could not agree on the timing or method, or because circumstances changed before the assault could begin. Cook sailed away unaware that any such discussion had taken place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.captaincooksociety.com/cooks-life/places/a-cook-s-tour-of-tonga" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: captaincooksociety.com">[captaincooksociety.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">captaincooksociety.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on captaincooksociety.com.</span></span></span>

There is no strong reason to dismiss the tradition. European vessels carried immense quantities of iron, tools and weapons, and the *Port au Prince* was seized in a comparable operation less than thirty years later. Tongan leaders were capable of strategic debate, and hospitality did not prevent them from evaluating visitors as possible threats or sources of valuable material.

Yet the story cannot be reconstructed minute by minute. It was recorded after the event and passed through several layers: participants or community memory, Tongan informants, Mariner, and finally John Martin’s edited publication. Later discussions with Queen Sālote and other holders of genealogical and ceremonial knowledge enriched the interpretation of eighteenth-century Tonga, but they also demonstrate that the historical record combines European journals with orally transmitted explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://catalogue.leidenuniv.nl/discovery/fulldisplay?context=L&amp;docid=alma990032252150302711&amp;vid=31UKB_LEU%3AUBL_V1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalogue.leidenuniv.nl">[Leiden University Catalogue]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalogue.leidenuniv.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on leidenuniv.nl.</span></span></span>

The safest conclusion is that a proposal to attack Cook was probably remembered in Tongan historical tradition, but its precise organisation and proximity to execution remain uncertain. Claims that Cook escaped death by a few seconds, or that a fully agreed massacre was cancelled at the final instant, go beyond what the evidence can securely demonstrate.

The episode also corrects the misleading simplicity of the “Friendly Islands” label. Tongan hospitality was genuine and politically meaningful, not merely a disguise. At the same time, chiefs had their own interests and were not passive hosts in a European story of discovery. Friendship, rivalry, ceremony and strategic calculation could coexist. The real historical mystery is less about whether smiling hosts secretly became villains than about how two sides interpreted an encounter governed by very different expectations.

## Was there really a Tongan Empire?

Between roughly the thirteenth and nineteenth centuries, Tonga stood at the centre of an extraordinary maritime network. Archaeology has identified stone tools in chiefly Tongan sites whose geological signatures connect them with Fiji, Samoa and more distant islands. Monumental building at Lapaha developed alongside expanding long-distance exchange, while oral traditions, language and material culture preserve evidence of sustained Tongan influence across the central Pacific.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4115531/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

This has often been described as the Tu‘i Tonga Empire. The term captures something important: Tongan rulers possessed exceptional maritime reach, maintained relationships with chiefs beyond the archipelago, received goods and spouses from other islands, and projected political and sacred prestige across great distances.

The problem is that “empire” can imply direct territorial control, permanent administration and regular tribute enforced from a capital. Evidence for that kind of uniform rule is limited. Tonga’s overseas relationships probably varied considerably. Some communities may have faced military intervention or political domination; others were allies, trading partners, relatives or participants in ceremonial exchange. Influence could be real without resembling the provinces of Rome or the British Empire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/50032004/Indigenous_Island_Empires_Yap_and_Tonga_Considered" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Indigenous Island Empires: Yap and Tonga Considered</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Indigenous Island Empires: Yap and Tonga Considered</span></span></span>

Imported stone tools provide particularly valuable evidence because they demonstrate actual movement across the ocean. An analysis of artefacts from important Tongan centres found material originating in several island groups, supporting the picture of a widely connected maritime polity. Yet an imported tool cannot reveal whether it arrived as tribute, trade, a diplomatic gift, a traveller’s possession or the property of an immigrant craft worker.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4115531/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The monumental landscape at Lapaha likewise confirms concentrated authority. From the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, rulers constructed large stone-faced tombs and reorganised the ceremonial centre. Such projects required labour, transport and political coordination. They show that Tonga possessed a powerful central system, but they do not map its overseas boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstreams/795dc36b-7232-4c7a-91f6-b57ffdf20d51/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au">[Open Research Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marked out as a monumental area with a...Read more...</span></span></span>

“Maritime chiefdom” or “maritime state” is therefore often more precise than “empire”, although no single term solves the problem. The disagreement is not simply about vocabulary. It concerns the form of power Tonga exercised: whether distant islands were ruled, periodically coerced, ritually subordinate, economically connected or bound through kinship.

This question remains one of Tonga’s largest historical enigmas because the evidence survives in different forms that do not fit together perfectly. Archaeology records movement and monuments. Linguistics records contact. Oral traditions preserve conquest, genealogy and tribute. European observers arrived late, after the system had already changed. None supplies a complete political map.

What can be stated confidently is that pre-European Tonga was not an isolated collection of islands. It was a major centre in an interconnected Pacific world, capable of moving people, stone, knowledge and authority over enormous distances. Calling it an empire may be defensible when carefully defined, but portraying every connected island as a directly governed Tongan possession turns a complex network into an overly tidy legend.

## What the mysteries reveal about Tonga’s past

Tonga’s historical mysteries survive partly because its past was recorded in several different ways. Genealogy, ceremony and oral tradition preserved relationships and political meaning; European journals recorded only brief encounters; early books filtered Tongan testimony through foreign editors; and archaeology recovered physical evidence that could not explain itself.

The most credible mysteries emerge where these records overlap without entirely agreeing. Ha‘amonga is unquestionably a royal monument, but its full ceremonial meaning is lost. Tongatapu’s earthworks prove early and extensive settlement organisation, but the stages by which it developed are still being reconstructed. The *Port au Prince* was certainly seized in Tonga, but the proposed wreck identification and treasure remain unconfirmed. A plan against Cook is plausible and historically meaningful, but known through retrospective testimony rather than Cook’s own observations. Tongan power extended far across the Pacific, but its exact political form resists a simple imperial map.

These uncertainties are not failures of history. They show why evidence must be read in context. Oral accounts should not be discarded merely because Europeans failed to witness the events they describe. Nor should tradition automatically be treated as a literal transcript. Archaeology can confirm long-distance movement and monumental labour, but it cannot by itself identify motives or legal relationships.

Tonga’s enduring enigmas matter because they challenge older pictures of Pacific peoples as isolated, technologically limited or historically passive. The surviving evidence instead reveals early urban development, sophisticated political organisation, monumental engineering and far-reaching maritime connections. The unanswered questions concern the detailed workings of an accomplished island civilisation, not whether such a civilisation existed.
