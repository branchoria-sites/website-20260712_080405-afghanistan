## Introduction

Who painted the remarkable cattle of Laas Geel? The short answer is that nobody knows their names, language or ethnic identity. Archaeologists agree that the artists were prehistoric cattle-herding communities living in what is now north-western [Somalia]({{ 'what-secrets-still-shape-somalias-past/' | relative_url }}), probably around 5,000 years ago, but there is no written record identifying them. That uncertainty is what makes Laas Geel one of Somalia's most intriguing historical mysteries. The paintings are among the finest surviving examples of prehistoric rock art in Africa, yet the people who created them left only images, not texts. Excavation, comparison with other rock art, and scientific dating have revealed much about their way of life, but the identity of the artists and the full meaning of their paintings remain open questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/en/laas-geel-african-lascaux" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[Archéologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archéologie Laas Geel: the African Lascaux &#124; Archéologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archéologie Laas Geel: the African Lascaux &#124; Archéologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

## What the shelters and paintings show

Laas Geel lies near Hargeisa and consists of around twenty granite rock shelters decorated with vivid polychrome paintings. Unlike many prehistoric sites where images have faded badly, these paintings remain exceptionally well preserved because they are protected beneath natural rock overhangs. They use reds, whites, yellows, browns and blacks to create striking scenes centred on long-horned cattle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/en/laas-geel-african-lascaux" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[Archéologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archéologie Laas Geel: the African Lascaux &#124; Archéologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archéologie Laas Geel: the African Lascaux &#124; Archéologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

The cattle dominate almost every shelter. Many are shown with exaggerated white horns, carefully painted udders and unusual rectangular neck coverings decorated with stripes or solid colours. Human figures stand beneath or beside them with raised arms, while dogs appear frequently and a few giraffes, antelopes and monkeys also feature. Geometric symbols are scattered among the animals.

The artwork immediately suggests that cattle were far more than livestock. The animals are often painted larger and more elaborately than the accompanying people, implying that they occupied the centre of ceremonial or spiritual life rather than merely representing everyday herding. Archaeologists have suggested that the decorated neck coverings may represent ceremonial mats or ornaments placed on prized animals during rituals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africanrockart.britishmuseum.org/country/somalia-somaliland/laas-geel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanrockart.britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanrockart.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Laas Geel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Laas Geel</span></span></span>

## Why nobody knows who painted them

The mystery is not whether humans created the paintings, but which humans.

Unlike later civilisations in the Horn of Africa, these communities left no inscriptions identifying themselves. Their settlements were built from perishable materials, and no historical documents survive from the period. Archaeologists therefore have to reconstruct their identity indirectly from excavated artefacts, animal remains, environmental evidence and the paintings themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/en/laas-geel-african-lascaux" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archeologie.culture.gouv.fr">[Archéologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archeologie.culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archéologie Laas Geel: the African Lascaux &#124; Archéologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archéologie Laas Geel: the African Lascaux &#124; Archéologie &#124; culture.gouv.fr</span></span></span>

Most researchers agree on several broad conclusions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The artists were early pastoralists whose economy depended heavily on cattle.
* They probably belonged to one of the first communities to establish long-term livestock herding in this part of the Horn of Africa.
* They were skilled painters using mineral pigments and carefully selected sheltered rock surfaces.
* Their artistic style is distinctive but shares some characteristics with other prehistoric rock art in Somaliland and neighbouring parts of the Horn, suggesting cultural connections rather than complete isolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africanrockart.britishmuseum.org/country/somalia-somaliland/laas-geel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanrockart.britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanrockart.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Laas Geel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Laas Geel</span></span></span>

</div>

What cannot be established is whether these painters were direct ancestors of later populations, spoke an early Afro-Asiatic language, belonged to another linguistic group entirely, or represented several generations of communities using the same shelters.

## Why the dating remains uncertain

Dating Laas Geel has proved easier than identifying its creators, but important uncertainties remain.

Excavations beneath painted shelters recovered archaeological deposits that allowed researchers to estimate when the site was in use. Fragments of painted rock found within excavated layers suggest that at least some of the paintings belong roughly to the middle of the fourth to the middle of the third millennium BCE. Many publications therefore describe the art as approximately 5,000 years old.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africanrockart.britishmuseum.org/country/somalia-somaliland/laas-geel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanrockart.britishmuseum.org">[africanrockart.britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanrockart.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Laas Geel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Laas Geel</span></span></span>

Even so, archaeologists are cautious about claiming an exact age.

Several factors complicate dating:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Different shelters may have been painted over long periods rather than during a single event.
* Pigments themselves are difficult to date directly because they contain little or no organic material suitable for radiocarbon analysis.
* Some paintings may have been retouched or added after the earliest phases of decoration.
* Excavated material provides dates for human activity at the site rather than precise creation dates for every painted figure.

</div>

As a result, scholars generally speak in ranges rather than exact years. The overall chronology is increasingly secure, but the sequence in which individual panels were created is still not fully understood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africanrockart.britishmuseum.org/country/somalia-somaliland/laas-geel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanrockart.britishmuseum.org">[africanrockart.britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanrockart.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Laas Geel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Laas Geel</span></span></span>

## What decorated cattle may have meant

The paintings raise another enduring question: why were these cattle decorated so elaborately?

The ceremonial appearance of the animals is one of Laas Geel's defining features. Their neck ornaments, careful colouring and repeated association with human figures suggest that the paintings recorded beliefs rather than simple observations of daily life.

Several interpretations have been proposed.

**Ceremonial livestock.** The decorated cattle may represent animals prepared for seasonal festivals, initiation ceremonies or other communal rituals. The repeated emphasis on ornamented necks supports this idea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africanrockart.britishmuseum.org/country/somalia-somaliland/laas-geel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanrockart.britishmuseum.org">[africanrockart.britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanrockart.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Laas Geel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Laas Geel</span></span></span>

**Symbols of prosperity.** In many pastoral societies, cattle represent wealth, family continuity and social status. The paintings may celebrate successful herds that ensured survival in an often harsh environment. This explanation fits the prominence given to healthy, well-fed animals with large horns and udders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saxafimedia.com/laas-geel-somaliland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saxafimedia.com">[SaxafiMedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saxafimedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Saxafi Media Laas Geel, Somaliland &#124; Saxafi Media</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saxafi Media Laas Geel, Somaliland &#124; Saxafi Media</span></span></span>

**Religious imagery.** Human figures with raised arms have been interpreted as participants in acts of reverence, blessing or ritual performance. Some appear almost subordinate to the cattle, suggesting that the animals possessed sacred significance beyond their economic value. However, no surviving evidence allows archaeologists to identify a specific religion or belief system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hornheritage.org/3d-scanning-of-somaliland-rock-art/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hornheritage.org">[Horn Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hornheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Horn Heritage3D Scanning of Somaliland rock Art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horn Heritage &#124; Digital MuseumJune 15, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 15, 2021</span></span></span>

No single explanation has gained universal acceptance because there are no written traditions from the painters themselves. The symbolism must therefore be inferred from recurring artistic patterns rather than direct testimony.

## Competing ideas about the artists

Over the past two decades, the debate has narrowed considerably. Early speculation sometimes linked the paintings to lost civilisations or mysterious outsiders, but there is little archaeological support for such claims.

The strongest evidence instead points towards indigenous prehistoric pastoral communities living in the Horn of Africa during the spread of livestock herding. The paintings match a local archaeological context that includes stone tools, pastoral evidence and neighbouring rock-art traditions rather than signs of an intrusive foreign civilisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://africanrockart.britishmuseum.org/country/somalia-somaliland/laas-geel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanrockart.britishmuseum.org">[africanrockart.britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanrockart.britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Laas Geel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Laas Geel</span></span></span>

Researchers continue to debate finer questions, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* whether the artists represented one community or several successive groups;
* how closely they were connected to neighbouring regions of Ethiopia and the wider Horn;
* whether the paintings document the earliest stages of cattle pastoralism in the region or a culture that was already well established.

</div>

These are active archaeological questions rather than unsolved historical riddles waiting for a single dramatic discovery.

## Why the mystery still matters

Laas Geel demonstrates both the power and the limits of archaeology. The paintings reveal remarkable artistic skill, careful observation and a society that placed enormous importance on cattle. They also preserve evidence for one of the earliest pastoral cultures known in the Horn of Africa.

Yet the artists remain anonymous. Their names, language, myths and ceremonies disappeared thousands of years before written history reached the region. Every new excavation adds pieces to the puzzle, but the central mystery persists: we can admire the paintings in extraordinary detail while knowing almost nothing about the individual people who climbed beneath these granite shelters to create them.

That combination of vivid evidence and missing identity is precisely what makes Laas Geel one of Somalia's most compelling historical mysteries.
