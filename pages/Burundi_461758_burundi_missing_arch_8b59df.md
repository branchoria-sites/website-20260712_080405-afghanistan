## Introduction

One of the biggest obstacles to understanding [Burundi]({{ 'what-secrets-still-haunt-burundis/' | relative_url }})'s history is not simply disagreement over what happened during periods of political violence. It is the loss, fragmentation and restricted access to the evidence itself. Colonial files were dispersed as Belgian rule ended, government records were created unevenly or disappeared during successive crises, witnesses died without giving formal testimony, and many victims were buried without identification. As a result, some of Burundi's most important state crimes remain only partly documented, even where there is broad agreement that atrocities occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/802255/files/A_HRC_30_42_Add.1-EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library System United Nations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library SystemUnited NationsFebruary 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2026</span></span></span>

The mystery is therefore not only who committed particular crimes, but why so many official records, forensic opportunities and documentary trails were lost. This continuing absence of evidence has shaped debates over the 1972 mass killings, later episodes of political violence and more recent investigations into enforced disappearances. Rather than a single missing archive, Burundi faces a landscape of incomplete records that has made historical certainty unusually difficult to achieve.

## Colonial and Government Archive Gaps

Burundi inherited an incomplete documentary record at independence in 1962. Like many former colonies, administrative papers were divided between documents left inside the country and records retained in Belgian repositories. Researchers have therefore had to reconstruct events by combining Burundian records with Belgian archives, United Nations documents, missionary collections, diplomatic correspondence and private papers. This dispersal has complicated investigations into politically sensitive events, especially when different archives contain only fragments of the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/802255/files/A_HRC_30_42_Add.1-EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library System United Nations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library SystemUnited NationsFebruary 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2026</span></span></span>

The problem became more severe during decades of coups, civil conflict and institutional instability. Governments changed repeatedly, ministries were reorganised, and archives often lacked systematic preservation. International experts reviewing Burundi's transitional justice efforts have noted that there has been no comprehensive national system for collecting, preserving and cataloguing records relating to past human rights violations. Important material may therefore have been misplaced, deteriorated or destroyed before historians or investigators could examine it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/802255/files/A_HRC_30_42_Add.1-EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library System United Nations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library SystemUnited NationsFebruary 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2026</span></span></span>

The consequences extend beyond missing paperwork. Without reliable administrative records, investigators struggle to answer basic questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* who was arrested and by which security unit;
* where detainees were transferred;
* whether official orders were issued in writing;
* how many people disappeared in particular localities; and
* which institutions retained responsibility for the surviving files.

</div>

These gaps have left many historical debates dependent on oral testimony rather than complete documentary chains.

## Missing Bodies and Forensic Barriers

Archives are only one part of the missing evidence. Thousands of people killed during Burundi's recurring episodes of mass violence were never formally identified, while numerous burial sites remained undocumented for decades.

The 1972 killings illustrate this problem particularly clearly. Survivors described large-scale executions and mass burials, yet systematic forensic investigation did not begin until many years later. By then, landscapes had changed, witnesses had died or relocated, and memories naturally differed over precise locations and events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/en/publications/managing-mass-graves-in-rwanda-and-burundi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pureportal.strath.ac.uk">[University of Strathclyde]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pureportal.strath.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of StrathclydeManaging Mass Graves in Rwanda and Burundi: Vernaculars of the Right to Truth - University of StrathclydeSeptemb...</span></span></span>

Since 2019, Burundi's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) has overseen extensive exhumation work, reporting hundreds of suspected burial sites, confirmation of many mass graves and the recovery of large numbers of human remains. These discoveries have provided important physical evidence that mass violence occurred, but they have not automatically resolved questions of individual responsibility, exact victim identities or command structures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4006783/files/A_HRC_WG.6_43_BDI_1-EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitallibrary.un.org">[United Nations Digital Library System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitallibrary.un.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ited Nations Digital Library System United Nations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ited Nations Digital Library SystemUnited NationsNovember 3, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 3, 2025</span></span></span>

Researchers studying these exhumations note an important distinction between recovering bodies and reconstructing history. Human remains can confirm that killings took place, but without accompanying records, forensic laboratories, DNA identification programmes or complete archival documentation, it is often impossible to establish:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the identities of every victim;
* precise dates of death;
* the chain of command behind individual killings; or
* whether separate burial sites relate to the same operation or different episodes of violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/en/publications/managing-mass-graves-in-rwanda-and-burundi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pureportal.strath.ac.uk">[University of Strathclyde]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pureportal.strath.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of StrathclydeManaging Mass Graves in Rwanda and Burundi: Vernaculars of the Right to Truth - University of StrathclydeSeptemb...</span></span></span>

</div>

The International Commission on Missing Persons similarly observes that Burundi still lacks definitive accounting for many people who disappeared during successive conflicts and that locating, identifying and documenting victims remains an unfinished task.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/burundi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[ICMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Burundi - International Commission on Missing Persons...</span></span></span>

## How Political Upheaval Preserved Uncertainty

Repeated political crises did more than generate new victims; they also prevented older crimes from being fully investigated.

Each major upheaval shifted official attention away from preserving evidence. Successive governments often prioritised immediate security or political survival over archival preservation, while periods of violence discouraged witnesses from speaking openly. Testimony that might have clarified earlier crimes was therefore postponed, forgotten or never formally recorded.

Fear also shaped the historical record. Families sometimes avoided reporting disappearances because they feared retaliation. Former officials had little incentive to preserve potentially incriminating documents, while local administrators often lacked the resources needed to maintain secure archives during conflict.

These conditions created what historians sometimes describe as cumulative evidential loss. Every decade made earlier events harder to reconstruct because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* documents deteriorated or disappeared;
* eyewitnesses died;
* burial sites became harder to locate;
* institutional memory weakened; and
* later conflicts disrupted earlier investigations.

</div>

Instead of one decisive loss of evidence, Burundi experienced many smaller losses that accumulated over time.

## Why Unresolved State Crimes Remain Contested

Several major historical crimes remain debated not because the underlying violence is doubted, but because surviving evidence is incomplete.

For many episodes—including the 1972 mass killings, later civil war abuses and more recent allegations of enforced disappearances—the broad occurrence of serious violations is well documented by witnesses, international organisations and later investigations. What remains disputed are questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the precise number of victims;
* the identities of every perpetrator;
* how centrally organised particular operations were;
* which officials issued orders; and
* whether all available state records have survived or been disclosed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icmp.int/the-missing/where-are-the-missing/burundi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icmp.int">[icmp.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icmp.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Burundi - International Commission on Missing Persons...</span></span></span>

</div>

The uncertainty has encouraged competing historical narratives. Different political groups have sometimes emphasised particular periods of violence while questioning interpretations of others. Because documentary evidence is incomplete, historical arguments frequently rely on different combinations of testimony, archival fragments and forensic discoveries rather than on one comprehensive official record.

Academic studies of Burundi's recent exhumations argue that truth-seeking itself has become politically significant. Decisions about which graves to excavate, which testimonies to prioritise and how discoveries are presented can influence collective memory as well as historical understanding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/en/publications/managing-mass-graves-in-rwanda-and-burundi/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pureportal.strath.ac.uk">[University of Strathclyde]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pureportal.strath.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of StrathclydeManaging Mass Graves in Rwanda and Burundi: Vernaculars of the Right to Truth - University of StrathclydeSeptemb...</span></span></span>

## Why the Missing Evidence Still Matters

Burundi's hidden records are not merely an academic puzzle. Missing archives affect criminal accountability, family mourning, historical scholarship and national reconciliation.

For relatives, the absence of reliable documentation means many people still do not know exactly where family members died or where they were buried. For investigators, incomplete files make it difficult to establish legal responsibility decades after events occurred. For historians, dispersed colonial archives, missing administrative records and fragmentary testimony require cautious reconstruction rather than definitive conclusions.

The central mystery is therefore broader than any single disappearance or political killing. Burundi's history contains many documented atrocities, but decades of disrupted record-keeping, inaccessible archives and delayed forensic investigation mean that the country's documentary memory remains incomplete. Every newly opened archive, recovered testimony or identified burial site narrows the gaps, yet many questions are likely to remain unresolved because some of the original evidence has almost certainly been lost forever.
