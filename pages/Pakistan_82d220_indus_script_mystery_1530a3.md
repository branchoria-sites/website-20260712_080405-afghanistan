## Introduction

The Indus script is one of the world's greatest undeciphered writing mysteries. Thousands of inscriptions survive from the Bronze Age cities of Harappa, [Mohenjo-daro]({{ 'mohenjo-daro/' | relative_url }}) and other Indus civilisation sites across present-day [Pakistan]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-pakistans/' | relative_url }}) and north-western India, yet nobody can confidently read them. The puzzle is not a lack of effort. Researchers have spent more than a century proposing decipherments, but none has won broad scholarly acceptance because the surviving evidence is unusually limited: the inscriptions are extremely short, there is no bilingual text comparable to the Rosetta Stone, and the language spoken by the Indus civilisation remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/tasks-methods-and-results-in-the-study-of-the-indus-script/E77A33DECB89FBB5327234211D83AE70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentTasks, Methods and Results in the Study of the Indus Script &#124; Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society...</span></span></span>

For Pakistan's history, this matters because the Indus civilisation built some of the ancient world's most sophisticated cities but left no readable historical records. Their architecture, trade networks and craftsmanship can be studied through archaeology, but their own words remain silent. Until the script can be understood—or researchers demonstrate conclusively that it was not a full writing system—the civilisation's political organisation, religious ideas, administration and even the names of its people will remain partly hidden.

## What the signs and inscriptions actually show

The Indus script appears on stamp seals, sealings, miniature tablets, pottery, metal objects, ivory and other artefacts dating mainly to around 2600–1900 BC. Most examples have been found in the Indus heartland, including Harappa in Punjab and Mohenjo-daro in Sindh, although objects bearing the signs have also been discovered at trading settlements beyond the civilisation's core.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mohenjodarorj.sindhila.org/index.php/journal/article/view/103" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mohenjodarorj.sindhila.org">[mohenjodarorj.sindhila.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mohenjodarorj.sindhila.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DECIPHERIN G THE INDUS SCRIPT &#124; Mohen Jo Daro</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DECIPHERING THE INDUS SCRIPT &#124; Mohen Jo DaroJune 12, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 12, 2025</span></span></span>

Several observations are widely accepted by specialists.

* Around 4,000 inscribed objects are known, although the exact total varies as new discoveries are made and duplicate impressions are removed from catalogues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interrogating Indus inscriptions to unravel their mechanisms of meaning conveyance &#124; Humanities and Social Sciences Communications...</span></span></span>
* Most inscriptions are remarkably short, averaging roughly five signs. Even the longest continuous texts contain only a few dozen characters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/tasks-methods-and-results-in-the-study-of-the-indus-script/E77A33DECB89FBB5327234211D83AE70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentTasks, Methods and Results in the Study of the Indus Script &#124; Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society...</span></span></span>
* Most seal inscriptions appear to have been written from right to left, based on sign orientation and layout.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interrogating Indus inscriptions to unravel their mechanisms of meaning conveyance &#124; Humanities and Social Sciences Communications...</span></span></span>
* The script uses hundreds of distinct signs, although the precise number depends on whether visually similar forms are treated as separate symbols or stylistic variants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mohenjodarorj.sindhila.org/index.php/journal/article/view/103" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mohenjodarorj.sindhila.org">[mohenjodarorj.sindhila.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mohenjodarorj.sindhila.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DECIPHERIN G THE INDUS SCRIPT &#124; Mohen Jo Daro</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DECIPHERING THE INDUS SCRIPT &#124; Mohen Jo DaroJune 12, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 12, 2025</span></span></span>

Although the inscriptions are brief, they are not random collections of symbols. Certain signs consistently appear at the beginning of texts, others near the end, while some combinations recur far more often than chance would predict. These regularities suggest deliberate ordering and fixed conventions. Statistical analyses using methods borrowed from linguistics and information theory have repeatedly identified positional rules and patterned sign sequences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0009506" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[plos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Statistical Analysis of the Indus Script Using n-Grams &#124; PLOS One</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistical Analysis of the Indus Script Using n-Grams &#124; PLOS OneMarch 19, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 19, 2010</span></span></span>

That finding is important, but it does not solve the mystery. A structured sign system is not automatically a full writing system. Road signs, heraldic symbols and modern product codes also follow strict rules without representing complete spoken language.

## Why proposed decipherments remain disputed

The history of Indus decipherment is filled with confident announcements followed by scholarly rejection. The main reason is that every proposed solution faces the same fundamental obstacles.

### Nobody knows the underlying language

A writing system cannot be read reliably without some idea of the language it represents. Competing proposals have argued that the script records an early Dravidian language, an early Indo-Aryan language, a lost language family, or something else entirely.

The Proto-Dravidian hypothesis has attracted sustained scholarly attention, particularly through the work of researchers such as Asko Parpola, but it remains a hypothesis rather than an accepted solution because no proposed readings consistently explain the full corpus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/tasks-methods-and-results-in-the-study-of-the-indus-script/E77A33DECB89FBB5327234211D83AE70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentTasks, Methods and Results in the Study of the Indus Script &#124; Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society...</span></span></span>

### The inscriptions are too short

Ancient writing systems are usually deciphered by identifying repeated grammatical patterns, personal names and longer passages. The Indus corpus offers very little of this.

With an average inscription of only around five signs, researchers have too little context to determine whether a repeated sequence represents a title, a person's name, a commodity, a number or something entirely different.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/tasks-methods-and-results-in-the-study-of-the-indus-script/E77A33DECB89FBB5327234211D83AE70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentTasks, Methods and Results in the Study of the Indus Script &#124; Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society...</span></span></span>

### There is no bilingual key

The decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs depended on the Rosetta Stone, while cuneiform benefited from multilingual inscriptions such as the Behistun inscription.

Nothing comparable has ever been found for the Indus script. No surviving inscription securely pairs Indus signs with a known language, leaving scholars without an external reference point.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/tasks-methods-and-results-in-the-study-of-the-indus-script/E77A33DECB89FBB5327234211D83AE70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentTasks, Methods and Results in the Study of the Indus Script &#124; Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society...</span></span></span>

### Every successful claim must explain all the evidence

Many claimed decipherments work only by selecting a handful of signs while ignoring difficult examples.

A convincing solution would need to explain sign order, account for variant spellings, fit archaeological contexts, predict the meanings of previously unread inscriptions and remain consistent across thousands of examples. No published proposal has yet met those standards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interrogating Indus inscriptions to unravel their mechanisms of meaning conveyance &#124; Humanities and Social Sciences Communications...</span></span></span>

## Is it really a writing system?

One of the most important debates today concerns a surprisingly basic question: does the Indus script actually encode spoken language?

Researchers broadly divide into two positions.

Those who regard it as writing point to its organised syntax, recurring positional patterns and statistical behaviour. Computer-based analyses have shown that the signs display neither completely random ordering nor rigid fixed sequences, but occupy an intermediate level similar to many communication systems. Network analyses also suggest underlying structural organisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0009506" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[plos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Statistical Analysis of the Indus Script Using n-Grams &#124; PLOS One</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistical Analysis of the Indus Script Using n-Grams &#124; PLOS OneMarch 19, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 19, 2010</span></span></span>

Other scholars argue that the evidence is still insufficient to classify it as full writing. Steve Farmer, Richard Sproat and Michael Witzel have argued that the brevity of the inscriptions, the absence of longer texts and the apparent stability of the sign inventory over centuries are more consistent with a symbolic or administrative emblem system than with language-based writing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/journals/ejvs/article/view/620" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de">[Heidelberg Asian Studies Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heidelberg Asian Studies PublishingThe Collapse of the Indus-Script Thesis: The Myth of a Literate Harappan Civilization &#124; Electronic Jou...</span></span></span>

Neither position has achieved universal acceptance. Even researchers who favour the linguistic interpretation generally acknowledge that current evidence cannot conclusively prove that every inscription records natural language. Likewise, critics of the writing hypothesis must explain why the sign system exhibits such clear structural regularities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.3017" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Statistical analysis of the Indus script using $n$-grams</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Statistical analysis of the Indus script using $n$-grams</span></span></span>

## What the inscriptions may have been used for

Even without knowing exactly what the signs mean, archaeologists can ask how inscribed objects functioned within Indus society.

Most seals appear to have been used for stamping clay sealings associated with goods, containers or transactions. This suggests roles connected with administration, trade, ownership or identity.

Some miniature tablets from Harappa have led researchers to suggest that at least part of the sign system may have recorded labour assignments, ration distributions or standardised economic transactions, drawing comparisons with early administrative documents from Mesopotamia and Elam. These interpretations remain provisional because they rely on archaeological context rather than deciphered text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.00049" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The important point is that archaeologists can sometimes infer an artefact's purpose without being able to translate its inscription.

## What a successful reading would need to prove

Because so many decipherments have been announced over the decades, specialists have developed clear expectations for what would count as genuine success.

A convincing decipherment would need to:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* identify the underlying language independently of wishful thinking;
* explain the majority of known inscriptions rather than selected examples;
* account for sign order and grammatical structure;
* make testable predictions about newly discovered inscriptions;
* fit securely with archaeological evidence from Indus settlements and trade networks;
* withstand independent verification by other specialists rather than relying on subjective interpretations.

</div>

Without these tests, proposed readings remain interesting hypotheses rather than accepted translations.

## Why the mystery still matters

The unread script limits what historians can know about one of the world's earliest urban civilisations. Archaeology reveals carefully planned cities, sophisticated drainage systems, long-distance trade, standardised weights and impressive craft production, yet fundamental questions remain unanswered because no contemporary historical texts can be read.

A reliable decipherment could reveal how officials organised cities, how merchants recorded trade, how religious ideas were expressed, what languages people spoke and perhaps even the names of rulers, places or communities. Equally, if future evidence were to demonstrate conclusively that the signs never represented full writing, that would reshape understanding of how a complex Bronze Age civilisation managed administration without conventional literacy.

For now, the Indus script remains one of Pakistan's most enduring historical mysteries: a system of symbols that clearly follows rules, yet continues to resist every serious attempt to discover exactly what those rules were meant to say.
